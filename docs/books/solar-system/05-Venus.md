# 5. Venus

*Venus is almost the Earth's twin in size, and it shines brighter in our sky than anything except the Sun and the
Moon. But under its unbroken clouds of sulphuric acid lies the hottest surface of any planet, crushed under air 92
times as heavy as ours. This chapter looks at how Venus got that way, how spacecraft from many countries have reached
it, and why it is the best warning we have of a greenhouse gone wrong. Mars, with the same kind of air but very
little of it, makes the perfect contrast.*

## 5.1 The Earth's twin { #twin }

Venus is **12,104 km** wide, 95% of the Earth's width, with 82% of the Earth's mass and nearly the same density. It is
the second planet from the Sun, at 0.72 AU, on the roundest orbit of any planet, and it goes round once in **225
days**. It has no moons.

Because its clouds reflect most of the sunlight that falls on them, Venus is the brightest planet in our sky, seen as
the morning or evening star (book 1, [section 13.3](../night-sky/13-Finding-the-Planets.md#inner)). In December 1610
Galileo saw through his telescope that it shows phases like the Moon, a key clue that it goes round the Sun (book 11,
[section 15.4](../history/15-Galileo-and-the-Telescope.md#venus)). In English it is named after the Roman goddess of
love.

## 5.2 Under the clouds { #clouds }

Venus's air is **96.5% carbon dioxide** and 3.5% nitrogen. There is so much of it that the pressure at the ground is
**92 times** the Earth's, like being nearly 1 km under the sea. At the bottom it is not quite a gas any more but a
hot, dense fluid. The average temperature at the surface is **464 °C**, day and night, pole to equator: hotter than
Mercury, though Venus is almost twice as far from the Sun.

From 45 to 70 km up, thick **clouds of sulphuric acid droplets** cover the whole planet, so we never see the surface
in ordinary light. The cloud tops race round the planet in about **4 days**, at about 300 km/h, 60 times faster than
the planet itself turns. This is called **super-rotation**. Down at the ground the wind is only a slow push of 0.3 to
1 m/s, but in such thick air even that can roll small stones along.

Higher up, Venus is surprisingly gentle (Figure A). At about 50 km the pressure is the same as at the Earth's surface,
and between 50 and 54 km the temperature is between 0 and 50 °C: the most Earth-like place in the solar system after
the Earth itself.

## 5.3 Backwards and slow { #spin }

Venus spins **backwards**: seen from above the north pole of the solar system, it turns clockwise, while nearly every
other planet turns anticlockwise. It is also the slowest spinner of all. One turn takes **243 Earth days**, longer
than its year of 225 days.

Put the backward spin and the orbit together and the Sun takes **117 Earth days** to go from one noon to the next. It
rises in the west and sets in the east, though nobody standing on the surface could see it through the clouds. Its
axis is tilted 177°, which is the same as saying it is upright and spinning the wrong way.

Why does it spin like this? The pull of the Sun's tides tries to slow Venus down until one side faces the Sun, while
the Sun's heating of Venus's massive air pushes the other way. The balance between them leaves a slow backward spin.

    - The backward (retrograde) spin, the sidereal day of 243 Earth days longer than its year, the solar day of

    - The rotation of −5,832.6 hours, the day of 2,802 hours and the tilt of 177.36°: NASA, Venus Fact Sheet
      (above).
    - The Sun rising in the west, and a 177° tilt being the same as an upright planet spinning backwards: our

## 5.4 A runaway greenhouse { #greenhouse }

Venus's bright clouds reflect 77% of the sunlight. If it had no greenhouse effect, the sunlight it keeps would warm it
to only about **227 K (−46 °C)**, colder than the Earth. Instead, its surface is 737 K. The reason is its air.

Sunlight that gets through the clouds warms the ground. The ground glows in infrared, but carbon dioxide stops
infrared from escaping, so heat builds up until the surface is hot enough to push enough of it out. This is the
**greenhouse effect** (book 2, [section 6.9](../light-and-telescopes/06-Hot-Things-Glow.md#balance) works out a
planet's temperature without it). On Venus it is stronger than anywhere else in the solar system.

**Where did the water go?** Venus is very dry today. But in the little water vapour left, heavy hydrogen, deuterium,
is **100 to 150 times** as common, compared with ordinary hydrogen, as on the Earth. The likely story: Venus once had
much more water, perhaps even oceans. As the young Sun brightened, the water evaporated, and water vapour is itself a
greenhouse gas, so the warming fed on itself: a **runaway greenhouse**. High in the air, sunlight broke the water
apart, and the light hydrogen escaped to space faster than the heavier deuterium, leaving deuterium behind. Some
scientists now think Venus may have been too hot for oceans all along. Either way, the carbon dioxide stayed.

The Earth has plenty of carbon too, but most of ours is locked up in limestone and other rocks, laid down with the
help of liquid water. Carl Sagan and others pointed to Venus as a warning of what too much carbon
dioxide in the air can do.

## 5.5 Under the clouds by radar { #surface }

Radar sees through clouds. NASA's **Magellan** orbiter, launched on 4 May 1989 and named after the Portuguese
explorer Ferdinand Magellan, mapped the surface in detail from 1990. Its maps show a world of **lava plains,
volcanoes and long cracks**, with about a thousand impact craters, far fewer than on the Moon or Mercury.

So few craters means the surface is young: about **300 to 600 million years** old, against billions of years for most
of Mars. Venus seems to have no moving plates like the Earth's, perhaps because, without water, its crust is too
stiff to sink back into the mantle. Instead, huge outpourings of lava may have repaved it.

Is it still erupting? In 2008 and 2009 ESA's Venus Express saw short-lived hot spots in a rift near the volcano
**Maat Mons**, and in 2023 a study of old Magellan images found a volcanic vent there that had changed shape over
eight months. The highest mountains, **Maxwell Montes**, rise about 11 km on the highland of Ishtar Terra.

**A world named after women.** Almost every feature on Venus has a female name. Many of the big ones are named after
goddesses: Ishtar Terra after the Akkadian goddess Ishtar, the plain Lakshmi Planum after the Hindu goddess
Lakshmi, Maat Mons after the Egyptian goddess of truth and justice. The craters, 900 of them named by 2017, honour
famous women:

## 5.6 Visiting Venus { #missions }

Venus was the first planet reached by spacecraft, and the Soviet Union's **Venera** program landed on it again and
again.

- **1962, Mariner 2** (United States) flew past on 14 December, the first successful visit to another planet.
- **1967, Venera 4** (Soviet Union) dropped into the air and showed that the surface must be nearly 500 °C and the
  air 95% carbon dioxide, much denser than expected.
- **1970, Venera 7** made the first soft landing on another planet, on 15 December, and sent back data from the
  surface. Some of its faint signal was only found weeks later, when the radio astronomer Oleg Rzhiga went back
  through the tapes.
- **1975, Veneras 9 and 10** sent back the first pictures from the surface, in black and white.
- **1982, Venera 13** landed on 1 March and took colour pictures. Built to last 32 minutes, it worked for at least
  127 minutes at 457 °C, and it made the first sound recording on another planet.
- **1985, Vega 1 and Vega 2**, led by the Soviet Union with Austria, Bulgaria, France, Hungary, Poland,
  Czechoslovakia and both German states, dropped landers and two **balloons**, the first craft to fly in the air of
  another planet, before going on to Halley's comet.
- **1990s, Magellan** (United States) mapped the surface by radar.
- **2006 to 2014, Venus Express**, ESA's first Venus mission, studied the air and found the hot spots.
- **2015 to 2024, Akatsuki**, the first Japanese spacecraft to orbit Venus, got there on 7 December 2015, five years
  after its first try at entering orbit failed. JAXA lost contact with it in April 2024.

Next, India's **Venus Orbiter Mission**, approved on 18 September 2024, is planned to launch in March 2028. NASA's
VERITAS and DAVINCI and ESA's EnVision are planned for the early 2030s, though their dates have slipped more than
once.

## 5.8 Working it out { #working }

**The solar day.** Venus turns backwards once in 243.025 days and goes round the Sun forwards once in 224.701 days.
Because the two turnings add, the Sun moves across its sky at the sum of the rates:

$$\frac{1}{\text{day}} = \frac{1}{224.701} + \frac{1}{243.025}, \qquad \text{day} = 116.75 \text{ Earth days}.$$

**Air over each square metre.** The pressure at the ground is the weight of the air above each square metre, so the
mass of that air is $m = P/g$:

| | Mars | Earth | Venus |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Pressure | 636 Pa | 101,400 Pa | 9,200,000 Pa |
| Gravity | 3.73 m/s² | 9.82 m/s² | 8.87 m/s² |
| Air, $P/g$ | 171 kg/m² | 10,330 kg/m² | 1,040,000 kg/m² |
| Carbon dioxide by mass | 96% | 0.064% | 98% |
| Carbon dioxide | 164 kg/m² | 6.6 kg/m² | 1,010,000 kg/m² |

The share by mass is the share by number of molecules times $44/\bar\mu$, where 44 is the mass of a CO₂ molecule and
$\bar\mu$ the average for the air: for the Earth, $0.000420 \times 44.01/28.97 = 0.00064$. Then Mars over the Earth
is $164/6.6 = 25$, and Venus over Mars is $1{,}040{,}000/171 = 6{,}100$.

**How deep in the sea?** In the sea, pressure grows by $\rho g h$. To add 91 bar, $h = 91 \times 10^5/(1{,}025 \times
9.82) = 900$ m.

    - Periods, pressures, gravity, composition and mean molecular weights (43.49 for Mars, 28.97 for the Earth,

## 5.9 How thick is the greenhouse? { #derive }

A simple way to measure a greenhouse is to ask how **opaque** the air is to infrared. Call the opacity $\tau$, the
optical depth: $\tau = 1$ means that infrared trying to get out is cut to about a third, $\tau = 0$ means clear air.
For a planet whose air absorbs infrared evenly at all wavelengths, a standard result is

$$T_s^4 = T_e^4\left(1 + \tfrac{3}{4}\tau\right),$$

where $T_e$ is the temperature the planet would have with no greenhouse (book 2, section 6.9) and $T_s$ the surface
temperature. Turned round:

$$\tau = \tfrac{4}{3}\left[\left(\frac{T_s}{T_e}\right)^4 - 1\right].$$

| | Mars | Earth | Venus |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $T_e$, no greenhouse | 209.8 K | 254.0 K | 226.6 K |
| $T_s$, real surface | 214 K | 288 K | 737 K |
| $(T_s/T_e)^4$ | 1.08 | 1.65 | 112 |
| Optical depth $\tau$ | 0.1 | 0.9 | 150 |

Mars's greenhouse is very weak: its air is thin, so even nearly pure carbon dioxide warms it by only about 4 K. The
Earth's is moderate, worth 34 K, and mostly due to water vapour and carbon dioxide together. Venus's air is opaque to
infrared about 150 times over, and the surface is 510 K warmer than it would be without it.

The model is rough. Real gases absorb some wavelengths strongly and let others through, and Venus's lower air is
stirred by rising and sinking currents. But it shows the key point: what matters is how much greenhouse gas lies
above the ground, not what share of the air it makes up.

??? note "Sources"
    - The grey-atmosphere result $T_s^4 = T_e^4(1 + 3\tau/4)$ and the meaning of optical depth: standard.
    - The temperatures: NASA, Mars, Earth and Venus Fact Sheets (above); 214 K for Mars is NASA's average.
    - The Earth's greenhouse due to water vapour and carbon dioxide together: standard.
    - Everything else: our arithmetic.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Why is Venus called the Earth's twin, and in what ways is it not one?

??? note "Answer"
    It is almost the same size, mass and density as the Earth. But its air is 92 times as heavy and almost all carbon
    dioxide, its surface is 464 °C, it has no water or moons, and it spins slowly backwards.

**2.** (beginner) What are Venus's clouds made of, and how high are they?

??? note "Answer"
    Droplets of sulphuric acid, from about 45 to 70 km above the ground.

**3.** (beginner) Which spacecraft made the first soft landing on another planet, and when?

??? note "Answer"
    The Soviet Venera 7, on Venus, on 15 December 1970.

**4.** (intermediate) Why is Venus hotter than Mercury, though it is almost twice as far from the Sun?

??? note "Answer"
    Its thick carbon dioxide air traps the infrared from the ground, a very strong greenhouse effect. Mercury has no
    air, so its night side cools quickly.

**5.** (intermediate) What does Venus's high ratio of deuterium to hydrogen suggest?

??? note "Answer"
    That it once had much more water. Light hydrogen escaped to space faster than heavy deuterium, so the water left
    behind became rich in deuterium.

**6.** (intermediate) How do we know Venus's surface is young?

??? note "Answer"
    It has few impact craters, about a thousand, so it must have been covered over by lava 300 to 600 million years
    ago.

**7.** (advanced) Mars's air has 95% carbon dioxide and the Earth's only 0.04%. Why does Mars still have so little
greenhouse warming?

??? note "Answer"
    Its air is so thin, 171 kg over each square metre, that the total carbon dioxide, 164 kg/m², is small. That is
    25 times the Earth's 6.6 kg/m², but the Earth's warming comes mostly from water vapour, which Mars barely has.

**8.** (expert) Suppose a planet has $T_e = 250$ K and $\tau = 10$. What is its surface temperature?

??? note "Answer"
    $T_s = 250 \times (1 + 7.5)^{1/4} = 250 \times 8.5^{1/4} = 250 \times 1.707 = 427$ K.

## Summary

- Venus is almost the Earth's size and mass, but it has no water or moons and spins slowly backwards.
- Its air is 96.5% carbon dioxide, 92 times as heavy as ours, and its surface is 464 °C.
- Sulphuric acid clouds from 45 to 70 km race round it in 4 days; at 50 km the air is like the Earth's.
- A runaway greenhouse made it hot; its deuterium-rich water hints that it once had much more water.
- Radar shows a young, volcanic surface, with features named after goddesses and women from around the world.
- Soviet Venera landers reached its surface; Vega balloons flew in its air.
- Mars has the same kind of air but 6,000 times less of it, so its greenhouse is very weak.
