# 11. Craters and impacts

*Look at the Moon through binoculars and you see craters on craters. Mercury and Mars are the same, and so are most
moons. The Earth has been hit just as often, but rain, rivers and moving plates wipe the scars away. This chapter is
about what happens when a rock from space hits a solid world: how a crater forms, why big ones look different from
small ones, how people proved craters were made by impacts at all, the impact that ended the dinosaurs, how big the
danger is today, and what the craters of Mars tell us.*

## 11.1 How a crater forms { #forming }

**Fast and hard.** Anything that falls onto the Earth from space arrives at **at least 11 km/s**, the escape speed,
because the Earth's own pull speeds it up that much. The fastest hits, from objects meeting the Earth head-on, reach
about **72 km/s**. The middle value is about **20 km/s**. At that speed each kilogram carries
$\tfrac12 \times 20{,}000^2 = 2 \times 10^8$ J, about **48 times** the energy in a kilogram of TNT (section 11.8). A
rock that big does not make a dent: it explodes.

The air slows small objects and breaks many of them up, so they land at a few hundred kilometres an hour or burn up
completely (book 1, [section 15.5](../night-sky/15-Meteors-and-Comets.md#fireballs)). Only larger ones, tens of metres
across or made of iron, keep most of their speed down to the ground.

**Three stages.** Geologists split what happens next into three stages, all over in seconds to minutes.

1. **Contact and compression.** The object hits and drives a **shock wave** into the ground and back into itself.
   The pressure is far above anything rock can stand. The object and the rock near it are squeezed, heated, and
   melted or turned to vapour.
2. **Excavation.** The squeezed rock springs out in an explosion. Rock is pushed down and sideways and thrown up and
   out as **ejecta**, opening a hole far wider than the object. Meteor Crater is 24 times as wide as the iron that
   made it; Chicxulub is about 20 times as wide as its asteroid.
3. **Modification.** The hole is not stable. Its walls slump, and in big craters the floor rises and the rim falls in
   (section 11.2).

**Why craters are round.** Most impacts come in at a slant, yet almost every crater is round. The crater is dug by
the explosion, not by the object pushing sideways, and an explosion spreads out from a point. Only very shallow hits
make oval craters. The result is a hole with a **raised rim** and a floor **below the ground** around it, often with
a blanket of ejecta and bright **rays** of thrown rock.

## 11.2 Simple and complex craters { #shapes }

**Simple craters.** Small craters are **bowls**. Their walls slide a little and some broken rock and melt slump to the
bottom, but the shape stays a smooth bowl. On the Earth craters stay simple up to about **4 km** across. The best
known is **Meteor Crater** in Arizona, also called Barringer Crater: about **1.2 km** wide and **170 m** deep, with a
rim **45 m** above the plain. Its depth is 0.14 of its width (Figure A).

**Complex craters.** In bigger craters the walls cannot hold. They slump inwards in steps, called **terraces**, the
floor ends up flat and wide, and the middle rises into a **central peak**. This rise is **not** an elastic bounce.
For a moment the shattered rock has almost no strength and flows under gravity like a liquid, the way a drop
splashing into water throws up a column in the middle, and then it freezes in place.

**Tycho** on the Moon is a fine example. It is about **85 km** wide and **4.7 km** deep, its central peaks rise
**2.5 km** above the floor, and its inner wall is slumped and terraced. At about **108 million years** it is young
for a lunar crater, and its bright rays cross much of the near side. Its depth is only 0.055 of its width: complex
craters are shallow for their size.

**Bigger still.** On the Moon, craters from about **35 to 170 km** wide have a central peak. Larger ones have a ring
of peaks instead, such as **Schrödinger**, and the largest have several rings and are called **multi-ring basins**,
such as **Orientale**, about 350 km across with rings out to 930 km. Above about 300 km a crater is usually called a
**basin**.

**Gravity sets the change.** Stronger gravity pulls walls down sooner, so the switch from simple to complex comes at
**2 to 4 km** on the Earth but about **20 km** on the Moon, where gravity is six times weaker. If the size where the
change happens goes as $1/g$, the Moon's 20 km becomes $20 \times 1.62/9.81 = 3.3$ km on the Earth, right in the
middle of the real range (section 11.8, and the reason in section 11.9).

## 11.3 Proving that craters are impacts { #history }

For a long time most scientists thought the Moon's craters were volcanoes, and nobody expected to find impact
craters on the Earth. Proving otherwise took about seventy years, and the evidence came from many countries.

**Arizona.** In November 1891 **Grove Karl Gilbert**, chief geologist of the US Geological Survey, studied Meteor
Crater. He reasoned that an impact crater should still hold the iron that made it. He found none, so he decided it was
a steam explosion. Yet in **1893** he argued that the Moon's craters were made by impacts. **Daniel M. Barringer**, a
mining engineer, was sure by 1903 that Meteor Crater was made from space. He spent **27 years** and about \$500,000
looking for the great mass of iron he expected below it, drilling to **419 m**, and found none. He was right about the
crater and wrong about the iron: most of it had melted or turned to vapour.

**The case builds.** In the 1920s the American geologist **Walter H. Bucher** studied several strange craters in the
United States and decided they were volcanic; in **1936** John D. Boon and Claude C. Albritton Jr. looked again and
said they were probably impacts. In **1949 Ralph Baldwin** wrote that the Moon's craters were mostly made by
impacts.

**The proof.** The clinching evidence was in the minerals. In **1960** **Edward C. T. Chao** and **Eugene Shoemaker**
found **coesite** at Meteor Crater, a form of silica made only at enormous pressure. Soon after, coesite turned up in
the melt rock of the **Nördlinger Ries** in Germany, proving that it too was an impact. **Shocked quartz** is the
other test: it forms only in impacts, in lightning, or in nuclear explosions, never in a volcano. Shoemaker, an
American geologist who lived from 1928 to 1997, helped train the Apollo astronauts in geology. After his death
some of his ashes were carried to the Moon on the Lunar Prospector probe.

**A search begins.** With these tests in hand, **Carlyle S. Beals** and colleagues at the Dominion Astrophysical
Observatory in Canada, and **Wolf von Engelhardt** of the University of Tübingen in Germany, began a methodical
search. By 1970 they had found more than 50 likely impact craters. The Apollo landings, at the same time, showed how
steadily the Moon had been hit.

**Craters people lived with.**

- **Kaali, Estonia.** On the island of Saaremaa a meteorite broke up in the air and made a main crater **110 m**
  wide and 22 m deep, now holding a lake, and eight smaller craters 12 to 40 m across. Its age is usually given as
  about 3,500 years, though estimates vary widely. **Ivan Reinvald** proved it was an impact in 1928, 1933 and 1937.
  The blast, about **80 TJ** or 20 kilotons of TNT, like the Hiroshima bomb, burned the forest up to 6 km away in a
  land where people already lived. Later the lake became a cult site, ringed by a stone wall, with animal bones that
  may be offerings.
- **Lonar, India.** In Maharashtra a crater **1.8 km** wide and 150 m deep was blasted into the dark lava of the
  Deccan Plateau. It holds a salty lake, the only lake in a meteorite crater in India. It was once thought to be a
  volcano. Its age is disputed: one method gives 52,000 years, another 570,000. Its ejecta flowed after landing and
  formed layers like those around craters on Mars (section 11.7), which makes it a useful model for Mars.
- **Kandimalal, Australia.** The Djaru people of Western Australia tell stories of the Wolfe Creek crater (book 11,
  [section 12.6](../history/12-Oceania-and-Australia.md#aboriginal)).
- **Sikhote-Alin, Russia.** In 1947 an iron meteorite shower made a field of craters while people watched.
- **Carancas, Peru.** On **15 September 2007** a stony meteorite came down near the village of Carancas, near Lake
  Titicaca, and left a water-filled hole. Many villagers felt ill afterwards, apparently from fumes.

## 11.4 Craters on the Earth { #earth }

**Why so few?** The Moon keeps its craters for billions of years (chapter 7,
[section 7.3](07-The-Moon.md#craters)). The Earth does not. Rain and rivers wear rims down, sediment fills hollows,
and moving plates carry the sea floor down into the mantle. The Earth gets one to three impacts big enough for a
20 km crater every million years, so there should be far more young craters than anyone has found. Most known
craters are younger than 500 million years, and they are mostly in the old, stable middles of continents. Few are
known under the sea.

**Counting them.** The **Earth Impact Database** was started in **1955** by the Dominion Observatory in Ottawa, led
by Carlyle S. Beals, and since 2001 it has been kept at the Planetary and Space Science Centre of the University of
New Brunswick in Canada. In November 2025 it listed **190** confirmed impact structures, the same number as in 2019.
Wikipedia's Impact crater page gives 196 as of August 2026. They range from a few tens of metres to about 300 km.

**The giants.** Some of the largest and oldest:

| Chicxulub | Mexico | about 200 km | 66 million years |

**Vredefort**, in Free State province, is the largest known. Claude C. Albritton and John D. Boon suggested an impact
as early as 1937, and it was settled in 1961, when the American geologists Robert S. Dietz and Robert B. Hargraves
found **shatter cones** there, cone-shaped fractures that only shock makes. Only **Yarrabubba** in Western Australia,
2.23 billion years old, is known to be older. **Sudbury** is rich in ore: nickel, copper, gold, silver, platinum and
other metals are mined there. **Popigai** ties with Acraman in Australia as the fourth largest; its shocked rock holds
diamonds, and the area was closed to scientists for decades. **Manicouagan** was made by an object about 5 km across.
Its ring-shaped lake around a central island is so clear from space that it is sometimes called the "eye of Quebec".

## 11.5 The crater that ended the dinosaurs { #chicxulub }

**A clue in the clay.** In 1980 the geologist **Walter Alvarez** and his father **Luis Alvarez**, a Nobel Prize
winning physicist, with the chemists Frank Asaro and Helen Michel, reported a thin layer of clay at the boundary
between the Cretaceous and the Paleogene periods at **Gubbio, Italy**. It held up to **160 times** the usual amount of
**iridium**, a metal rare in the Earth's crust but common in asteroids. They proposed that an asteroid about 10 km
across had hit the Earth, that its dust had spread round the world, and that this had killed the dinosaurs. The Dutch
paleontologist **Jan Smit** reached the same idea independently.

**The missing crater.** In 1978 the geophysicists **Glen Penfield** and **Antonio Camargo**, working for Mexico's
state oil company Pemex on an airborne survey north of the Yucatán Peninsula, found a huge buried arc. Their evidence
was presented in 1981, the same week as a meeting on impacts, but few scientists noticed. In **1990** Penfield got in
touch with **Alan R. Hildebrand**, and samples from old oil wells showed that it was an impact. The evidence includes
**shocked quartz**, a **gravity anomaly** and **tektites**, drops of glass thrown out by the impact, in the area
around.

**What happened.** Slightly over **66 million years ago** an asteroid about **10 km** across hit what was then a
shallow sea. A study in 2021 found it came in at about **20 km/s** from the north-east, at 45 to 60° to the ground.
Estimates of the energy range from about $10^{23}$ J to $10^{25}$ J; one common figure is **$4.2 \times 10^{23}$ J**,
equal to 100 million megatons of TNT. It dug a **transient cavity** about 100 km wide and 30 km deep, which collapsed
into a crater about **200 km** wide. It is now buried under about a kilometre of sediment and named after the village
of Chicxulub Pueblo. Waves more than **100 m** high spread out across the sea.

**The extinction.** About **75%** of plant and animal species died, including all the dinosaurs except the birds. In
March 2010 a team of **41 scientists** reviewed twenty years of research and concluded that the Chicxulub impact was
the cause, ruling out other ideas such as huge volcanic eruptions. In **2016** Expedition 364 of the International
Ocean Discovery Program drilled into the crater's **peak ring** down to **1,335 m** below the sea floor and brought
up its rocks.

## 11.6 Danger and defence { #defence }

**How often.** Small rocks hit all the time; big ones are rare.

- A rock about **4 m** across enters the air about **once a year**.
- One of about **7 m** comes about **every 5 years**, carrying the energy of the Hiroshima bomb, about 16 kilotons,
  though it bursts in the air with only about 5 kilotons.
- One of about **20 m**, like the one that burst over **Chelyabinsk** in 2013, comes about **twice a century**. The
  Chelyabinsk and Tunguska blasts are in book 1 (section 15.5, above).
- One of **1 km** hits about every **500,000 years**, and one of **5 km** about every **20 million** years.
- The last of **10 km** or more was Chicxulub, 66 million years ago.

As a rule, craters get rarer about as the **cube** of their size: a crater ten times wider is about a thousand times
rarer (Figure B).

**Apophis.** Asteroid 99942 Apophis, about **340 to 370 m** across, was found on **19 June 2004** by Roy A. Tucker,
David J. Tholen and Fabrizio Bernardi at Kitt Peak National Observatory. By **27 December 2004** the chance that it
would hit the Earth in 2029 had reached **2.7%**, and it was rated 4 on the Torino scale, the highest any object has
reached. Older images found the same day ruled the hit out. On **13 April 2029** it will pass about **31,600 km**
above the Earth's surface, closer than the satellites in geostationary orbit, and shine at magnitude 3.1, bright
enough to see without a telescope from parts of Europe, Africa and western Asia. NASA's **OSIRIS-APEX** will study it
after the flyby, and the ESA–JAXA mission **Ramses** is planned to meet it before.

**2024 YR4.** The ATLAS survey's station at **Río Hurtado in Chile** found 2024 YR4 on **27 December 2024**. Its
chance of hitting the Earth on 22 December 2032 rose to **3.1%** on **18 February 2025**, and by 23 February more
measurements had ruled it out. The James Webb Space Telescope measured it at about **60 m**. Then the Moon became the
target: in May 2025 there was about a **4%** chance it would hit the Moon, until two more Webb observations in
February 2026 brought that to **zero**. It will pass about **21,200 km** above the Moon's surface.

**Finding them first.** Most of the danger is from objects not yet found. In 2005 the US Congress asked NASA to find
90% of near-Earth objects larger than **140 m**. **NEO Surveyor**, an infrared telescope planned to launch in
September 2027, will watch from a point 1.5 million km sunward of the Earth, where it can see asteroids inside the
Earth's orbit. Asteroids are dark, but warm, so they shine in infrared light. If one is found heading for us early
enough, a small push can make it miss: NASA's DART did this to Dimorphos in 2022, and ESA's Hera is due there in
November 2026 to measure the result (chapter 9, [section 9.6](09-The-Asteroid-Belt-and-Ceres.md#visits)).

**Crowded with craters.** Mars has **hundreds of thousands** of craters wider than 1 km, but only about a thousand
have names. Big ones are named after scientists and science-fiction writers, smaller ones under 60 km after towns
on the Earth, and none after living people. The total wider than 25 m may be about 90 million.

**Basins.** The biggest are **Utopia**, about **3,300 km** across and now buried, the largest recognised impact basin
in the solar system, then **Hellas** at 2,300 km, **Argyre** at 1,800 km and **Isidis** at 1,500 km. The whole low
northern half of Mars may be one even larger basin, but that is disputed (chapter 8,
[section 8.2](08-Mars-among-the-Planets.md#halves)). Hellas's floor is about **7,152 m** deep, 3,000 m deeper than
the Moon's South Pole–Aitken basin.

**Mud-like ejecta.** Many Martian craters are **rampart craters**. Their ejecta did not fly out and fall like
gravel; it flowed along the ground like a mudflow and stopped in a low ridge, sometimes going round small obstacles.
Small rampart craters are found only at high latitudes, where ice is thought to lie close to the surface: the
impact has to reach the ice to make the ejecta flow. On the Earth only one example is known, the Nördlinger Ries in
Germany, and Lonar in India has similar layered ejecta (section 11.3).

**A crater made in 2021.** On **24 December 2021** a meteoroid hit **Amazonis Planitia** and dug a crater about **150
m** wide and 21 m deep, throwing out blocks of buried **ice**. NASA's InSight lander felt it as a magnitude 4 quake,
and the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter photographed the fresh crater. The results were published in *Science* on 27
October 2022. Mars's air is so thin that even small meteoroids reach the ground, so new craters are still being made.

**Where rovers landed.** Craters make good landing sites, because their floors are flat and their walls expose
deep layers.

- **Gale**, 154 km and 3.5 to 3.8 billion years old, is named after **Walter F. Gale**, an amateur astronomer from
  Sydney, Australia, who observed Mars in the late 1800s. NASA's **Curiosity** landed there on 6 August 2012 and is
  climbing **Aeolis Mons**, a mound of layered rock 5.5 km high in the middle.
- **Jezero**, 45 km across, once held a lake fed by a river that left a **delta**. NASA's **Perseverance** landed
  there on 18 February 2021.
- **Utopia** is where NASA's **Viking 2** landed on 3 September 1976 and China's **Zhurong** rover on 14 May 2021.

**The clock, moved to Mars.** Chapter 7 showed how crater counts on the Moon, tied to dated Apollo and Chang'e 5
rocks, give ages in years. Mars has no dated rocks from known places, so scientists use the Moon's clock and adjust
it: Mars is nearer the asteroid belt, so it is hit more often, and its gravity and lower impact speeds change the
size of each crater. That is how the ages of Mars's periods in chapter 8 were found
([section 8.4](08-Mars-among-the-Planets.md#ages)), and why they carry large uncertainties.

## 11.8 Working it out { #working }

**Energy per kilogram.** At 20 km/s,

$$\tfrac12 v^2 = \tfrac12 \times 20{,}000^2 = 2 \times 10^8 \text{ J per kg}.$$

TNT gives about $4.184 \times 10^6$ J per kg, so the rock carries $2 \times 10^8 / 4.184 \times 10^6 = 48$ times as
much as its own weight of TNT.

**Chicxulub.** Take a ball of rock 10 km wide, so 5,000 m in radius, with density 3,000 kg/m³:

$$m = \tfrac43 \pi r^3 \rho = \tfrac43 \pi \times 5{,}000^3 \times 3{,}000 = 1.57 \times 10^{15} \text{ kg},$$

$$E = \tfrac12 m v^2 = \tfrac12 \times 1.57 \times 10^{15} \times 20{,}000^2 = 3.1 \times 10^{23} \text{ J}.$$

A megaton of TNT is $4.184 \times 10^{15}$ J, so this is 75 million megatons, close to the 100 million of section
11.5.

**Meteor Crater.** An iron ball 50 m wide (radius 25 m, density 7,800 kg/m³) has a mass of
$\tfrac43 \pi \times 25^3 \times 7{,}800 = 5.1 \times 10^8$ kg. At 12.8 km/s,

$$E = \tfrac12 \times 5.1 \times 10^8 \times 12{,}800^2 = 4.2 \times 10^{16} \text{ J} = 10 \text{ megatons},$$

the same as the published estimate. If half the iron really boiled away in the air, the ground got about 5 megatons.

**Simple to complex.** If the switch size goes as $1/g$ (section 11.9), scale the Moon's 20 km:

$$D_\text{Earth} = 20 \times \frac{1.62}{9.81} = 3.3 \text{ km}, \quad D_\text{Mars} = 20 \times \frac{1.62}{3.73} = 8.7
\text{ km}, \quad D_\text{Mercury} = 20 \times \frac{1.62}{3.70} = 8.8 \text{ km}.$$

The Earth's 3.3 km is inside the observed 2 to 4 km, which is a fair test of the idea.

**Apophis in 2029.** Its 31,600 km above the surface plus the Earth's radius of 6,371 km puts it 37,971 km from the
Earth's centre, about 6 Earth radii. Geostationary satellites are at $35{,}786 + 6{,}371 = 42{,}157$ km. The Moon is
384,400 km away, ten times farther.

**How wide for its size.** Meteor Crater against its iron: $1{,}200/50 = 24$. Chicxulub against its asteroid:
$200/10 = 20$.

## 11.9 Crater size from energy { #derive }

Why does a crater ten million times more energetic come out only about 56 times wider? A simple energy balance gives
the answer.

**Lifting the rock.** A crater of width $D$ is roughly a hole of depth about $D/5$, so the rock removed has a volume
proportional to $D^3$ and a mass proportional to $\rho D^3$, where $\rho$ is the density of the ground. Much of it
has to be lifted out, through a height proportional to $D$, against gravity $g$. The work needed is

$$W \propto \rho D^3 \times g \times D = \rho g D^4.$$

If a fixed share of the impact energy $E$ goes into this lifting, then $E \propto \rho g D^4$, or

$$D \approx \left(\frac{k E}{\rho g}\right)^{1/4}$$

for some constant $k$. This holds for large craters, where gravity, not the strength of the rock, is what the
explosion fights.

**Testing it.** For Meteor Crater, $E = 4.2 \times 10^{16}$ J, $\rho = 2{,}500$ kg/m³ for the sandstone and
limestone, and $g = 9.81$:

$$\left(\frac{E}{\rho g}\right)^{1/4} = \left(\frac{4.2 \times 10^{16}}{2{,}500 \times 9.81}\right)^{1/4} = (1.7 \times
10^{12})^{1/4} = 1{,}143 \text{ m}.$$

The real crater is 1.2 km, so $k$ is close to 1. Now use the same formula for Chicxulub, with $E = 4.2 \times 10^{23}$
J, ten million times more:

$$D = 1{,}143 \times (10^7)^{1/4} = 1{,}143 \times 56.2 = 64 \text{ km}.$$

That is to be compared with the **transient cavity**, about 100 km, before it collapsed to 200 km. A single constant
from a 1 km crater gets within a factor of two of a 100 km one: not bad for a jump of ten million in energy. Careful
scaling laws, fitted to experiments, use powers close to, but not exactly, 1/4, because the share of energy that goes
into lifting changes a little with size and speed.

**Another check, on the Moon.** Had 2024 YR4 hit the Moon, its energy would have been about 5.2 megatons, or
$2.2 \times 10^{16}$ J. With $g = 1.62$,

$$D = \left(\frac{2.2 \times 10^{16}}{2{,}500 \times 1.62}\right)^{1/4} = 1{,}500 \text{ m},$$

inside the 500 to 2,000 m that the full models predicted.

**The same hit on other worlds.** With everything else equal, $D \propto g^{-1/4}$. The Meteor Crater impact would
make a crater $(9.81/3.73)^{1/4} = 1.27$ times wider on Mars, about 1.46 km, and $(9.81/1.62)^{1/4} = 1.57$ times
wider on the Moon, about 1.8 km. Weaker gravity means a bigger hole for the same punch. In practice impacts on the
Moon and Mars are also slower on average, which works the other way.

**Why the bowls collapse.** A crater wall of height $h$ presses on its base with a stress of about $\rho g h$, the
same idea as the pressure inside Ceres in chapter 9 ([section 9.9](09-The-Asteroid-Belt-and-Ceres.md#derive)). The
wall gives way when this beats the strength $Y$ of the broken rock. Since $h$ is a fixed fraction of $D$, the
largest crater that can stay a bowl has

$$D_\text{switch} \propto \frac{Y}{\rho g}.$$

For the same kind of rock, the switch size goes as $1/g$, which is the rule used in sections 11.2 and 11.8, and it
fits the Earth and the Moon.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Why has the Moon kept billions of years of craters while the Earth has lost most of its own?

??? note "Answer"
    The Moon has no rain, rivers or moving plates. On the Earth, erosion wears craters down, sediment buries them and
    plates carry the sea floor into the mantle.

**2.** (beginner) What is the difference between a simple crater and a complex one?

??? note "Answer"
    A simple crater is a smooth bowl. A complex crater is bigger: its walls have slumped into terraces, its floor is
    flat and it has a central peak or a ring of peaks.

**3.** (beginner) What did Chao and Shoemaker find at Meteor Crater in 1960, and why did it matter?

??? note "Answer"
    Coesite, a form of silica made only at enormous pressure. Volcanoes cannot make it, so it proved the crater came
    from an impact.

**4.** (intermediate) Give three pieces of evidence that a 10 km asteroid hit the Earth 66 million years ago.

??? note "Answer"
    Any three of: the worldwide clay layer rich in iridium; shocked quartz; tektites; the buried Chicxulub crater,
    with its gravity anomaly, dated to the same time as the extinction.

**5.** (intermediate) Why are small rampart craters on Mars found only at high latitudes?

??? note "Answer"
    The ejecta flows like mud only if the impact reaches ice in the ground. Near the poles the ice is close to the
    surface, so even small impacts reach it; nearer the equator only bigger impacts dig deep enough.

**6.** (intermediate) Roughly how often does a 1 km object hit the Earth, and how often a 20 m one?

??? note "Answer"
    A 1 km object about every 500,000 years; a 20 m one about twice a century.

**7.** (advanced) A stony object 20 m across (density 3,000 kg/m³) hits the air at 18 km/s. What is its energy in
kilotons of TNT ($4.184 \times 10^{12}$ J each)?

??? note "Answer"
    $m = \tfrac43 \pi \times 10^3 \times 3{,}000 = 1.26 \times 10^7$ kg, and
    $E = \tfrac12 \times 1.26 \times 10^7 \times 18{,}000^2 = 2.0 \times 10^{15}$ J, about 490 kilotons, close to the
    Chelyabinsk blast.

**8.** (expert) Using $D \approx (E/(\rho g))^{1/4}$, how much more energy is needed to make a crater twice as wide?
And how wide would the Meteor Crater impact make a crater on Mars?

??? note "Answer"
    $2^4 = 16$ times the energy. On Mars, $D$ grows by $(9.81/3.73)^{1/4} = 1.27$, so about
    $1.14 \times 1.27 = 1.46$ km using the formula, or about 1.5 km scaling the real 1.2 km.

## Summary

- Impacts on the Earth come at 11 to 72 km/s. The rock explodes and digs a hole about 20 times its own size.
- Small craters are bowls; bigger ones collapse into terraces and central peaks. The change comes at 2 to 4 km on the
  Earth and 20 km on the Moon, set by gravity.
- Coesite and shocked quartz proved craters are impacts; Kaali, Lonar, Meteor Crater and the Ries were early cases.
- About 190 impact structures are confirmed on the Earth; Vredefort is the largest and among the oldest.
- An asteroid about 10 km wide made the Chicxulub crater 66 million years ago and ended the dinosaurs.
- 1 km objects hit every half million years; surveys like NEO Surveyor aim to find dangerous ones in time.
- Mars has hundreds of thousands of craters, mud-like rampart ejecta, buried giant basins and new craters every year.
- Crater size grows only as the fourth root of the energy, and craters are wider where gravity is weaker.
