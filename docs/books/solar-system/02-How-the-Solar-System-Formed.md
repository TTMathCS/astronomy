# 2. How the solar system formed

*About 4.6 billion years ago a cold cloud of gas and dust collapsed. Most of it became the Sun; a thin, spinning disc
round the young Sun became everything else. This chapter follows the story from the cloud to the planets, shows how
we know it, from meteorites to pictures of discs round other young stars, and asks why Mars ended up so small.*

## 2.1 The clues { #clues }

Nobody saw the solar system form, so the story has to be pieced together from what it looks like now. Any good story
has to explain these clues:

- **One direction.** All eight planets go round the Sun the same way, and that is also the way the Sun spins.
- **One plane.** Their orbits lie nearly in one flat plane.
- **Nearly circles.** Most of the orbits are close to circles.
- **Two kinds of planet.** The small rocky planets are close to the Sun and the giants far out (chapter 1).
- **The spin.** The Sun has almost all the mass but almost none of the spin; the planets have the rest (section 1.9).
- **The age.** The oldest solid grains in meteorites are 4,567 million years old, and nothing in the solar system is
  older.

Those grains are small white lumps called **calcium-aluminium-rich inclusions**, found in some stony meteorites. They
formed from very hot gas, over 1,300 K, and are the first solids that condensed as the young disc cooled. The first
one was described in 1968 by Mireille Christophe Michel-Lévy, who found it in the Vigarano meteorite. Their age,
measured from the decay of uranium into lead, is used as the start of the solar system: **4,567.3 million years**.

There are exceptions to the rules. Venus spins backwards, and Neptune's big moon Triton goes round its planet the
wrong way. Exceptions like these are clues too: they suggest that later collisions and captures changed a few things.

## 2.2 A collapsing cloud { #collapse }

Between the stars there are cold, dark clouds of gas and dust. The gas is mostly hydrogen and helium; the dust, a
tiny share of the mass, is grains of rock, metal and ice. Sometimes part of a cloud starts to fall in on itself
under its own gravity.

Every cloud turns a little. As it falls in, it turns faster, the way a skater spins faster by pulling in their arms.
Gas falling along the spin axis has nothing to stop it, but gas falling in from the sides is held out by its spin.
So the cloud flattens. In about **100,000 years** the collapse made a spinning disc about **200 AU across**, with a
hot, dense ball in the middle: the young Sun.

At first the young Sun shone only from the heat of its collapse. It was a **T Tauri star**, a kind of very young
star with strong winds, and nuclear fusion started in its core within about 50 million years. Discs like the one
round it last less than about 10 million years before the gas is gone. The oldest disc found round another star is
25 million years old.

We can now see such discs. In 2014 the radio telescope **ALMA** took a picture of the disc round **HL Tauri**, a young
star about 450 light-years away. It showed bright rings separated by dark gaps. "These features are almost certainly
the result of young planet-like bodies that are being formed in the disc," said Stuartt Corder, ALMA's deputy
director. The star is no more than a million years old, so planets seem to start forming very early.

## 2.3 From dust to planets { #growth }

Planets grew from the dust in the disc, in steps (Figure A).

- **Dust to pebbles.** Grains bump gently into each other and stick, as dust does in a corner of a room, making
  fluffy clumps and then pebbles.
- **Pebbles to planetesimals.** Somehow pebbles a centimetre across become bodies a kilometre or more across, called
  **planetesimals**. This is the least understood step. One idea is that swarms of pebbles crowd together in the gas
  until their own gravity pulls each swarm into one body.
- **Planetesimals to embryos.** Once a body is big enough, its gravity pulls in more. The biggest grow fastest, so a
  few bodies race ahead of the rest, called **runaway growth**. Near the Sun these **planetary embryos** reached
  about 0.05 Earth masses, close to the mass of Mercury, within about 100,000 years.
- **Embryos to planets.** The embryos then crashed into each other. Over less than about 100 million years the
  collisions built the four rocky planets. The Earth took 50 to 100 million years to reach its full size.

Near the Sun only rock and metal could be solid, and they make up only about 0.6% of the mass of the cloud. That is
why the rocky planets are small.

## 2.4 The snow line { #snowline }

Close to the young Sun it was too warm for ice. Water could only be gas there. Farther out, where it was colder than
about 150 to 170 K, water froze onto the dust as ice. The distance where that happens is called the **snow line** or
frost line (Figure B).

This one line explains the two kinds of planet:

- **Inside the snow line** only rock and metal were solid, so the embryos stayed small: Mercury, Venus, the Earth and
  Mars.
- **Outside it** there was ice as well, and ice was more plentiful than rock and metal. Cores there grew to about 4
  Earth masses within about 3 million years, big enough to pull in hydrogen and helium from the disc.

**Jupiter** grew just outside the snow line, where material piled up, and became the biggest. **Saturn** formed a few
million years later and got less gas. **Uranus and Neptune** came last, when the young Sun's wind had already blown
much of the gas away. They got no more than about 1 Earth mass of hydrogen and helium each, and are sometimes called
"failed cores".

Where exactly was the snow line? The inner asteroid belt is mostly dry, while the outer belt has icy asteroids, so
when the planetesimals formed the line was probably at about 2.7 AU. Ceres, at 2.77 AU, sits almost on it and seems
to have an icy mantle. The line moved as the disc changed. Today bare ice in full sunlight lasts only beyond about
5 AU; nearer in, it survives only when buried or in shade. Section 2.9 works out the temperatures.

## 2.5 Who worked it out { #history }

The idea that the planets formed from a spinning cloud is nearly 300 years old.

- **1734 to 1796.** Emanuel Swedenborg proposed parts of it in 1734. Immanuel Kant developed it in 1755 in his
  *Universal Natural History and Theory of the Heavens*: slowly turning clouds collapse and flatten, and planets form
  in them. Pierre-Simon Laplace proposed a similar picture in 1796 in his *Exposition du système du monde*: a
  shrinking, cooling cloud spins faster and leaves rings behind that become planets. This is the **nebular
  hypothesis**.
- **Out of favour.** It could not explain the spin clue: why does the Sun turn so slowly? By the start of the 20th
  century most astronomers had given it up. Others tried instead: the planetesimal theory of Thomas Chamberlin and
  Forest Moulton (1901), the idea of James Jeans (1917) that a passing star pulled the planets out of the Sun, the
  accretion model of Otto Schmidt (1944) and the protoplanet theory of William McCrea (1960).
- **The modern picture.** Otto Schmidt founded a school of planet-formation research in the USSR. One of its members,
  the Soviet astronomer **Viktor Safronov** (1917–1999), worked out in detail how planets grow from a disc of dust
  and planetesimals. His book of 1969, published in Moscow, was translated into English by NASA in 1972. George
  Wetherill developed his ideas further and found runaway growth. A minor planet and a region on Pluto, Safronov
  Regio, are named after Safronov.
- **The disc.** The Japanese astrophysicist **Chushiro Hayashi** (1920–2010), born in Kyoto and later a professor
  at Kyoto University, is known for the Hayashi tracks that show how young stars settle down. His 1981 model of the
  disc is still quoted for the snow line: 170 K at 2.7 AU.
- **The spin.** Hannes Alfvén suggested that the young Sun's strong wind carried its spin away. Magnetic fields
  tying the young Sun to its disc may have helped as well.

Today the theory is tested against thousands of planets round other stars and against pictures of discs like HL
Tauri's. For the history of astronomy as a whole, see the [history book](../history/index.md); Kant appears there
in [chapter 19](../history/19-Galaxies-and-the-Expanding-Universe.md#milkyway).

## 2.6 Wandering giants { #migration }

The planets did not all form where they are now. A big planet in a gas disc trades pulls with the gas around it and
slowly drifts in or out, called **migration**. Two well-known models describe what the giants may have done.

- **The Grand Tack.** Jupiter formed at about 3.5 AU and drifted inwards with the gas, as far as 1.5 AU, about where
  Mars is now. Then Saturn caught up and the two locked into a resonance, a regular pattern of meetings (book 3,
  [chapter 11](../orbits/11-Resonances.md#apart)). Together they drifted back out, and Jupiter ended near 5.2 AU. The
  name comes from sailing: a boat "tacks" when it turns to sail back the other way. On its way in, Jupiter cleared
  away most of the planetesimals beyond about 1 AU, and it crossed the asteroid belt twice, stirring it up.
- **The Nice model.** Worked out in 2005 at the Côte d'Azur Observatory in Nice, France, it starts the giants closer
  together and moves them out to their present orbits long after the gas was gone. Their shuffling would have thrown
  many icy bodies inwards and outwards. It is used to explain the **Late Heavy Bombardment** of the inner planets,
  the Kuiper belt, the Oort cloud and the Trojan asteroids that share Jupiter's and Neptune's orbits.

Neither model is proved, but both fit many clues, and both say the young solar system was far less calm than it is
now.

??? note "Sources"
    - The Grand Tack, Jupiter forming at 3.5 AU, moving in to 1.5 AU, turning when Saturn was caught in resonance,
      halting near 5.2 AU, the disc cut off at 1.0 AU, and Jupiter crossing the asteroid belt twice: Wikipedia,
      [Grand tack hypothesis](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Grand_tack_hypothesis) (weaker).
    - The Nice model, from 2005 at the Côte d'Azur Observatory, the giants migrating after the disc was gone, and what

## 2.7 Why Mars is small { #mars }

Mars has only about a tenth of the Earth's mass. Why did it stop growing?

In 2011 Nicolas Dauphas of the University of Chicago and Ali Pourmand of the University of Miami used meteorites from
Mars to time its birth. They used **hafnium-182**, which turns into tungsten-182 with a half-life of 9 million years.
A planet that made its iron core while hafnium-182 was still around ends up with extra tungsten-182 in its rocks.
From the amount in Mars's rocks they found that Mars formed in as little as **2 to 4 million years**, while the
Earth took 50 to 100 million. Earlier estimates for Mars had gone as high as 15 million years.

"Earth was made of embryos like Mars," Dauphas said, "but Mars is a stranded planetary embryo that never collided with
other embryos to make an Earthlike planet." The idea had first been suggested about 20 years before from computer
models of planets growing.

So Mars may be one of the embryos of section 2.3 that survived. Why was there so little left to collide with near
1.5 AU? The Grand Tack offers one answer: when Jupiter swung in, it cleared away most of the planetesimals beyond
about 1 AU, so Mars was left short of material.

??? note "Sources"
    - Dauphas and Pourmand's study in Nature, the 2 to 4 million years against 50 to 100 for the Earth, the earlier
      estimates of up to 15 million years, the half-life of 9 million years, Dauphas's words, and the idea first
      proposed 20 years before: University of Chicago (above).
    - The Grand Tack limiting the material for Mars: Wikipedia, Grand tack hypothesis (above).
    - Extra tungsten-182 in a planet whose core formed early: standard.

## 2.8 Working it out { #working }

**Spinning up.** Something moving sideways round a centre keeps the same **speed times distance** as it falls in:
this is the angular momentum of section 1.9 again. Suppose a bit of gas is 10,000 AU from the centre, moving sideways
at only 10 m/s (0.01 km/s). When it has fallen to 100 AU it is moving sideways at

$$v = 0.01 \times \frac{10{,}000}{100} = 1 \text{ km/s}.$$

To stay in orbit at 100 AU it would need $29.8/\sqrt{100} = 3.0$ km/s, so it keeps falling. It stops falling when its
spin speed matches the orbit speed:

$$\frac{0.01 \times 10{,}000}{r} = \frac{29.8}{\sqrt r} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \sqrt r = \frac{100}{29.8} = 3.4, \quad
r = 11 \text{ AU}.$$

So even a very slow turn far out stops the gas at about 11 AU, where it goes into orbit at about 9 km/s. Gas falling
along the spin axis has no sideways motion and keeps falling, so the cloud ends up as a flat disc.

**A clock that runs out.** Hafnium-182 halves every 9 million years, so the share left after $t$ million years is
$(1/2)^{t/9}$.

| | Mars | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Distance from the Sun | 1.52 AU | 1 AU |
| Temperature of a dark ball there (section 2.9) | 225 K | 278 K |
| Inside the snow line? | yes | yes |
| Time to grow | 2 to 4 million years | 50 to 100 million years |
| Hafnium-182 left when it finished growing | 74% to 86% | 2% to 0.05% |
| Mass | 0.107 Earths | 1 |

For Mars, $(1/2)^{2/9} = 0.86$ and $(1/2)^{4/9} = 0.73$: most of the hafnium-182 was still there, so the clock left
a clear mark. For the Earth, $(1/2)^{50/9} = 0.02$: the clock had nearly run out, which is why the Earth's age is
harder to pin down this way.

**Where the time went.** The Earth took 12 to 50 times as long to grow as Mars: $50/4 = 12.5$ and $100/2 = 50$.

## 2.9 The snow line worked out { #derive }

How warm does a body get at distance $r$ from the Sun? Book 2,
[section 6.9](../light-and-telescopes/06-Hot-Things-Glow.md#balance), balances sunlight in against infrared out:

$$T = \left(\frac{S(1 - A)}{4\sigma}\right)^{1/4},$$

with $S$ the sunlight on each square metre, $A$ the share reflected and $\sigma = 5.67 \times 10^{-8}$ W m⁻² K⁻⁴.
Dust in the disc is dark, so take $A = 0$. Sunlight spreads out as $1/r^2$, with $S = 1361$ W/m² at 1 AU:

$$T = \left(\frac{1361}{4 \times 5.67 \times 10^{-8}}\right)^{1/4} \left(\frac{r}{1 \text{ AU}}\right)^{-1/2} = 278
\text{ K} \times \left(\frac{r}{1 \text{ AU}}\right)^{-1/2}.$$

This gives Mercury 447 K, the Earth 278 K, Mars 225 K, Ceres 167 K, Jupiter 122 K and Neptune 51 K (Figure B).

**The snow line.** Turn the formula round: ice forms where $T$ is below $T_\text{ice}$, beyond

$$r_\text{snow} = \left(\frac{278 \text{ K}}{T_\text{ice}}\right)^2 \text{ AU}.$$

For $T_\text{ice} = 150$ K, $r = 3.4$ AU; for 170 K, $r = 2.7$ AU, the same as Hayashi's value.

**A fainter Sun.** The Sun is slowly brightening, by about 10% every 1.1 billion years. Going back 4.57 billion years
at that rate, the young Sun was about $1.1^{-4.57/1.1} = 0.67$ of today's brightness. $T$ goes as $L^{1/4}$, so
$r_\text{snow}$ goes as $\sqrt L$: $\sqrt{0.67} = 0.82$, and the snow line moves in to 2.8 AU for 150 K or 2.2 AU for
170 K.

**The real disc.** These numbers are for a dark grain in plain sunlight. The young disc was thick and dusty, so
sunlight hardly reached its middle, which stayed cooler. Models of the disc put the snow line between about 2.7 and
3.2 AU, close to our faint-Sun answer. Bare ice today is another matter: in full sunlight it slowly turns to gas even
when it is colder than 150 K, so it lasts only beyond about 5 AU, where $T = 278/\sqrt 5 = 124$ K. Either way, the
line falls between Mars and Jupiter.

    - The Sun brightening by 10% every 1.1 billion years: Wikipedia, Formation and evolution of the Solar System

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) How old is the solar system, and what is the oldest thing we have from it?

??? note "Answer"
    About 4,567 million years. The oldest things are small white grains in some meteorites, the
    calcium-aluminium-rich inclusions.

**2.** (beginner) Why does a collapsing cloud end up as a flat disc instead of a ball?

??? note "Answer"
    It spins faster as it shrinks. Gas falling in from the sides is held out by its spin, but gas falling along the
    spin axis is not, so the cloud flattens.

**3.** (beginner) Name the four steps from dust to planets.

??? note "Answer"
    Dust sticks into pebbles; pebbles gather into planetesimals; the biggest planetesimals run away into planetary
    embryos; embryos collide and merge into planets.

**4.** (intermediate) What is the snow line, and why are the giant planets outside it?

??? note "Answer"
    The distance from the young Sun beyond which water froze into ice. Outside it there was ice as well as rock and
    metal, so cores grew big enough, a few Earth masses, to pull in hydrogen and helium from the disc before the gas
    was gone.

**5.** (intermediate) Why did astronomers give up the nebular hypothesis around 1900, and who brought the modern form
of it?

??? note "Answer"
    It could not explain why the Sun spins so slowly when the planets carry nearly all the spin. The modern form
    traces to Viktor Safronov, in the USSR, whose 1969 book showed how planets grow from a disc of planetesimals.

**6.** (intermediate) Why are Uranus and Neptune called "failed cores"?

??? note "Answer"
    They grew late, after the young Sun's wind had blown most of the gas away, so they gathered no more than about
    1 Earth mass of hydrogen and helium each.

**7.** (advanced) How much hafnium-182 was left after 3 million years, and after 75 million years?

??? note "Answer"
    $(1/2)^{3/9} = 0.79$, so 79%; $(1/2)^{75/9} = 0.003$, so 0.3%.

**8.** (expert) A star is 4 times as bright as the Sun. Where is its snow line in plain light, taking ice to form
below 150 K?

??? note "Answer"
    $r_\text{snow}$ goes as $\sqrt L$, so it is $\sqrt 4 = 2$ times the Sun's: $2 \times 3.4 = 6.9$ AU.

## Summary

- The solar system formed from a collapsing, spinning cloud of gas and dust about 4,567 million years ago.
- The cloud spun up and flattened into a disc about 200 AU across, with the young Sun in the middle.
- Dust grew into pebbles, planetesimals and embryos; the embryos near the Sun merged into the rocky planets.
- Beyond the snow line, ice let cores grow big enough to gather gas and become the giants.
- The idea goes back to Kant and Laplace; Safronov and others made the modern theory, and ALMA has photographed
  discs where planets are forming now.
- The giants probably wandered in and out, which may have shaped the asteroid belt and left Mars short of material.
- Mars grew in only 2 to 4 million years and stopped: it may be a stranded planetary embryo.
