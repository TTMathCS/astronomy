# 15. Moons of the giants

*The giant planets have hundreds of moons between them. Most are small lumps, but a few are worlds as big as
planets, and some of them are more interesting than the planets they go round. Io has more volcanoes than anywhere
else in the solar system. Europa, Ganymede and Enceladus hide oceans of salty water under their ice. Titan has thick
air, methane rain, and seas. Triton goes round Neptune backwards and has one of the coldest surfaces known. This
chapter covers the big moons, Io's volcanoes, the buried oceans, Titan and the rest, how the moons were found, the
spacecraft that have visited and the ones on the way, how the moons compare with Mars, and how the heat coming up from
inside sets the thickness of a lid of ice.*

    - how the moons compare with Mars, its two small moons and its buried ice

## 15.1 The big moons { #basics }

**Seven big ones.** Seven moons in the solar system are wider than 2,500 km. Four go round Jupiter, one round Saturn,
one round Neptune, and one is our own Moon.

| Moon | Planet | Radius | Density | Time to go round |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Ganymede | Jupiter | 2,631 km | 1,940 kg/m³ | 7.155 days |
| Titan | Saturn | 2,575 km | 1,880 kg/m³ | 15.945 days |
| Callisto | Jupiter | 2,410 km | 1,830 kg/m³ | 16.689 days |
| Io | Jupiter | 1,822 km | 3,530 kg/m³ | 1.769 days |
| the Moon | Earth | 1,737 km | 3,344 kg/m³ | 27.32 days |
| Europa | Jupiter | 1,561 km | 3,010 kg/m³ | 3.551 days |
| Triton | Neptune | 1,353 km | 2,050 kg/m³ | 5.877 days, backwards |

**Wider than a planet.** Mercury's radius is 2,440 km, so **Ganymede** is 8% wider and **Titan** 5% wider. But
Mercury is mostly iron and rock, while these two moons are a mix of rock and ice, so Titan has only **40%** of
Mercury's mass.

**Rock or ice?** The density tells you. Ice is a little under 1,000 kg/m³ and rock about 3,000, so Io and Europa,
like our Moon, are mostly rock, while Ganymede, Callisto and Titan, near 1,900, must have a lot of ice in them. The
four big moons of Jupiter get less dense the farther they are from it: Io's density is between rock and iron, and
Callisto's between ice and rock.

**One face to the planet.** Like our Moon, the big moons have been slowed by tides until they turn once in each trip
round their planet, so they always keep the same face towards it (book 3,
[section 8.6](../orbits/08-Tides.md#locking)).

**And the small ones.** Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune together have hundreds of moons (chapters 12 to 14).
Most are only a few kilometres across and many are probably captured bits of rock and ice. Only the bigger ones have
been pulled round by their own gravity.

## 15.2 Io, the volcano moon { #io }

**More volcanoes than anywhere else.** Io has **more than 400** active volcanoes. Some throw plumes of sulfur and
sulfur dioxide up to **500 km** high, and the lava comes out at **1,300 K** or more, some as hot as **1,600 K**. The
biggest volcanic pit, **Loki Patera**, is 202 km across and on average gives out about a quarter of all Io's heat.
Most of the surface is plains coated in sulfur and sulfur dioxide frost. There are no impact craters to be seen,
because lava covers them faster than they are made.

**Mountains.** Io also has 100 to 150 mountains, not volcanoes but blocks of crust pushed up by squeezing. They
average 6 km high, and the tallest, South Boösaule Montes, reaches **17.5 km**, twice the height of Everest.

**Squeezed by Jupiter.** Io is about the size of our Moon, and a moon that small should have cooled long ago
(chapter 6, [section 6.9](06-The-Earth-as-a-Planet.md#derive)). What
keeps it hot is a tide. Io goes round Jupiter twice for each trip of Europa and four times for each of Ganymede's, and
their regular tugs keep its orbit slightly stretched. So Io's distance from Jupiter changes a little in every orbit,
and Jupiter's huge tide squeezes and relaxes it, heating it the way a ball of putty warms when you knead it. Stan
Peale, Patrick Cassen and R. Reynolds worked this out shortly before Voyager 1 flew past in March 1979, and soon
afterwards Linda Morabito, a navigation engineer, spotted a plume in one of its pictures (book 3,
[section 11.4](../orbits/11-Resonances.md#io)).

**Inside.** Unlike most moons of the outer solar system, Io is made of rock round a core of iron or iron sulfide.
In 2009 a new look at the Galileo spacecraft's magnetic readings suggested a layer of molten rock, a **magma ocean**,
about 50 km down. But a study in *Nature* in 2024, using gravity measured on two close passes by Juno, found no such
ocean. The magma seems instead to sit in large pockets joined through a mostly solid shell, rather like a sponge.

**A ring of gas round Jupiter.** Io's volcanoes throw out up to about a tonne of sulfur dioxide every second, and much
of it ends up as a doughnut of charged sulfur and oxygen round Jupiter, the **Io torus** (chapter 12,
[section 12.5](12-Jupiter.md#magnet)).

## 15.3 Oceans under the ice { #oceans }

**How can you find an ocean you cannot see?** Nobody has seen the water under the ice of these moons. The evidence
comes in four kinds:

- **Magnetism.** As Jupiter's tilted magnetic field sweeps past a moon, it makes electric currents flow in anything
  that conducts. Salty water does. The Galileo spacecraft, in orbit round Jupiter from 1995 to 2003, found that
  Europa, Ganymede and Callisto all have magnetic fields made this way, which a buried salty layer explains.
- **Aurorae.** In 2015 Hubble measured how Ganymede's aurorae shift as Jupiter's field sweeps past. A big ocean of
  salt water changes the way they move, and the measurements confirmed Ganymede's ocean.
- **Wobbles.** Enceladus rocks back and forth a little as it goes round Saturn, by 0.120°. That is far too much for a
  solid moon, but just right if its icy crust floats free of the rock inside, on a **global ocean**.
- **Sliding crusts.** On Titan, Cassini saw surface features move by up to 30 km between 2005 and 2007, as if the crust
  were sliding over something liquid.

**Europa.** Europa's surface is the smoothest of any solid world in the solar system, with no big mountains or
craters, only cracks and streaks. Under it lies a layer of water roughly 100 km thick, ice on top and liquid below.
The ice is perhaps 10 to 30 km thick, as hard as granite at the surface, which averages **110 K** (−160 °C) at the
equator and only 50 K at the poles. The ocean under it may be up to 100 km deep, with **2 to 3 times** as much water
as all the Earth's oceans. Some
scientists think the ice is only a few kilometres thick, but most think that model counts only its stiff top layer.
Patches called **chaos terrain**, where the surface looks broken up and refrozen, may lie over lakes of water trapped
in the ice. Hubble has seen what may be plumes of water vapour, and in 2018 old Galileo readings were found to
support them. The James Webb Space Telescope found carbon dioxide in a young patch called Tara Regio, which seems to
have come up from the ocean.

**Ganymede.** Ganymede is the only moon known to make its own magnetic field, probably by churning in its core. Its
ocean may be the largest in the solar system, and Juno has seen salts on its surface that probably came up from it.
One model has several layers of ocean between different kinds of ice, about **800 km** down to the rock floor.

**Callisto.** Callisto has the oldest surface of any of these moons, so packed with craters that a new crater can only
erase an older one. Its biggest scar, **Valhalla**, has a bright middle 600 km across and rings out to 1,800 km from
the centre. Callisto is not in step with the other three, so it gets little heat from tides, but it may still have an
ocean more than 100 km down.

**Enceladus.** Enceladus is only 504 km across, yet it is one of the most active worlds known. From long cracks near
its south pole, the **tiger stripes**, more than 100 geysers
throw out about **200 kg** of water vapour, ice and salt every second. Some falls back as snow, up to 700 m deep;
the rest feeds Saturn's E ring (chapter 13, [section 13.3](13-Saturn-and-its-Rings.md#rings)). Its ocean is about 26
to 31 km deep under a crust of ice, and the geysers bring samples of it up for passing spacecraft to taste. They have
found salt, hydrogen, and in 2023 phosphates, which completed the set of six elements life on Earth is built from:
carbon, hydrogen, nitrogen, oxygen, phosphorus and sulfur.

**Why the water does not freeze.** These moons are far from the Sun; Europa's surface is colder than anywhere on the
Earth. The heat comes from inside. On Europa, as on Io, tides squeeze the moon, and the heat this makes is about 100
times what radioactive rocks inside could give. Salts and ammonia help too, because they lower the freezing point.
Enceladus is a puzzle: its south pole gives out about **4.7 billion watts**, 200 mW for every square metre, about ten
times what radioactivity could give and more than the tides seem able to make today. Section 15.9 works out what this
heat means for the thickness of the ice.

## 15.4 Titan, Triton and the rest { #titan }

**A moon with weather.** Titan is the only moon with thicker air than the Earth's. The pressure at the ground is
**1.45 times** ours, and the air is mostly nitrogen, like ours, with some methane. High up, sunlight breaks the methane
apart, and the pieces join into a brown haze that hides the surface. At the ground it is **94 K** (−179 °C). At that
temperature methane does what water does on the Earth: it forms clouds, falls as rain, cuts river channels, and fills
lakes and seas. The three biggest seas, Kraken Mare, Ligeia Mare and Punga Mare, all near the north pole, hold about
80% of the area of Titan's seas and lakes. Near the equator, wind has piled up dunes that look dark to radar, 1 to
2 km wide, 80 to 130 m high and sometimes more than 100 km long.

**Water below too.** Under the crust of water ice, Titan probably has an ocean of water and ammonia, perhaps as much as
200 km deep in one model. So Titan has liquid in two places: methane on top, water underneath.

**Triton.** Neptune's big moon goes round backwards, against the planet's spin, which is why it is thought to be a
captured body from the Kuiper belt (chapter 14, [section 14.5](14-Uranus-and-Neptune.md#inside)). Its icy surface
reflects most of the sunlight, so it is very cold, about **38 K** (−235 °C), one of the lowest temperatures recorded
in the solar system. Even so, Voyager 2 saw a few plumes of gas and dark dust rising up to **8 km**, and the surface
has very few craters, so it is probably less than 100 million years old. Only about 40% of it has ever been seen.

**Uranus's moons.** Uranus has five round moons. The smallest, **Miranda**, has one of the most varied landscapes of
any world. One cliff, **Verona Rupes**, is roughly 20 km high, perhaps the highest cliff in the solar system.

**Saturn's odd ones.** **Iapetus** is dark on the side that faces forwards in its orbit and bright on the other, and a
ridge runs three-quarters of the way round its equator, so high that it changes the moon's shape. **Mimas** has a
crater, Herschel, 139 km across, about a third of the moon's own width.

## 15.5 Finding the moons { #history }

- **China, 365 BC.** Chinese records say that **Gan De** saw a small reddish star beside Jupiter. It may have been
  Ganymede, seen with the naked eye, though nobody can explain why it was called red (history book,
  [section 15.3](../history/15-Galileo-and-the-Telescope.md#jupiter)).
- **Padua, 1610.** On 7 January 1610 **Galileo Galilei**, with a telescope that magnified 20 times, saw small "stars"
  beside Jupiter, though Io and Europa were too close together to split. On 8 January they were apart, and over the
  nights that followed Galileo found four moons going round Jupiter. **Simon Marius** may have found them
  independently at about the same time. It was Marius who named them, in 1614, following an idea from Kepler in
  October 1613: Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto, people in the stories of Zeus. Moons going round another world
  helped convince people that not everything goes round the Earth.
- **The Hague, 1655.** On 25 March 1655 the Dutch astronomer **Christiaan Huygens**, with help from an elder brother,
  Constantijn, found **Titan**, the first moon of Saturn known.
- **Paris, 1670s.** **Giovanni Domenico Cassini**, an Italian-born French astronomer, found **Iapetus** in October
  1671, then three more moons of Saturn. The eclipses of Jupiter's moons, as they pass into its shadow, came so
  regularly that Cassini and Jean Picard used them as a clock to map France afresh, and Ole Rømer used Io's to show
  that light takes time to travel (history book, [section 16.3](../history/16-Newton-Halley-and-Romer.md#light)).
- **Slough, 1789.** **William Herschel** found **Mimas** and **Enceladus** with a new 40-foot telescope, then the
  largest in the world.
- **Liverpool, 1846.** The English astronomer **William Lassell** found **Triton** on 10 October 1846, only 17 days
  after Neptune itself was found.
- **Air on Titan.** **Josep Comas i Solà** saw that the edge of Titan looked darker than its middle, the first hint
  of air (Wikipedia gives both 1903 and 1907). In 1944 **Gerard Kuiper** found methane in it with a spectroscope.
- **Texas, 1948.** Kuiper found **Miranda** on 16 February 1948, at McDonald Observatory.
- **The space age.** Voyager 1's pictures in 1979 showed volcanoes on Io, and Voyager 2 visited Miranda in 1986 and
  Triton in 1989. Since then Galileo, Cassini and Juno have turned the moons from points of light into places.

## 15.6 Spacecraft at the moons { #missions }

**So far.** The Voyagers gave the first close views in 1979 to 1989. NASA's **Galileo** orbited Jupiter from 1995 to
2003 and found the signs of the buried oceans; at the end it was crashed into Jupiter so that it could never land on
Europa and carry microbes from the Earth there (chapter 12, [section 12.6](12-Jupiter.md#missions)). **Cassini**
orbited Saturn from 2004 to 2017, found the geysers of Enceladus and mapped Titan's seas, and its **Huygens** probe
landed on Titan in 2005 (chapter 13, [section 13.6](13-Saturn-and-its-Rings.md#missions)). **Juno**, in orbit round
Jupiter since 2016, has flown close past Ganymede, Europa and Io.

**JUICE.** Europe's **Jupiter Icy Moons Explorer** was launched on 14 April 2023 from Kourou in French Guiana and is
using four gravity assists to get to Jupiter. On **28 September 2026** it swung past the Earth, gaining 3.5 km/s, and
it is due back for one last pass in 2029. It should reach Jupiter in July 2031, fly past Europa, Ganymede and
Callisto, and in December 2034 settle into orbit round **Ganymede**. It will be the first spacecraft to orbit a moon
other than our own. It cost ESA about €1.5 billion.

**Europa Clipper.** NASA's **Europa Clipper**, the largest spacecraft NASA has sent to another planet, was launched on
14 October 2024. It swung past Mars in March 2025 and is due to swing past the Earth in December 2026. It should
reach Jupiter in April 2030. Europa lies deep inside Jupiter's magnetic field, where the radiation is fierce, so
Clipper will not orbit it. Instead it
will orbit Jupiter and dive past Europa **49 times** from 2031, as close as 25 km, getting out of the radiation between
passes.

**Dragonfly.** NASA's **Dragonfly** is a nuclear-powered drone with eight rotors, the size of a car, due to
launch in July 2028 and reach Titan in 2034. It will land among the dunes of **Ahmakiq Undae**, named in April 2026
after the Mayan wind spirit Ahmakiq, and then fly in hops of up to 8 km towards **Selk**, a young crater 90 km across
with organic chemicals and signs of liquid water in the past. Flying is easy on Titan: the air is 4 times as dense as
ours and gravity is only 14% of the Earth's, so it takes about 40 times less power than flying here.

**And next.** China plans **Tianwen-4** to Jupiter and its moons (chapter 12). Missions have also been proposed to
land on Europa, to fly through the plumes of Enceladus, and to go back to Triton.

**Two kinds of moon.** Mars's moons are small dark lumps, not round worlds: Phobos is about 26 km long and Deimos
16 km (chapter 7, [section 7.7](07-The-Moon.md#mars)). Mars itself is only about 30% wider than Ganymede, and denser
than any of these moons.

**Water under ice.** In 2018 radar on Mars Express found a bright echo **1.5 km** under the south polar ice of Mars,
read as a lake of very salty water at about −68 °C. Others think the echo could come from volcanic rock, or from
brine soaked between grains rather than an open lake (history book,
[section 20.10](../history/20-Radio-Astronomy.md#mars)). Section 15.9 shows why it is hard to
keep water liquid there: with the heat Mars is thought to give out, the bottom of the ice would be about −100 °C. A
lake would need more than twice as much heat as Mars is thought to give, or a local hot spot. On Europa the problem
is the other way round: the ice is thick, but there is plenty of tidal heat beneath it.

**Air and wings.** Mars's air is thin, so its little helicopter **Ingenuity**, which flew in 2021, needed big, fast
blades (history book, [section 21.4](../history/21-The-Space-Age-at-Mars.md#rovers)). Dragonfly will be only the
second craft to fly on another world, and on Titan, with its thick air and low gravity, the flying will be easy. Both
worlds also have big fields of dunes, shaped by the wind.

**Where to look for life.** Mars once had rivers and lakes (chapter 8, [section 8.4](08-Mars-among-the-Planets.md#ages))
and still has a great deal of ice.
The ocean moons have liquid water now, in contact with rock, and Enceladus has every main ingredient for life. Which
is the better place to look is the subject of chapter 20.

## 15.8 Working it out { #working }

**Density of Io.** $\rho = M/(\tfrac43 \pi R^3) = 8.932 \times 10^{22}/(\tfrac43 \pi \times (1.8215 \times 10^6)^3)
= 3{,}528$ kg/m³, as in the table.

**How fast the plumes start.** To rise 500 km above Io, where gravity at the surface is
$g = GM/R^2 = 1.80$ m/s², gas must leave at a speed $v$ with $\tfrac12 v^2 = GM(1/R - 1/(R+h))$, which gives
$v = 1{,}190$ m/s, about 1.2 km/s. Pretending gravity stays the same all the way up gives $\sqrt{2gh} = 1.34$ km/s,
too high, because gravity weakens with height.

**Enceladus's geysers.** At 200 kg/s for 4.5 billion years, Enceladus would lose
$200 \times 4.5 \times 10^9 \times 3.156 \times 10^7 = 2.8 \times 10^{19}$ kg, about **26%** of its mass of
$1.08 \times 10^{20}$ kg. Wikipedia gives about 30%. So the geysers cannot always have been as strong as they are
now, or Enceladus started out bigger.

**Air on Titan.** The air pressing on each square metre of ground weighs $P = mg$, so its mass is $m = P/g$. On Titan,
$g = 1.354$ m/s², and $m = 1.448 \times 101{,}325/1.354 = 108{,}000$ kg. On the Earth, $101{,}325/9.81 = 10{,}300$ kg.
Titan has about **10 times** as much air over each square metre as the Earth.

**Weighing Jupiter with its moons.** By Kepler's third law, $a^3/T^2$ is the same for all moons of one planet. With
$a$ in thousands of km and $T$ in days, Io gives $421.8^3/1.769^2 = 2.398 \times 10^7$, Europa $2.397 \times 10^7$
and Callisto $2.396 \times 10^7$. Converting to metres and seconds and using $GM = 4\pi^2 a^3/T^2$:
$GM = 4\pi^2 \times 2.397 \times 10^{25}/86{,}400^2 = 1.268 \times 10^{17}$ m³/s², so
$M = 1.90 \times 10^{27}$ kg, the mass of Jupiter.

**Ganymede against Mercury.** $2{,}631.2/2{,}439.7 = 1.078$: Ganymede is 8% wider. Titan: $2{,}575/2{,}439.7 = 1.055$.

## 15.9 How thick is the ice? { #derive }

A lid of ice over an ocean is a blanket. Heat leaks up through it from the warm water below to the cold surface, and
the thicker the ice, the less heat gets through. So if we know how much heat is coming up, we can work out how thick
the ice must be, and the other way round.

**Heat through a layer.** Heat flows down a temperature slope. If the temperature rises by $dT$ over a depth $dz$, the
heat flow through each square metre, in W/m², is

$$q = k \frac{dT}{dz},$$

where $k$ is the **thermal conductivity**. If nothing is warming or cooling inside the ice, the same $q$ passes
through every layer.

**Cold ice conducts better.** For ordinary ice, a standard fit to laboratory measurements is

$$k \approx \frac{567}{T}\ \text{W/(m·K)},$$

with $T$ in kelvin. At 273 K that gives 2.1, and at 110 K, 5.2: cold ice passes heat about two and a half times as
well as ice near melting.

**Adding up the layers.** Put the two together: $q = (567/T)\,dT/dz$, so $q\,dz = 567\,dT/T$. Integrate from the
surface, at $T_s$, down to the bottom of the ice, at depth $d$, where it meets water at $T_b$:

$$q\,d = 567 \ln\frac{T_b}{T_s}.$$

For pure water $T_b = 273$ K.

**Europa.** At the equator $T_s = 110$ K, so $q\,d = 567 \ln(273/110) = 515$ W/m and $d = 515/q$. Ice 10 km thick
means $q = 515/10{,}000 = 0.052$ W/m²; ice 30 km thick means 0.017 W/m² (Figure B). Over Europa's whole surface,
$4\pi R^2 = 3.06 \times 10^{13}$ m², that is 0.5 to 1.6 TW. The Earth loses about 47 TW, or 0.092 W/m² (chapter 6,
[section 6.8](06-The-Earth-as-a-Planet.md#working)); with that much heat Europa's ice would be only 5.6 km thick.

**Thicker at the poles.** At Europa's poles $T_s = 50$ K, and $567 \ln(273/50) = 962$ W/m. For the same heat flow the
ice there should be $962/515 = 1.9$ times as thick as at the equator.

**Enceladus.** Take its south pole: $T_s = 75$ K and $q = 0.2$ W/m². Then $d = 567 \ln(273/75)/0.2 = 3{,}700$ m. If
all that heat leaked up through solid ice, the ice would be under 4 km thick, far thinner than the 30 to 40 km
estimated for the region. So most of the heat near the tiger stripes probably does not seep through a solid lid. It
comes out through the cracks themselves, carried by the warm vapour of the geysers.

**Mars.** Under the south polar cap of Mars, take the surface at about 150 K, the temperature of carbon dioxide frost
at Mars's air pressure, and the heat flow at 0.049 W/m², chapter 6's estimate. After 1.5 km of ice, the depth of the
radar echo,

$$T_b = T_s\, e^{q d/567} = 150\, e^{0.049 \times 1{,}500/567} = 150 \times 1.14 = 171 \text{ K},$$

about −100 °C. Brine at −68 °C, 205 K, would need $q = 567 \ln(205/150)/1{,}500 = 0.12$ W/m², more than the Earth's
average and well over twice Mars's estimate. Fresh water would need 0.23 W/m². With Mars's heat flow, pure water
could only stay liquid about 7 km down, deeper than the ice cap goes.

**What we left out.** Thick ice does not only conduct: its warm lower part can slowly churn, carrying heat up faster,
which is why the 10 to 30 km estimate for Europa includes a layer of "warm ice". Salts and ammonia lower the melting
point. Dust in Mars's ice, and changes in its climate over the last few million years, change the answer a little. But
the simple sum shows the main point: thick ice needs little heat beneath it, and thin ice needs a lot.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Ganymede and Titan are wider than Mercury. Why do they have less mass?

??? note "Answer"
    Mercury is mostly iron and rock. Ganymede and Titan are a mix of rock and ice, which is much less dense, so the
    same size holds much less mass. Titan has only 40% of Mercury's mass.

**2.** (beginner) Why is Io so volcanic when it is only the size of our Moon?

??? note "Answer"
    Tides. Europa and Ganymede keep Io's orbit slightly stretched, so its distance from Jupiter changes on every orbit
    and Jupiter's tide squeezes and relaxes it, heating its inside.

**3.** (beginner) Give two pieces of evidence for an ocean under Europa's ice.

??? note "Answer"
    Any two of: a magnetic field made by Jupiter's field sweeping through a conducting salty layer; a very young,
    smooth surface with chaos terrain where the ice seems to have broken and refrozen; possible plumes of water
    vapour; carbon dioxide in Tara Regio that seems to have come up from below.

**4.** (intermediate) How is Enceladus connected to one of Saturn's rings?

??? note "Answer"
    The geysers at its south pole throw out about 200 kg of water vapour and ice every second. What does not fall back
    as snow goes into orbit round Saturn and makes the E ring.

**5.** (intermediate) Why will flying be easier on Titan than on the Earth or on Mars?

??? note "Answer"
    Titan's air is about 4 times as dense as the Earth's and its gravity only 14% of ours, so rotors get a lot of grip
    and need to hold up little weight: flying takes about 40 times less power than on the Earth. Mars's air is very
    thin, so Ingenuity needed big, fast blades.

**6.** (intermediate) What suggests that Triton was captured rather than formed round Neptune?

??? note "Answer"
    It goes round Neptune backwards, against the planet's spin. A moon that formed with Neptune would go round the
    same way as it spins. So Triton was probably a Kuiper belt object that Neptune caught.

**7.** (advanced) Enceladus has a mass of $1.08 \times 10^{20}$ kg and loses 200 kg/s. How long would it take to lose
1% of its mass at that rate?

??? note "Answer"
    1% is $1.08 \times 10^{18}$ kg. Dividing by 200 kg/s gives $5.4 \times 10^{15}$ s, and dividing by
    $3.156 \times 10^7$ s in a year gives about 170 million years.

**8.** (expert) Suppose 0.03 W/m² flows up through Europa's ice everywhere. How thick would the ice be at the equator
and at the poles, if it only conducts?

??? note "Answer"
    At the equator $d = 567 \ln(273/110)/0.03 = 515/0.03 = 17{,}000$ m, about 17 km. At the poles
    $d = 567 \ln(273/50)/0.03 = 962/0.03 = 32{,}000$ m, about 32 km. The ice would be almost twice as thick at the
    poles.

## Summary

- Seven moons are wider than 2,500 km; Ganymede and Titan are wider than Mercury, but made of rock and ice.
- Io has more than 400 active volcanoes, kept going by tides from Jupiter, which Europa and Ganymede keep pumping.
- Europa, Ganymede and Enceladus, and perhaps Callisto and Titan, have oceans under their ice, found from magnetic
  fields, aurorae, wobbles and geysers.
- Enceladus throws out 200 kg of its ocean every second and has all the main chemical ingredients of life.
- Titan has air thicker than ours and a methane weather cycle with rain, rivers and seas; Triton is captured, very
  cold, and still has plumes.
- The moons were found from Galileo's in 1610 to Miranda in 1948, with a possible sighting of Ganymede in China in
  365 BC.
- JUICE and Europa Clipper are on their way to Ganymede and Europa, and Dragonfly should reach Titan in 2034.
- Thick ice needs little heat below it: Europa's 10 to 30 km means 0.017 to 0.052 W/m², while a lake under 1.5 km of
  Mars's polar ice would need more heat than Mars is thought to give.
