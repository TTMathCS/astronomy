# 8. Mars among the planets

*Every chapter so far has ended by comparing a world with Mars. This one turns the question round and looks at Mars
itself: a planet with two very different halves, the biggest volcano and the longest canyon in the solar system,
and a history that was busy and wet for its first billion years and cold and dry ever since. Then we go round the
family one planet at a time and see what Mars shares with each.*

## 8.1 Mars at a glance { #glance }

Mars is the fourth planet from the Sun, at an average of **1.52 AU** (228 million km). Its orbit is quite stretched,
so its distance from the Sun runs from 206.7 to 249.3 million km. It is **6,792 km** wide, a little over half the
Earth's width, with about a tenth of the Earth's mass, and its surface gravity is **3.73 m/s²**, 38% of ours.

A day on Mars, a **sol**, is **24 hours 39 minutes 35 seconds**, and a year is **687 Earth days**, or about 669
sols. The axis is tilted by **25.19°**, close to the Earth's 23.44°, so Mars has seasons, on average nearly twice as
long as ours. The air is 95% carbon dioxide and very thin, about 6 thousandths of the Earth's pressure at the surface,
and the average temperature is about **−59 °C**. Mars has two small moons, Phobos and Deimos (chapter 7,
[section 7.7](07-The-Moon.md#mars)).

Other books in this series cover the rest. Book 1 shows Mars in our sky
([chapter 14](../night-sky/14-Mars-Backward-Loop-and-Close-Approaches.md#loop)), the sky seen from Mars
([chapter 21](../night-sky/21-The-Night-Sky-from-Mars.md#sunset)) and how time is kept there
([section 9.7](../night-sky/09-Telling-Time-by-the-Sky.md#mars)). Book 3 covers how to get there
([chapter 15](../orbits/15-The-Road-to-Mars.md#aim)), and book 11 the history of its exploration
([chapter 21](../history/21-The-Space-Age-at-Mars.md#first)).

## 8.2 Two halves { #halves }

The first thing a map of Mars shows is that its two halves are different. The **southern highlands** are old and
battered, crowded with craters. The **northern lowlands** are smooth, with far fewer craters, and lie **1 to 3 km
lower**. This split is called the **Martian dichotomy**. Even the crust is different: about 58 km thick in the
south and only 32 km in the north, against an average of 45 km.

Nobody knows for sure how it happened. One idea is that it is the scar of a single giant impact, a basin named the
**Borealis Basin**. If so, it would be the largest impact crater known in the solar system, and the same crash may
have thrown up the rubble that made Phobos and Deimos (book 3,
[section 19.2](../orbits/19-Orbits-at-Mars.md#origin)). The problem is that the lowlands are not quite round, so
other processes would be needed as well. Another family of ideas has slow movements deep in the mantle thinning the
crust on one side. Whichever is right, it probably happened very early, in the Pre-Noachian (section 8.4).

The southern highlands keep a record of that early time. Their crust is the part of Mars that is magnetised in
stripes, from the days when Mars had a magnetic field (chapter 6,
[section 6.7](06-The-Earth-as-a-Planet.md#mars)).

## 8.3 Volcanoes, a canyon and a basin { #features }

**Tharsis.** Near the equator in the western half of Mars is a huge volcanic plateau, **Tharsis**, covering 10 to
30 million km², up to a quarter of the planet. On it stand three giant volcanoes in a row, Arsia Mons, Pavonis Mons
and Ascraeus Mons, the **Tharsis Montes**. Most scientists think Tharsis sits over a hot spot, like the ones under
volcanic island chains on the Earth. Just off its edge is **Olympus Mons**, about 22 km above its surroundings and
600 km wide, the largest volcano in the solar system (chapter 6, [section 6.7](06-The-Earth-as-a-Planet.md#mars)).

**Valles Marineris.** East of Tharsis, along the equator, runs a system of canyons more than **4,000 km** long, up to
200 km wide and **7 km deep**, the largest canyon in the solar system. It is named after Mariner 9, the orbiter that
discovered it in 1971 and 1972. The usual view is that it began as cracks, **rift faults**, as the crust stretched
round the swelling Tharsis, and was then widened by erosion.

**Hellas.** In the southern highlands is the **Hellas basin**, an impact basin about **2,300 km** across, a third of
Mars's width. Its floor, about 7,152 m deep, holds the lowest point on Mars. Hellas formed
between 4.1 and 3.8 billion years ago, and it is a well-known starting place for Mars's global dust storms.

So Mars, half the Earth's width, has a greater **range of heights** than any other rocky world: about 30 km from the
floor of Hellas to the top of Olympus Mons, against 20 km on the Earth (Figure A).

## 8.4 Three ages, two timescales { #ages }

Very few Martian rocks have ever been dated, so the planet's history is pieced together mostly by **counting
craters**. Crowded ground is old, and the counts are turned into years with the clock worked out on the Moon
(chapter 7, [section 7.3](07-The-Moon.md#craters)), adjusted for Mars. Meteorites from Mars can be dated in a
laboratory, and so far they agree with the crater ages, but nobody knows exactly where on Mars each one came from.

The periods are named after places on Mars (Figure B):

- **Pre-Noachian**, from Mars's formation about 4.5 billion years ago until the Hellas basin formed, between 4.1 and
  3.8 billion years ago. Little of it survives. The dichotomy probably dates from this time.
- **Noachian**, after Noachis Terra, from 4.1 to about 3.7 billion years ago. Its ground is scarred with big craters.
  Tharsis probably grew then, and **flowing water** carved networks of river valleys. There may have been large
  lakes, even seas.
- **Hesperian**, after Hesperia Planum, from 3.7 to about 3.0 billion years ago. Lava spread over wide plains,
  Olympus Mons probably began to grow, and sudden **floods** cut huge outflow channels.
- **Amazonian**, after Amazonis Planitia, from about 3.0 billion years ago to today. Craters are few, the land is cold
  and dry, and only small amounts of water have flowed. The start of this period is very uncertain: it could be
  anywhere from 3.0 to 1.5 billion years ago.

In 2006 a team using the OMEGA instrument on Europe's **Mars Express** orbiter suggested another timescale, based on
the minerals that water and air made from the rock:

- **Phyllocian**, until about 4.0 billion years ago, when **clay** minerals formed in water that was probably not
  acidic. These clay-rich rocks are among the best places to look for signs of past life.
- **Theiikian**, to about 3.5 billion years ago, when volcanoes poured out sulphur dioxide, the water turned acidic
  and **sulphate** minerals such as gypsum formed.
- **Siderikan**, from about 3.5 billion years ago to now, as volcanoes and water faded and the iron-rich rock slowly
  **rusted**. These iron oxides are what make Mars red.

## 8.5 Water and dust, in brief { #water }

Water shaped Mars in its first billion years, and it is still there. Most of it is now **ice**, in the two polar caps
and under the ground, and the ice moves from place to place as Mars's tilt changes (book 3,
[section 20.5](../orbits/20-Mars-Changing-Orbit-and-Tilt.md#iceages)). Chapter 20 looks at Mars's water and the
search for life in detail.

Mars's air changes with the seasons. In each hemisphere's winter so much carbon dioxide freezes onto the polar cap
that the pressure swings from about 4.0 to 8.7 thousandths of a bar during the year. Winds lift fine **dust**, and
now and then a storm grows until it wraps the whole planet. One such storm, in June 2018, cut off the sunlight to
the solar-powered rover Opportunity, which never woke again (book 11,
[section 21.4](../history/21-The-Space-Age-at-Mars.md#rovers)). Chapter 19 compares Mars's weather with other worlds'.

## 8.6 Mars and each planet in turn { #family }

- **Mercury.** The two have almost exactly the **same surface gravity**, 3.70 and 3.73 m/s², though Mercury is only
  72% as wide: it is much denser, mostly iron (chapter 4). Both are single-plate worlds with old, cratered ground,
  and neither has much air. Mercury has kept a weak global magnetic field; Mars has not.
- **Venus.** The same kind of air, nearly all carbon dioxide, but Venus has about 6,000 times as much over each
  square metre. One planet held on to a thick atmosphere and roasted; the other lost most of its air and froze
  (chapter 5).
- **The Earth.** The most alike in day and tilt: the sol is only 2.7% longer than our day, and the tilts differ by
  less than 2°. But the Earth kept its plates, its ocean, its magnetic field and its warmth, largely because it is
  bigger (chapter 6).
- **The Moon.** The southern highlands of Mars look rather like the Moon's highlands, and Mars's history is dated
  with the Moon's crater clock. Both have dark lava plains (chapter 7).
- **Jupiter.** Jupiter may be the reason Mars is so small. In the Grand Tack idea, Jupiter swung inwards early on
  and cleared away much of the material Mars would have grown from (chapter 2,
  [section 2.7](02-How-the-Solar-System-Formed.md#mars)). Jupiter's pull also stirs the asteroid belt just beyond
  Mars, which keeps supplying the impacts that crater it.
- **Saturn and Neptune.** Nothing like Mars inside, but their tilts, 26.7° and 28.3°, are close to Mars's 25.2°, so
  they also have seasons, though each lasts years: Saturn's year is 29 of ours and Neptune's 164.
- **Uranus.** The opposite case: it is tipped over at 97.8°, so its seasons are extreme in a way Mars's are not.

??? note "Sources"
    - Gravities, widths, tilts, years, the global fields of Mercury and Mars: NASA,
      [Planetary Fact Sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/). The percentages and years are our
      arithmetic.
    - Mercury's iron, Venus's 6,000 times, the Earth's size, and the Moon's clock: chapters 4 to 7.
    - The Grand Tack: chapter 2. Jupiter stirring the asteroid belt, Mercury as a single-plate world, and the look of
      the southern highlands: standard.

## 8.8 Working it out { #working }

**The sol and the year.** The year is 686.980 days of 24 hours and the sol is 24.6597 hours, so a year is

$$\frac{686.980 \times 24}{24.6597} = 668.6 \text{ sols},$$

and the sol is $24.6597/24 = 1.027$ Earth days, 2.7% longer.

**How big is Valles Marineris?** Mars's circumference is $2\pi \times 3{,}389.5 = 21{,}300$ km. The canyon, more than
4,000 km long, stretches round at least $4{,}000/21{,}300 = 19\%$ of it, about a fifth.

**How much of Mars is Tharsis?** Mars's surface is $4\pi \times 3{,}389.5^2 = 144$ million km². Tharsis's 10 to 30
million km² is 7% to 21% of it.

**From Hellas to Olympus Mons.** If the 7.152 km depth of Hellas and the 21.287 km height of Olympus Mons are both
measured from the same reference level, together they make 28.4 km, close to NASA's round 30 km.

**The same gravity.** Mercury and Mars have $3.70/3.73 = 0.99$ of each other's gravity. Since $g = GM/R^2 = \tfrac43
\pi G \rho R$, the same $g$ needs the same $\rho R$: Mercury's width is 0.72 of Mars's, so it must be about $1/0.72 =
1.4$ times as dense. The real ratio is $5{,}429/3{,}934 = 1.38$.

      Planetary Fact Sheet (above).
    - 4,000 km, 10 to 30 million km², 7,152 m and 21.287 km: Wikipedia, Valles Marineris, Tharsis and Hellas
      Planitia (above), and [Olympus Mons](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Olympus_Mons) (weaker).
    - $g = GM/R^2$: standard. Everything else: our arithmetic.

## 8.9 How tall can a mountain be? { #derive }

A mountain presses down on the rock at its base. If the mountain has height $h$ and is made of rock of density
$\rho$, the pressure at the bottom is about $\rho g h$, the same rule as for the pressure under water. Rock can only
take so much. If the pressure goes above the rock's strength $\sigma$, the base slowly squeezes out sideways and the
mountain sinks. So the tallest possible mountain is about

$$h_{\max} \approx \frac{\sigma}{\rho g}.$$

We can find $\sigma$ from the Earth. Mauna Kea in Hawaii rises about 10 km from its base on the sea floor (9,966 m to
the deepest part of the trough round it), and it is made of basalt, about 3,000 kg/m³:

$$\sigma \approx 3{,}000 \times 9.82 \times 9{,}966 = 2.9 \times 10^8 \text{ Pa},$$

about 2,900 times the air pressure at sea level. Since $\sigma$ and $\rho$ are about the same for rocky worlds,
$h_{\max}$ just goes as $1/g$:

$$h_{\max} = 9{,}966 \text{ m} \times \frac{9.82}{g}.$$

| | Earth | Venus | Mars | Mercury | Moon |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $g$ (m/s²) | 9.82 | 8.87 | 3.73 | 3.70 | 1.62 |
| $h_{\max}$ | 10 km | 11 km | 26 km | 26 km | 60 km |
| Tallest real mountain | Mauna Kea, 10 km from its base | Maxwell Montes, 11 km | Olympus Mons, about 22 km | — | — |

For the Earth, Venus and Mars, the tallest mountains are close to the limit. Maxwell Montes on Venus stands about
11 km above the average level, and Olympus Mons, at about 22 km above its surroundings, is not far below Mars's 26
km. So Mars's mountains are tall mostly because its **gravity is weak**, together with a hot spot that kept feeding
the same place for a very long time (chapter 6).

Mercury and the Moon show the other side. Their limits are higher still, but their tallest peaks are nowhere near
them, because nothing built big mountains there: no long-lived hot spots and no moving plates. Weak gravity lets
mountains stand tall, but something still has to build them.

    - $h_{\max} \approx \sigma/(\rho g)$, the pressure $\rho g h$, and basalt's density of about 3,000 kg/m³:
      standard. That mountains at the limit sink and spread slowly: standard.
    - Mauna Kea's 9,966 m from base to peak, the low end of the range given: Wikipedia,
      [Mauna Kea](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mauna_Kea) (weaker).
    - Maxwell Montes, 11 km high: Wikipedia, [Maxwell Montes](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Maxwell_Montes)
      (weaker). Olympus Mons, nearly 22 km above its surroundings: Wikipedia, Olympus Mons (above).
    - The gravities: NASA fact sheets (above). Everything else: our arithmetic.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) How long is a day on Mars, and how long is its year?

??? note "Answer"
    A sol is 24 hours 39 minutes 35 seconds; a year is 687 Earth days, about 669 sols.

**2.** (beginner) What is the Martian dichotomy?

??? note "Answer"
    The difference between Mars's two halves: old, cratered southern highlands, and smooth northern lowlands 1 to 3
    km lower, with thinner crust.

**3.** (beginner) What and where is Valles Marineris?

??? note "Answer"
    The largest canyon in the solar system, more than 4,000 km long and up to 7 km deep, running along Mars's
    equator east of Tharsis.

**4.** (intermediate) How do scientists put dates on the Martian periods, when almost no Martian rock has been
dated?

??? note "Answer"
    By counting craters, since older ground has more, and turning the counts into years with the clock calibrated
    on the Moon with dated Moon rock, adjusted for Mars. Meteorites from Mars give some laboratory ages that agree.

**5.** (intermediate) What does the Siderikan era tell you about why Mars is red?

??? note "Answer"
    Since about 3.5 billion years ago, with little volcanism or water, the iron-rich rock has slowly oxidised. The
    iron oxides, a kind of rust, give Mars its colour.

**6.** (intermediate) Which planet has almost the same surface gravity as Mars, and why, though it is smaller?

??? note "Answer"
    Mercury, 3.70 against 3.73 m/s². It is only 72% as wide, but much denser because it is mostly iron.

**7.** (advanced) Valles Marineris is 4,000 km long and Mars's radius is 3,389.5 km. What fraction of the way round
Mars does it reach?

??? note "Answer"
    $4{,}000/(2\pi \times 3{,}389.5) = 4{,}000/21{,}300 = 0.19$, about a fifth.

**8.** (expert) Using $h_{\max} = 9{,}966 \text{ m} \times 9.82/g$, what is the tallest mountain possible on Ceres,
where $g = 0.28$ m/s²? Why might real mountains there be lower?

??? note "Answer"
    $9{,}966 \times 9.82/0.28 = 350{,}000$ m, 350 km, which is more than a third of Ceres's width, so the simple
    rule breaks down. Ceres also has a lot of ice, which is weaker than rock, and nothing there builds mountains on
    that scale.

## Summary

- Mars is 6,792 km wide, with 38% of the Earth's gravity, a sol of 24 h 39 min 35 s and a year of 687 days.
- Its southern half is old and cratered; its northern half is smooth and 1 to 3 km lower.
- Tharsis carries giant volcanoes; Valles Marineris is the largest canyon and Hellas the deepest basin.
- Its periods, the Noachian, Hesperian and Amazonian, are dated by crater counts; most of its wet history came early.
- A mineral timescale runs from clays to sulphates to the iron oxides that make it red.
- It shares gravity with Mercury, air with Venus, day and tilt with the Earth, and a crater clock with the Moon.
- Weak gravity lets its mountains stand tall; the limit is about 26 km.
