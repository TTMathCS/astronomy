# 14. Uranus and Neptune

*Beyond Saturn lie two planets that nobody in the ancient world knew about. Uranus was found by chance in 1781 and
Neptune by arithmetic in 1846. They are twins of a kind: each about four times as wide as the Earth, both pale blue,
both made mostly of water, ammonia and methane rather than hydrogen. But Uranus spins on its side and gives out
almost no heat of its own, while Neptune, much farther from the Sun, has the fastest winds of any planet. Only one
spacecraft has ever visited them. This chapter covers what they are like, their weather, Uranus's strange seasons,
how they were found, what is inside them, the visit of Voyager 2 and what may come next, how they compare with Mars,
and why a planet tipped far enough gets more sunshine at its poles than at its equator.*

## 14.1 The ice giants { #basics }

**Two pale blue twins.** Uranus and Neptune are about **4 times** as wide as the Earth and less than half as wide as
Saturn. Uranus is a little bigger, 25,559 km in radius at the equator against Neptune's 24,764 km, but Neptune is
heavier, **17.2 Earths** against **14.5**, so it is denser: **1,638 kg/m³** against **1,270**.

**Made of ices.** Only about a fifth of their mass is hydrogen and helium. Most of the rest is what planet scientists
call **ices**: water, ammonia and methane. Deep inside they are not frozen but a hot, dense fluid. That is why
Uranus and Neptune are called **ice giants**, a different kind from the gas giants Jupiter and Saturn (chapter 3,
[section 3.6](03-Rocky-Worlds-and-Giant-Worlds.md#icegiants)).

**Why blue.** Their air is mostly hydrogen and helium with 1.5 to 2.3% methane. Methane soaks up red light, so the
sunlight that comes back out is blue-green.

**Days.** Uranus turns in **17 hours 14 minutes 52 seconds**, timed from the turning of its aurorae; Neptune in
about **16 hours 7 minutes**. Both turn faster than the Earth, and both bulge a little at the equator, Uranus by
2.3% and Neptune by 1.7%.

**In the sky.** Uranus, around magnitude **5.7**, is just visible to the naked eye under a dark sky and easy in
binoculars, if you know where to look. Neptune, around magnitude **7.8**, needs binoculars or a telescope, which show
it as a tiny blue disc (book 1, [section 13.6](../night-sky/13-Finding-the-Planets.md#faint)).

## 14.2 Weather in the cold { #weather }

**Bland Uranus.** When Voyager 2 flew past in 1986, Uranus looked almost featureless: a smooth blue-green ball.
Since then, telescopes have seen clouds, bands and a bright cap come and go as the seasons turn. Winds reach about
**900 km/h**, 250 m/s. At its coldest layer, the tropopause, the temperature falls to **49 K** (−224 °C), the lowest of
any planet. Uranus is so cold because it gives out barely more heat than it gets from the Sun, about **1.1 times** as
much. Nobody knows why its inner heat is so low. One idea is that the giant impact that tipped it over (section 14.3)
let most of its heat escape; another is that a layer inside stops the heat getting out.

**Stormy Neptune.** Neptune is over 50% farther from the Sun and gets only about **40%** of Uranus's sunlight, yet its
weather is far livelier. It gives out about **2.6 times** as much energy as it receives, and that inner heat drives
the strongest steady winds in the solar system, up to **2,100 km/h**, 580 m/s. When Voyager 2 passed in 1989 it saw
the **Great Dark Spot**, a storm 13,000 by 6,600 km, about the size of the Earth, with bright white clouds like
cirrus beside it. Unlike Jupiter's Great Red Spot, it drifted north and south. Dark spots form and fade every few years;
from Voyager and Hubble pictures, Neptune seems to have one somewhat more than half the time, and a new one was found
in 2018.

**Not so different in colour.** The famous Voyager pictures made Neptune look deep blue next to a pale Uranus, but
their contrast had been boosted "to better reveal the clouds, bands and winds". When the colours were put on an even
footing, most fully in late 2023, the two turned out much alike: Neptune only slightly bluer, partly because Uranus
has thicker haze.

## 14.3 A planet on its side { #tilt }

**Tipped over.** Uranus's axis is tilted **98°**, so it lies almost flat in the plane of its orbit. It rolls round the
Sun like a ball rather than spinning like a top. Its rings and big moons go round its tipped equator, so the whole
system is on its side (Figure A).

**42 years of day, 42 of night.** Uranus takes 84 years to go round the Sun. Near a solstice one pole points almost
at the Sun, which circles low in its sky without setting, for years on end, while the other pole is in darkness. Each
pole gets about **42 years** of daylight and then 42 years of night. Only near the equinoxes does the Sun shine on the
equator, and then every part of Uranus has day and night as on other planets. When Voyager 2 flew past in January
1986, the south pole was pointed almost straight at the Sun. The last equinox was in December 2007; the north pole
faces the Sun around 2030, and the next equinox comes around 2050 (section 14.8).

**More sun at the poles.** Averaged over its year, Uranus's polar regions get **more** sunshine than its equator,
about one and a half times as much (section 14.9). Yet its equator is still the warmer. Why is not known.

**How it fell over.** Nobody saw it happen. The usual idea is that early in the solar system's history, a body at
least as big as the Earth crashed into the young Uranus and knocked it over. Simulations by Jacob Kegerreis at Durham
University suggest a body larger than the Earth, 3 to 4 billion years ago. A giant impact is also the favourite idea for
how our Moon formed (chapter 7, [section 7.4](07-The-Moon.md#origin)).

**Neptune, by contrast,** is tilted 28.3°, close to the Earth's 23.4° and Mars's 25.2°. So it has seasons much like
ours, but each lasts about 40 years.

## 14.4 Found by telescope and by pen { #history }

**Seen but not noticed.** Uranus is just bright enough to see without a telescope, but it moves so slowly that no
ancient watcher picked it out as a planet. It was seen many times and taken for a star. A star in **Hipparchus**'s
catalogue of 128 BC, later copied into Ptolemy's *Almagest*, may be Uranus: the star does not exist, and Uranus was in
that place in April 128 BC. **John Flamsteed** recorded it at least six times from 1690, as the star 34 Tauri; James
Bradley, Tobias Mayer and Pierre Charles Le Monnier saw it too.

**A musician's discovery.** On **13 March 1781**, from the garden of his house in Bath, England, the musician
**William Herschel** saw it in his home-made telescope, a reflector 6.2 inches across, and noticed that, unlike the
stars, it looked bigger when he magnified it more. He thought it was a comet. The Astronomer Royal, Nevil Maskelyne,
wrote that he did not know what to call it. **Anders Johan Lexell**, a Finnish-Swedish astronomer working in Russia,
was the first to work out its orbit, which was nearly round, like a planet's. **Johann Elert Bode** in Berlin agreed
it was a planet. Herschel wanted to call it the "Georgian star" after King George III; Bode's name, **Uranus**, the
Greek sky god Ouranos, was the one that stuck (history book,
[section 17.1](../history/17-Herschel-and-the-Canals-of-Mars.md#herschel)). It was the first planet found with a
telescope, and it doubled the size of the known solar system.

**Found with a pen.** Soon after Alexis Bouvard published tables of its motion in 1821, Uranus began to stray from
them. In the
1840s **Urbain Le Verrier** in Paris and **John Couch Adams** in Cambridge each worked out where an unseen planet must
be to pull it off course. Le Verrier sent his result to **Johann Gottfried Galle** at the Berlin Observatory. The
student **Heinrich d'Arrest** suggested comparing the sky with a new star chart, and on the evening of
**23 September 1846**, the day the letter came, they found the planet within 1° of the predicted place. François
Arago said Le Verrier had found it "with the point of his pen". A bitter argument over Adams's share followed; the
Royal Society gave Le Verrier its Copley Medal in 1846 and Adams his in 1848 (book 3,
[section 10.3](../orbits/10-Wobbling-Orbits-and-the-Finding-of-Neptune.md#neptune)). Seventeen days later William
Lassell found its big moon **Triton**. Galileo had drawn Neptune on 28 December 1612 and 27 January 1613 next to
Jupiter, taking it for a star, and Lalande in 1795 and John Herschel in 1830 also saw it without knowing.

**Names round the world.** Galle suggested Janus and Challis Oceanus; Le Verrier wanted it named after himself, but
**Neptune**, backed by Friedrich Struve in Saint Petersburg, won. In **Chinese**, **Japanese** and **Korean**, Uranus
is the "heavenly king star", 天王星, and Neptune the "sea king star", 海王星; **Vietnamese** also calls Uranus the
heavenly king. In **Mongolian**
Uranus is Tengeriin Van, "king of the sky", and Neptune Dalain Van, ruler of the sea. In **Thai** one name for Uranus
is Dao Maruettayu, from the Sanskrit word for death. In **Hawaiian** Uranus is Heleʻekela, from "Herschel". In modern
**Greek** Neptune is Poseidonas, and in **Hebrew** it is Rahab, chosen by a vote of the Academy of the Hebrew
Language in 2009.

**Rings found by a star.** On 10 March 1977 James Elliot, Edward Dunham and Jessica Mink, flying in NASA's Kuiper
Airborne Observatory to watch Uranus pass in front of a star, saw the star blink out five times before and after the
planet hid it. Uranus had rings: nine were found that year. Neptune's rings were found the same way in 1984 and 1985,
as broken arcs.

## 14.5 Inside, magnetism, rings and moons { #inside }

**Three layers.** The usual picture of each ice giant has a rocky core of silicate and iron, a thick mantle of hot,
dense water, ammonia and methane, and an outer envelope of hydrogen and helium. In Uranus the ices make up between
9.3 and 13.5 Earth masses, hydrogen and helium only 0.5 to 1.5. Deep in the mantle, methane may break up and its
carbon form crystals of **diamond** that rain down like hail. Deeper still, water may become **superionic**: its
oxygen atoms lock into a crystal while hydrogen moves freely through it. Other models also fit the data, and only a
visit can tell them apart.

**Lopsided magnetic fields.** Voyager 2 found that both planets have magnetic fields tipped far from their spin axes
and set well off their centres. Uranus's is tilted **59°** and shifted a third of the planet's radius towards the
south pole, so at the cloud tops it ranges from 0.1 gauss in the south to 1.1 gauss in the north. Neptune's is tilted
**47°** and shifted by about half its radius. The likely reason is that the fields are made not deep down, as in the
Earth, Jupiter and Saturn, but in a shallow layer, the electrically conducting water–ammonia ocean. Uranus's magnetic
moment is about **50 times** the Earth's (section 14.8). A study in 2024 found that Voyager 2 reached Uranus during an
unusual burst of strong solar wind, which squeezed its magnetic bubble, so what Voyager saw may not be typical.

**Dark rings.** Uranus has **13** known rings, from about 38,000 to 98,000 km from its centre. Most are narrow, only a
few kilometres wide, and they are very dark, reflecting at most 2% of the light: probably ice darkened by
radiation. They are made of lumps 20 cm to 20 m across and are thought to be young, no more than 600 million years
old, perhaps from moons that smashed together. Neptune's rings are faint and dusty and are named after the people in
its story: Galle, Le Verrier, Lassell, Arago and Adams. The outer **Adams ring** has five bright arcs named
**Liberté**, **Égalité** 1 and 2, **Fraternité** and **Courage**, the first four from the motto of the French
Revolution. They have stayed bunched since the 1980s, probably held by the small moon Galatea.

**Moons.** Uranus's moons are named after characters in the plays of William Shakespeare and a poem by Alexander
Pope. Its five main moons are **Miranda, Ariel, Umbriel, Titania** and **Oberon**; the largest, Titania, is less than
half as wide as our Moon. Neptune's **Triton** holds more than 99.5% of the mass going round Neptune. It goes round
backwards, so it was probably a dwarf planet from the Kuiper belt that Neptune captured. Chapter 15 visits them.

## 14.6 One visit { #missions }

**Voyager 2.** Only one spacecraft has been to either planet. NASA's Voyager 2, launched in August 1977, used a rare
lining-up of the outer planets to swing from Jupiter to Saturn to Uranus to Neptune, each pull bending its path
towards the next (book 3, [chapter 16](../orbits/16-Gravity-Assists.md)).

- **Uranus, 24 January 1986.** It passed within 81,500 km of the cloud tops. It found 10 new moons and 2 new rings,
  made the first close study of the five big moons, and measured the tilted magnetic field, whose tail is twisted into
  a corkscrew by the planet's sideways spin.
- **Neptune, 25 August 1989.** It came within 4,400 km of Neptune's atmosphere, then flew past Triton the same day.
  It saw the Great Dark Spot, timed the day from radio signals, and found the field tilted and off-centre like
  Uranus's.

**Waiting for a return.** In 2009 there was talk of sending Cassini on from Saturn to Uranus, but the trip would have
taken about 20 years, and it was crashed into Saturn instead (chapter 13). The American planetary science survey for
2023 to 2032 made a **Uranus Orbiter and Probe** its highest-priority big mission: an orbiter to study the planet and
moons for four and a half years and a probe to dive into the air. It was first aimed at a launch in 2031 with a swing
past Jupiter, arriving in 2044, but a shortage of plutonium for its power supply now makes a launch in the mid to late
2030s more likely. A Neptune orbiter, **Neptune Odyssey**, was studied too but not chosen. China plans for its
**Tianwen-4** Jupiter mission, launching in 2029, to carry a small probe that would fly past Uranus in March 2045, and
has spoken of a later Tianwen-5 to Uranus or Neptune.

**From the Earth.** Meanwhile big telescopes keep watch. The Hubble Space Telescope and adaptive optics on large
telescopes in Hawaii and elsewhere follow the clouds, and the James Webb Space Telescope photographed Uranus's rings in
infrared light in 2023.

**Tilts that move.** Mars today is tilted 25°, almost the same as Neptune. But Mars has no big moon to steady it, and
over millions of years its tilt can wander anywhere from about 0° to 60° (book 3,
[section 20.1](../orbits/20-Mars-Changing-Orbit-and-Tilt.md#tilt)). Above about **54°**, a planet's poles get more
sunshine in a year than its equator (section 14.9), so at its most tipped Mars has been, for a while, a little like
Uranus. At a tilt of 60° its poles would get about 12% more than its equator. Even at 35° the polar ice cannot last
the summers and moves to the high volcanoes near the equator (book 3,
[section 20.5](../orbits/20-Mars-Changing-Orbit-and-Tilt.md#iceages)).

**Doomed moons.** Triton goes round Neptune backwards, against the planet's spin, so the tide it raises slowly drags
it in. Phobos goes round Mars faster than Mars turns, and its tide drags it in too (book 3,
[section 8.7](../orbits/08-Tides.md#mars)). Both will one day come inside the Roche limit and be torn apart: Phobos in
30 to 50 million years, Triton in billions.

**Captured moons?** Triton was almost certainly captured from the Kuiper belt. Mars's moons may also have been
captured, as asteroids, though they may instead have formed from rubble thrown up by a giant impact (book 3,
[section 19.2](../orbits/19-Orbits-at-Mars.md#origin)). Japan's MMX, which left for Phobos in October 2026, should
help settle it (chapter 7, [section 7.7](07-The-Moon.md#mars)).

**The same discoverer.** William Herschel, who found Uranus, also studied Mars closely. He watched its polar caps
shrink and grow, measured its tilt and timed its day (history book,
[section 17.3](../history/17-Herschel-and-the-Canals-of-Mars.md#herschelmars)).

## 14.8 Working it out { #working }

**Densities.** Uranus's mass of $8.681 \times 10^{25}$ kg in a ball of mean radius 25,362 km gives

$$\rho = \frac{8.681 \times 10^{25}}{\tfrac43 \pi \times (2.536 \times 10^7)^3} = 1{,}270 \text{ kg/m}^3,$$

and Neptune's $1.024 \times 10^{26}$ kg in a radius of 24,622 km gives 1,638 kg/m³. Neptune is the densest of the four
giants.

**Years from Kepler.** $19.19^{1.5} = 84.1$ years and $30.07^{1.5} = 164.9$ years, as measured.

**Sunlight.** $1{,}361/19.19^2 = 3.70$ W/m² at Uranus and $1{,}361/30.07^2 = 1.51$ W/m² at Neptune, 0.41 times as much.

**Neptune's first lap.** Neptune was found on 23 September 1846, 1846.73 in years. One Neptune year of 164.79 years
later is 2011.52, in July 2011. Wikipedia gives 12 July 2011 for its return to the longitude where it was found.

**Seasons on Uranus.** A quarter of an 84-year orbit is 21 years, so seasons come round about every 21 years. Working
out the direction of the Sun from Uranus with NASA's orbital elements and the direction of its pole gives the equinox
of 2007.93, which is 7 December 2007, a southern summer solstice in late 1985, when the Sun stood 82° from Uranus's
equator, just before Voyager 2 arrived, the next solstice in 2030 and the next equinox in early 2050. The gaps are not
exactly 21 years because the orbit is a little stretched.

**Magnetic moments.** As in chapter 13, the moment goes as the field at the equator times the radius cubed. Uranus:
$(0.228/0.306) \times (25{,}600/6{,}378)^3 = 0.745 \times 64.7 = 48$ times the Earth's. Neptune:
$(0.142/0.306) \times (24{,}765/6{,}378)^3 = 0.464 \times 58.5 = 27$ times.

**Light time.** At their average distances from the Sun, light takes
$2.867 \times 10^{12}/3.00 \times 10^8 = 9{,}560$ s, or 2.7 hours, to reach Uranus, and 4.2 hours to reach Neptune.

**Voyager's long trip.** From launch in August 1977 to Uranus in January 1986 took 8.4 years, and to Neptune in August
1989, 12.0 years.

**Winds against spin.** Neptune's equator turns at $2\pi \times 24{,}764/(16.11 \times 3{,}600) = 2.68$ km/s. Its
fastest winds, 0.58 km/s, are a fifth of that.

**From Hipparchus to Herschel.** From 128 BC to AD 1781 is 1,908 years (there was no year 0), about 23 trips of Uranus
round the Sun.

## 14.9 Sunshine on a tipped planet { #derive }

Why should Uranus's poles get more sunshine in a year than its equator? And how far does a planet have to be tipped
for that to happen?

**Sunlight on flat ground.** Sunlight of strength $S$, arriving with the Sun at height $h$ above the horizon, spreads
over a patch of flat ground, which gets $S \sin h$. We ignore the air and clouds, and take the orbit as a circle.

**The Sun's path.** As a planet goes round, the Sun's position north or south of the planet's equator, its
**declination** $\delta$, swings back and forth. If $\lambda$ is how far the planet has gone round its orbit since its
northern spring equinox, then

$$\sin \delta = \sin \varepsilon \sin \lambda,$$

where $\varepsilon$ is the tilt. This is the same rule that gives the Sun's height at the Earth's solstices.

**A day at the pole.** At a pole the Sun neither rises nor sets during a day: it circles the sky at a height equal to
$\delta$ (book 3, [section 20.9](../orbits/20-Mars-Changing-Orbit-and-Tilt.md#derive)). So the ground gets
$S \sin \delta$ all day while $\delta > 0$, and nothing while the Sun is below the horizon.

**A day at the equator.** At the equator the Sun rises and sets every day. When it has turned an angle $H$ from noon,
its height obeys $\sin h = \cos \delta \cos H$. It is up for half the turn, from $H = -90°$ to $+90°$, so the average
over a whole day is

$$\frac{1}{2\pi}\int_{-\pi/2}^{\pi/2} S \cos\delta \cos H \, dH = \frac{S \cos \delta}{\pi}.$$

**A year at the pole.** The Sun is up at the north pole for half the year, while $\lambda$ goes from 0 to $\pi$. The
average over the whole year is

$$Q_{\text{pole}} = \frac{1}{2\pi}\int_0^{\pi} S \sin\varepsilon \sin\lambda \, d\lambda
= \frac{S\sin\varepsilon}{\pi}.$$

**A year at the equator.** Here every day counts, so we average $S\cos\delta/\pi$ over the whole orbit:

$$Q_{\text{eq}} = \frac{S}{\pi} \times \text{average of} \sqrt{1 - \sin^2\varepsilon \sin^2\lambda}.$$

For a small tilt the average is close to $1 - \tfrac14 \sin^2\varepsilon$. For a tilt of 90° the square root becomes
$|\cos\lambda|$, whose average is $2/\pi$, so $Q_{\text{eq}} = 2S/\pi^2 = 0.203\,S$.

**Where the poles win.** The pole's share, $\sin\varepsilon/\pi$, grows with the tilt; the equator's shrinks. They are
equal where $\sin\varepsilon$ equals the average of the square root, and working that out numerically gives
$\varepsilon = 53.9°$. Tip a planet more than about **54°** and its poles get more sunshine in a year than its equator
(Figure B).

**The numbers.**

- **The Earth**, $\varepsilon = 23.4°$: $Q_{\text{pole}} = 0.127\,S = 172$ W/m² and $Q_{\text{eq}} = 0.305\,S = 416$
  W/m², 2.4 times as much at the equator. These are the well-known yearly figures for sunshine at the top of the
  Earth's air.
- **Uranus**, $\varepsilon = 82.2°$ (the same as 97.8° for sunshine): $Q_{\text{pole}} = 0.315\,S = 1.16$ W/m² and
  $Q_{\text{eq}} = 0.208\,S = 0.77$ W/m². The poles get 1.5 times as much as the equator.
- **Mars at 60°**, a tilt it may have reached: the poles get $0.276\,S$ and the equator $0.245\,S$, 1.12 times as much
  at the poles.

**And yet.** The arithmetic is simple, but Uranus does not follow it: its equator is warmer than its poles. The heat
must be carried, by winds or from inside, in ways not yet understood, one of the questions a Uranus orbiter would try
to answer.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Why are Uranus and Neptune called ice giants rather than gas giants?

??? note "Answer"
    Most of their mass is "ices", water, ammonia and methane, now a hot, dense fluid deep inside, with only about a
    fifth hydrogen and helium. Jupiter and Saturn are mostly hydrogen and helium.

**2.** (beginner) What is odd about Uranus's tilt, and what does it do to the seasons?

??? note "Answer"
    It is tilted 98°, lying almost on its side. Near the solstices one pole faces the Sun for years on end, so each
    pole has about 42 years of day and then 42 years of night.

**3.** (beginner) How was Neptune found?

??? note "Answer"
    Uranus was drifting from its predicted path. Le Verrier, and separately Adams, worked out where an unseen planet
    must be to pull it. Galle and d'Arrest in Berlin looked there on 23 September 1846 and found Neptune within 1° of
    Le Verrier's place.

**4.** (intermediate) Neptune gets only 40% of Uranus's sunlight, yet its winds are far faster and its weather livelier.
Why might that be?

??? note "Answer"
    Neptune has a strong source of heat inside: it gives out about 2.6 times the energy it gets from the Sun, while
    Uranus gives out only about 1.1 times. That inner heat drives Neptune's storms and winds.

**5.** (intermediate) What is strange about the magnetic fields of Uranus and Neptune, and what might explain it?

??? note "Answer"
    Both are tilted far from the spin axis, 59° and 47°, and set well off the planet's centre. They are probably made
    in a fairly shallow, electrically conducting layer of water and ammonia, not deep in a core.

**6.** (intermediate) Why did people long think Neptune was a much deeper blue than Uranus?

??? note "Answer"
    The Voyager 2 pictures of Neptune had their contrast boosted to show the clouds. When the colours of the two
    planets were compared fairly, they turned out much alike, Neptune only slightly bluer.

**7.** (advanced) Neptune was found on 23 September 1846, and its year is 164.79 Earth years. When did it first get
back to where it was found?

??? note "Answer"
    23 September 1846 is about 1846.73. Adding 164.79 years gives 2011.52, in July 2011. (The date given is 12 July
    2011.)

**8.** (expert) For a planet tipped exactly 90°, find the yearly sunshine at the pole and at the equator as shares of
$S$, and the ratio between them.

??? note "Answer"
    At the pole, $\sin 90°/\pi = 1/\pi = 0.318$. At the equator the average of $|\cos\lambda|$ is $2/\pi$, so
    $Q_{\text{eq}} = (1/\pi)(2/\pi) = 2/\pi^2 = 0.203$. The pole gets $(1/\pi)/(2/\pi^2) = \pi/2 = 1.57$ times as
    much as the equator.

## Summary

- Uranus and Neptune are about four times as wide as the Earth and made mostly of water, ammonia and methane: ice
  giants, not gas giants.
- Uranus is bland, the coldest planet, with little inner heat; Neptune gives out 2.6 times the energy it gets and has
  the fastest winds, up to 2,100 km/h, and dark storms that come and go.
- Uranus is tipped 98°, perhaps by a giant impact, so each pole has 42 years of day and 42 of night.
- Uranus was found by William Herschel in 1781, after being taken for a star since at least 1690; Neptune was found
  in 1846 at the place worked out by Le Verrier, and Adams.
- Their magnetic fields are tipped and off-centre, probably made in a shallow ocean; their rings are dark and narrow,
  and Triton goes round Neptune backwards.
- Only Voyager 2 has visited, in 1986 and 1989; a Uranus orbiter is the top priority for a big new NASA mission, and
  China plans a flyby in 2045.
- Mars's wandering tilt can reach 60°; Triton and Phobos are both spiralling in to be torn apart.
- A planet tipped more than 54° gets more sunshine at its poles over a year than at its equator, as Uranus does.
