# 16. Pluto and the Kuiper belt

*For 76 years Pluto was the ninth planet: a faint dot found in 1930, far out beyond Neptune. Then astronomers found
that it is only one of a huge swarm of icy bodies, the Kuiper belt, and in 2006 it was renamed a dwarf planet. In
2015 a spacecraft flew past it at last, and found a world far livelier than anyone expected, with glaciers of frozen
nitrogen, mountains of water ice, and blue haze in a thin sky. This chapter covers Pluto and its moons, what New
Horizons saw, the Kuiper belt and its other dwarf planets, the argument about what counts as a planet, how it was all
found, how Pluto compares with Mars, and why Pluto's air is so sensitive to a degree or two of warming.*

## 16.1 Pluto and its family { #basics }

**Small and far.** Pluto is **2,377 km** across, smaller than our Moon, with only 0.2% of the Earth's mass. Its average
distance from the Sun is **39.5 AU**, and its orbit is so stretched that it swings from 29.6 AU to 48.8 AU. For 20
years, from 1979 to 1999, it was closer to the Sun than Neptune, but the two can never collide, because Pluto goes
round twice for every three trips of Neptune (book 3, [section 11.3](../orbits/11-Resonances.md#apart)).

**A long, cold day.** Pluto turns once in **6.387 days**, and it is tipped right over, by about 120°, so it spins
backwards like Uranus and has seasons of decades. Sunlight there is **0.87 W/m²**, about 1/1,560 of what reaches the
Earth. The surface is somewhere around −230 °C.

**Rock and ice.** At 1,854 kg/m³, Pluto must be a mix of rock and ice, much like Ganymede, Titan or Triton (chapter
15, [section 15.1](15-Moons-of-the-Giants.md#basics)).

**Five moons.** **Charon** is half as wide as Pluto and has an eighth of its mass. It is so big that the point the two
go round, their **barycentre**, lies 2,126 km from Pluto's centre, outside Pluto itself. Each keeps the same face to
the other, so from one side of Pluto Charon hangs still in the sky, and from the other side it is never seen. Four
small moons, **Styx**, **Nix**, **Kerberos** and **Hydra**, go round both, from 42,000 to 65,000 km out. All five may
come from a collision between Pluto and another body of about the same size, much as our Moon may have come from a
collision with the Earth.

## 16.2 Pluto up close { #pluto }

**A heart of ice.** The first thing everyone noticed in the pictures from New Horizons was a huge bright patch shaped
like a heart, now called **Tombaugh Regio**. Its western half, **Sputnik Planitia**, is a basin about **1,400 by
1,200 km**, probably dug by a giant impact and filled with bright glaciers of frozen **nitrogen**, with some carbon
monoxide.

**Ice that churns.** Most of Sputnik Planitia is broken into polygons about **33 km** across, separated by troughs.
These are **convection cells**: nitrogen ice, softer than water ice, warmed a little from below, rises slowly in the
middle of each cell and sinks at the edges, like a pan of thick soup. New Horizons found no craters at all on the
plain, so its surface must be less than about 10 million years old. Glaciers flow down valleys into the basin from the
highlands to the east. On the western side, winds have piled grains of methane ice into dunes.

**Mountains of water ice.** At Pluto's temperatures, water ice is as hard as rock, and it forms the mountains and the
bedrock. Nitrogen and methane ice are the softer stuff on top. Two large mountains, **Wright Mons** and **Piccard
Mons**, may be made of many smaller ice volcanoes merged together, which would mean Pluto still has some heat inside.

**An ocean?** Pluto may have an ocean of liquid water deep under its ice. In 2016 a computer study of the impact that
made Sputnik Planitia found that the basin is best explained if liquid water welled up from below afterwards, which
would mean an ocean at least 100 km deep.
Sputnik Planitia also lies almost exactly opposite Charon. That would happen by chance less than 5% of the time, and it
suggests that the heavy ice filling the basin made Pluto roll over until the extra mass lined up with Charon.

**Thin air and blue haze.** Pluto has air: **99% nitrogen**, with a little methane and carbon monoxide. At the ground
the pressure is about **1 Pa**, a hundred-thousandth of ours. The air comes from the ices on the ground, which turn
straight into gas where they are warmed. Radiation turns some of it into a **haze** of about 20 thin layers, reaching
more than 200 km up, which New Horizons saw glowing blue. NASA gives the surface temperature as 24 to 38 K;
Wikipedia's article on the air says 40 to 60 K. Either way it is colder than −210 °C.

**Painting its moon.** Charon's north pole has a large dark red cap, **Neverland Regio**, which the New Horizons team
first nicknamed "Mordor". The best explanation is that gas escaping from Pluto drifts across, freezes onto Charon's
cold winter pole, and is turned by sunlight into reddish material.

## 16.3 The Kuiper belt { #belt }

**A belt of ice.** Beyond Neptune lies the **Kuiper belt**, a wide ring of icy bodies from Neptune's orbit at 30 AU
out to about 50 AU. It is like the asteroid belt, but about 20 times as wide and 20 to 200 times as heavy. Thousands
of its members are known, and more than 100,000 are thought to be over 100 km across. Even so, all of them together
add up to only about **2%** of the Earth's mass.

**Shaped by Neptune.** Neptune's pull sorts the belt into groups (Figure A).

- **Plutinos.** At 39.4 AU a body goes round twice while Neptune goes round three times. This **3:2 resonance**
  protects them, as it protects Pluto. About a quarter of all known Kuiper belt objects are here. They are named for
  creatures of the underworld, such as Orcus and Ixion.
- **The classical belt.** Between 42 and 48 AU, between the 3:2 and the 2:1 resonances, bodies can keep fairly round
  orbits for billions of years. Some, the "cold" ones, probably formed where they are. Between 40 and 42 AU, by
  contrast, no orbit is stable for long.
- **The Kuiper cliff.** At the 2:1 resonance, 47.7 AU, the number of bodies suddenly drops. Nobody knows why.
- **The scattered disc.** Farther out, bodies like **Eris** are on long, stretched orbits, flung there long ago by
  Neptune. Eris swings from 38.4 to 97.7 AU.

**Why it is there.** The belt is left over from when the planets formed. Out there the material was too thinly spread
to build a planet, so it stayed as many small bodies. As Neptune moved outwards in the young solar system, its pull
herded many of them into resonances and scattered others (chapter 2,
[section 2.6](02-How-the-Solar-System-Formed.md#migration)). Triton, Neptune's big moon, was probably one of them,
caught by the planet (chapter 15, [section 15.4](15-Moons-of-the-Giants.md#titan)).

**Other dwarf planets.** **Eris** is a little smaller than Pluto but **27% heavier**, and it is very bright, probably
because fresh frost keeps coating it. It has a moon, **Dysnomia**, whose orbit gave Eris's mass. Other big members of
the belt include **Makemake**, **Haumea** and **Quaoar**, and chapter 1 lists the bodies most astronomers count as
dwarf planets (chapter 1, [section 1.6](01-A-Tour-of-the-Solar-System.md#planet)).

**Arrokoth.** Not every Kuiper belt object is big. **Arrokoth** is a "snowman" of two flattened lobes, 20 and 15 km
across, joined by a narrow neck. The two probably formed as a pair and then merged gently, and its red surface has few
craters. It has probably changed little since the solar system was born, which makes it one of
the best samples we have of the building blocks of planets.

## 16.4 Planet or not? { #planet }

**Too many Plutos.** For decades Pluto was the odd one out among the planets: tiny, icy and on a tilted, stretched
orbit. After 1992, as more and more Kuiper belt objects turned up, it began to look like the biggest known member of a
crowd rather than a planet. Then in 2005 a team at Palomar Observatory announced **Eris**, which is more massive than
Pluto. If Pluto was a planet, Eris surely was too, and perhaps dozens more would follow.

**The Prague vote.** The International Astronomical Union settled it at its meeting in Prague on **24 August 2006**.
A planet must go round the Sun, be massive enough to be pulled round by its own gravity, and have **cleared the
neighbourhood** around its orbit. Pluto passes the first two tests but not the third, because it shares its space with
countless Kuiper belt objects, so it became a **dwarf planet** (chapter 1,
[section 1.6](01-A-Tour-of-the-Solar-System.md#planet)).

**The argument goes on.** Only 424 astronomers were still in the hall for the vote, fewer than 5% of the profession,
because it came on the last day of a ten-day meeting. Alan Stern, the leader of New Horizons, objected that the Earth,
Mars, Jupiter and Neptune have not fully cleared their paths either: the Earth shares its part of space with
thousands of near-Earth asteroids, and Jupiter with many Trojans. "If Neptune had cleared its zone, Pluto wouldn't be
there," Stern said. Others answer that the big planets control everything near them, as Neptune controls Pluto, and
that each is far heavier than everything else near its orbit put together (section 16.8).

## 16.5 Finding Pluto and the belt { #history }

- **Planet X.** In the early 1900s Percival Lowell's observatory in Flagstaff, Arizona, hunted for a ninth planet
  thought to be pulling on Uranus. In 1909 Lowell and William H. Pickering suggested places to look (book 3,
  [section 10.5](../orbits/10-Wobbling-Orbits-and-the-Finding-of-Neptune.md#failures)).
- **Flagstaff, 1930.** Clyde Tombaugh, a young self-taught astronomer from a Kansas farm, photographed the sky in pairs
  of plates taken days apart and flicked between them with a **blink comparator**, looking for anything that moved.
  On **18 February 1930** a faint dot did (history book,
  [section 17.8](../history/17-Herschel-and-the-Canals-of-Mars.md#illusion)).
- **Oxford, 1930.** The name came from **Venetia Burney**, an eleven-year-old schoolgirl in Oxford interested in
  myths. When Venetia's grandfather, Falconer Madan, read out the news over breakfast, Venetia suggested Pluto, god of
  the underworld. Madan passed it to the astronomer Herbert Hall Turner, who cabled it to Lowell Observatory. Out of
  about 150 suggestions, Pluto was chosen, and it was published on 1 May 1930.
- **Names round the world.** In Japan, **Houei Nojiri** suggested *Meiōsei*, "star of the king of the underworld",
  which Chinese and Korean took up too. Hindi and Vietnamese use the name of **Yama**, the god of death, and Māori uses
  **Whiro**, the god of the underworld.
- **Charon, 1978.** On 22 June 1978 **James Christy**, of the United States Naval Observatory, noticed that Pluto's
  image on photographic plates had a bump that came and went. It was a moon, Charon, and it finally let Pluto be
  weighed: far too light to pull on Uranus (book 3, [section 6.4](../orbits/06-Weighing.md#nomoon)).
- **Air, 1985 and 1988.** As Pluto passes in front of a star, the star fades gradually if Pluto has air. **Noah Brosch
  and Haim Mendelson** at the Wise Observatory in Israel saw this on 19 August 1985, though their data were poor. On
  9 June 1988 observers at eight sites, the best on the Kuiper Airborne Observatory, proved Pluto had an atmosphere.
- **Guessing at a belt.** Soon after 1930 Frederick C. Leonard wondered whether Pluto was the first of many such
  bodies. In 1943 **Kenneth Edgeworth** suggested that beyond Neptune the material was too spread out to make planets
  and stayed as small bodies; in 1951 **Gerard Kuiper** suggested a version of the idea. In 1980 **Julio Ángel
  Fernández** predicted a belt of comets there. In 1988 the Canadian team of Martin Duncan, Tom Quinn and Scott
  Tremaine found by computer that the Oort cloud could not supply all the short-period comets, and Tremaine named the
  region the Kuiper belt.
- **Mauna Kea, 1992.** **David Jewitt** and **Jane Luu** searched for five years, at Kitt Peak in Arizona, Cerro Tololo
  in Chile and finally the University of Hawaii's 2.24 m telescope on Mauna Kea. On **30 August 1992** they announced
  1992 QB1, now called Albion, the first Kuiper belt object found after Pluto and Charon.
- **Palomar, 2005.** Mike Brown, Chad Trujillo and David Rabinowitz found **Eris** on 5 January 2005 in pictures taken
  in October 2003.

## 16.6 New Horizons { #missions }

**The fastest launch.** NASA's **New Horizons**, led by Alan Stern, was launched from Cape Canaveral on **19 January
2006** at **16.26 km/s** relative to the Sun, faster than anything launched before it. It passed the Moon's orbit in
nine hours. On 28 February 2007 it swung past Jupiter, which flung it on faster (book 3,
[chapter 16](../orbits/16-Gravity-Assists.md)). Then it slept through most of the long cruise, waking now and then for
checks.

**Pluto at last.** On **14 July 2015** New Horizons flew **12,500 km** above Pluto at 13.78 km/s, when Pluto was 34 AU
from the Sun. It could not stop, so it had to take all its pictures in one pass. Radio signals took about 4.7 hours to
reach the Earth, and at 2 kilobits per second it took more than 15 months to send all the data home.

**Things it carried.** About 30 g of Clyde Tombaugh's ashes ride on board. A dust counter built by students is named
after Venetia Burney.

**Arrokoth.** While New Horizons was on its way, a team led by Marc Buie used the Hubble Space Telescope to find a
Kuiper belt object it could reach. They found one on 26 June 2014. On **1 January 2019** New Horizons flew past it,
the most distant object ever visited by a spacecraft. Its name, **Arrokoth**, comes from the Powhatan people of the
Tidewater region of Virginia and Maryland, where the Hubble Space Telescope and the laboratory that runs New Horizons
are operated from, and it was given with the permission of elders of the Pamunkey tribe.

**Still going.** In April 2026 New Horizons was 64.5 AU from the Sun. NASA plans to keep it working until it leaves
the Kuiper belt, in 2028 or 2029. No other spacecraft is on its way to Pluto.

**Air that freezes.** Mars and Pluto both have air that freezes onto the ground. On Mars, so much carbon dioxide
freezes onto each winter polar cap that the pressure swings from about 4.0 to 8.7 mb during the year (chapter 8,
[section 8.5](08-Mars-among-the-Planets.md#water)). On Pluto it is nitrogen, and the swings are far bigger: the
pressure tripled between 1988 and 2015, even though Pluto was moving away from the Sun. The likely reason is that
Pluto's north pole came into sunlight in 1987, after 124 years of night, and its nitrogen frost started turning to gas
(section 16.9).

**Glaciers.** Both have glaciers. Mars's are water ice, mostly buried under dust and rock, and its polar caps are
water ice with a skin of carbon dioxide frost. Pluto's are nitrogen ice, soft enough to flow and churn, renewing
the surface within 10 million years.

**Great basins.** Sputnik Planitia, 1,400 km across, is a basin dug by an impact, like **Hellas** on Mars, about
2,300 km across (chapter 8, [section 8.3](08-Mars-among-the-Planets.md#features)). Hellas is a dry hollow; Sputnik
Planitia filled up with ice.

**Big moon, small moons.** Charon is half Pluto's width. Mars's two moons are tiny lumps a few tens of kilometres long
(chapter 7, [section 7.7](07-The-Moon.md#mars)). Yet both families may come from collisions: Pluto's moons from a
smash with a body of Pluto's size, and Mars's perhaps from rubble thrown up by a giant impact (book 3,
[section 19.2](../orbits/19-Orbits-at-Mars.md#origin)).

**Colour.** Pluto is coloured from charcoal black to dark orange and white. Its colour is closer to Io's than to Mars's,
with less red.

## 16.8 Working it out { #working }

**Density and gravity.** $\rho = 1.303 \times 10^{22}/(\tfrac43 \pi \times (1.188 \times 10^6)^3) = 1{,}855$ kg/m³, and
$g = GM/R^2 = 6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times 1.303 \times 10^{22}/(1.188 \times 10^6)^2 = 0.62$ m/s².

**Where the resonances are.** By Kepler's third law, $a^3/T^2$ is the same for everything going round the Sun. A body
that goes round twice for Neptune's three trips has $T = \tfrac32 T_N$, so $a = a_N \times 1.5^{2/3} = 30.07 \times
1.310 = 39.4$ AU. For the 2:1 resonance, $a = 30.07 \times 2^{2/3} = 47.7$ AU. Arrokoth's year:
$44.2^{1.5} = 294$ years.

**Sunlight.** $1{,}361/39.48^2 = 0.873$ W/m², 1/1,560 of the Earth's. At Pluto's nearest point it gets
$1{,}361/29.65^2 = 1.55$ W/m², and at its farthest $1{,}361/48.83^2 = 0.571$ W/m², 2.7 times less.

**The barycentre.** It lies $19{,}596 \times 1.586/(13.03 + 1.586) = 2{,}126$ km from Pluto's centre, outside its
radius of 1,188 km.

**Eris against Pluto.** $1.66/1.303 = 1.27$: Eris is 27% heavier. Its average distance is
$(38.4 + 97.7)/2 = 68$ AU, and $68^{1.5} = 561$ years, close to its 558.

**Light time.** At 34 AU, $34 \times 1.496 \times 10^{11}/(3.00 \times 10^8) = 17{,}000$ s, or 4.7 hours.

**Cleared or not.** Pluto has 0.0022 Earths of mass; the Kuiper belt as a whole about 0.02. So Pluto is roughly a
tenth of the belt it moves through. Neptune, at 17 Earths, is hundreds of times heavier than the whole belt. That is
the difference the 2006 rules try to capture.

    - Masses, radius, distances, Charon's distance, Neptune's 30.07 AU: NASA fact sheets (above and
      [Neptune fact sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/neptunefact.html)). The Kuiper belt's 0.02
      Earth masses, Eris's mass and orbit: Wikipedia (above).
    - Kepler's third law, the solar constant of 1,361 W/m², $G$ and the speed of light: standard. Everything else: our

## 16.9 Why Pluto's air comes and goes { #derive }

Pluto's air is not held in place like the Earth's. It is in balance with the frost on the ground: nitrogen ice turns to
gas until the gas presses down hard enough, and then as much gas freezes back as escapes from the ice. So the pressure
depends almost only on the temperature of the frost. How strongly?

**The rule for frost and gas.** For a solid in balance with its own vapour, the pressure $P$ changes with the
temperature $T$ as

$$\frac{dP}{dT} = \frac{L P}{R T^2},$$

the **Clausius–Clapeyron** equation, where $L$ is the heat needed to turn one mole of the solid into gas and
$R = 8.314$ J/(mol·K). (It assumes the gas is ideal and takes up far more room than the solid, both true here.)
Dividing by $P$ and integrating,

$$\ln P = -\frac{L}{R T} + \text{constant}.$$

So the pressure rises **exponentially** as the frost warms.

**Fitting it.** Wikipedia gives the pressure above nitrogen frost as about **10 Pa at 40 K** and close to **10,000 Pa
at 60 K**. Then

$$\frac{L}{R} = \frac{\ln(10{,}000/10)}{1/40 - 1/60} = \frac{6.91}{0.00833} = 829 \text{ K},$$

so $L = 829 \times 8.314 = 6.9$ kJ/mol.

**Pluto in 2015.** New Horizons found about 1 Pa. Solving $\ln(10/1) = 829 \,(1/T - 1/40)$ gives $1/T = 0.025 +
0.00278$, so $T = 36.0$ K: the nitrogen frost was at about **36 K** (Figure B).

**One degree matters.** Near 36 K, $d \ln P/dT = L/(RT^2) = 829/36^2 = 0.64$ per kelvin, and $e^{0.64} = 1.9$. Each
degree of warming almost **doubles** the air. The tripling from 1988 to 2015 needs the frost to warm by only
$\ln 3/0.64 \approx 1.6$ K, or exactly, from 34.4 K to 36.0 K.

**Towards the far point.** Pluto gets 2.7 times less sunlight at its farthest point than at its nearest. If the frost
temperature followed the sunlight straight away, as $T \propto r^{-1/2}$, it would fall from 36 K at 34 AU to
$36 \times \sqrt{34/48.8} = 30$ K when Pluto is farthest out, around 2114. Then

$$P = 10 \times e^{-829\,(1/30 - 1/40)} = 0.010 \text{ Pa},$$

a hundredth of today's: the air would almost all freeze onto the ground. But the ground stores heat, and the frost
moves from place to place as the seasons change, so the more careful models predict that Pluto keeps a real
atmosphere all the way round its orbit.

**A check on Triton.** Our curve gives 3.4 Pa at 38 K. Triton, at 38 K, has 1.4 to 1.9 Pa. That is off
by a factor of about 2, as close as two rough points from Wikipedia can give, and the shape is right.

**And Mars.** Mars works the same way with carbon dioxide instead of nitrogen. Its polar caps, at the frost point of
carbon dioxide, hold the pressure near 6 mb, and as each cap grows and shrinks over the year the pressure swings by
about a factor of two (section 16.7).

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Why is Pluto called a dwarf planet rather than a planet?

??? note "Answer"
    It goes round the Sun and is round, but it has not cleared its neighbourhood: it shares its part of space with
    huge numbers of other Kuiper belt objects, and it is only a small part of the belt's mass.

**2.** (beginner) What is Sputnik Planitia, and why does it have no craters?

??? note "Answer"
    It is the western half of Pluto's bright "heart", a basin about 1,400 by 1,200 km filled with glaciers of
    nitrogen ice. The ice slowly churns in convection cells and flows, so craters are wiped out; the surface is less
    than about 10 million years old.

**3.** (beginner) What is special about how Pluto and Charon go round each other?

??? note "Answer"
    Charon is so big that the point they both go round lies outside Pluto. Each keeps the same face to the other, so
    from one side of Pluto Charon never moves in the sky, and from the other side it is never seen.

**4.** (intermediate) Why do so many Kuiper belt objects sit at 39.4 AU?

??? note "Answer"
    There they go round twice while Neptune goes round three times. This 3:2 resonance means Neptune always passes
    them when they are far from it, so they are protected. About a quarter of known Kuiper belt objects are there.

**5.** (intermediate) Pluto was moving away from the Sun between 1988 and 2015. Why did its air get thicker?

??? note "Answer"
    Its north pole came out of a 124-year night in 1987, so sunlight began warming nitrogen frost there. Because the
    pressure depends so strongly on the frost temperature, a warming of only about 1.6 K was enough to triple it.

**6.** (intermediate) Why is Arrokoth so interesting to scientists?

??? note "Answer"
    It is a small, red, little-cratered "snowman" of two lobes that merged gently, on a nearly round orbit that has
    kept it away from the planets. It has probably hardly changed since it formed 4.5 billion years ago, so it shows
    what the building blocks of planets were like.

**7.** (advanced) A Kuiper belt object goes round the Sun at an average distance of 43 AU. How long is its year, and
which part of the belt is it in?

??? note "Answer"
    $43^{1.5} = 282$ years. At 43 AU it lies between the 3:2 resonance at 39.4 AU and the 2:1 at 47.7 AU, so it is in
    the classical belt's region.

**8.** (expert) Using $L/R = 829$ K, by what factor would Pluto's air pressure change if the nitrogen frost cooled from
36 K to 34 K?

??? note "Answer"
    $P_{34}/P_{36} = e^{-829\,(1/34 - 1/36)} = e^{-829 \times 0.001634} = e^{-1.35} = 0.26$. The pressure would fall to
    about a quarter, from about 1 Pa to 0.26 Pa.

## Summary

- Pluto is 2,377 km across, smaller than our Moon, on a stretched orbit from 29.6 to 48.8 AU that is kept safe from
  Neptune by the 3:2 resonance.
- Charon is half Pluto's width; the two go round a point outside Pluto, each facing the other. Four small moons go
  round both.
- New Horizons found a young plain of churning nitrogen glaciers, water-ice mountains, possible ice volcanoes, perhaps
  an ocean, and blue haze in air about a hundred-thousandth as thick as ours.
- The Kuiper belt runs from 30 to about 50 AU, holds about 2% of an Earth's mass, and is sorted by Neptune into
  plutinos, a classical belt, the Kuiper cliff and the scattered disc.
- In 2006 the IAU made Pluto a dwarf planet, because it has not cleared its neighbourhood; some scientists disagree.
- Pluto was found by Clyde Tombaugh in 1930 and named by Venetia Burney; the belt was predicted for decades and found
  by Jewitt and Luu in 1992.
- Mars and Pluto both have air that freezes onto the ground; Pluto's is far more sensitive.
- Pluto's pressure about doubles for every degree its nitrogen frost warms, by the Clausius–Clapeyron rule.
