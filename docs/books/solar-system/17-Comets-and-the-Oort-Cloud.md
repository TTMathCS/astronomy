# 17. Comets and the Oort cloud

*Book 1 met comets as sights in the sky: a fuzzy head, two tails, and a famous few that come back. This chapter goes
behind the sight. It follows comets back to where they are stored, in the Kuiper belt and in the huge Oort cloud that
nobody has ever seen. It asks what they are made of and how they die, how people around the world worked them out,
and what the spacecraft that flew past them, hit one and landed on another have found. It ends at Mars, which a comet
from another star passed in 2025, and with a short calculation that shows why a comet's dust tail curves and why its
finest dust leaves the solar system for good.*

## 17.1 Comets at a glance { #basics }

**A frozen lump with a long reach.** A comet is a small body of ice, dust and rock. Far from the Sun it is only a dark,
frozen nucleus a few kilometres across. Near the Sun it warms up, and gas and dust stream off it into a **coma**, which
can be 15 times wider than the Earth, and into **tails** that can stretch more than 1 AU (book 1,
[section 15.6](../night-sky/15-Meteors-and-Comets.md#comets)).

**Sorted by orbit.** Astronomers sort comets by how long they take to go round the Sun.

| Kind | Time to go round | Tilt of the orbit | Example |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Jupiter-family | less than 20 years | low, up to 30° | 67P/Churyumov–Gerasimenko, 6.45 years |
| Halley-type | 20 to 200 years | anything, even backwards | Halley's comet, about 76 years |
| Long-period | more than 200 years, up to millions | anything | Hale–Bopp |
| Interstellar | never comes back | anything | 3I/ATLAS |

**Two homes.** Short-period comets, those that come back within 200 years, come from the **Kuiper belt** and the
**scattered disc** beyond Neptune (chapter 16, [section 16.3](16-Pluto-and-the-Kuiper-Belt.md#belt)). Most of them
go round the same way as the planets, close to the plane of the planets' orbits. Long-period comets come mostly from
the **Oort cloud**, far beyond that, and they fall in from every direction (section 17.2). Interstellar comets come from
other stars altogether.

**Names.** A comet's name starts with a letter for its kind of orbit: **P/** for a period of 200 years or less,
**C/** for anything longer, **D/** for a periodic comet that has disappeared, and **X/** when the orbit is unknown.
Then come the year it was found and, usually, its finders. Periodic comets seen at more than one return get a number:
67P was the 67th. Objects from other stars get an **I**: 3I/ATLAS was the third found.

**How many.** By November 2021, 4,584 comets were known. That is a tiny part of all the comets out there.

**Fast near the Sun.** A comet that falls in from very far away passes the Earth's distance from the Sun at about
**42 km/s**, $\sqrt{2}$ times the Earth's own 29.8 km/s (section 17.8).

## 17.2 The Oort cloud { #oort }

**A cloud nobody has seen.** Long-period comets fall in from every direction, on orbits that reach thousands of AU
from the Sun. In 1932 the Estonian astronomer **Ernst Öpik** suggested that they come from a cloud of icy bodies at the
edge of the solar system, and in 1950 the Dutch astronomer **Jan Oort** took up the idea again to solve a puzzle
(section 17.5). It is now called the **Oort cloud**, or the Öpik–Oort cloud. No object has ever been seen out there:
the bodies are small, dark and far too far away. The cloud's size and shape come from the orbits of the comets that
fall out of it.

**Two parts.** The cloud is thought to have two parts.

- The **inner Oort cloud**, also called the **Hills cloud**, is a thick ring from about **2,000 to 20,000 AU**.
  Models suggest it holds tens or hundreds of times as many bodies as the outer cloud.
- The **outer Oort cloud** is a sphere from about **20,000 to 50,000 AU**. Its edge is not sharp: some count the cloud
  out to **200,000 AU**, more than three light-years.

A few bodies whose orbits never come near the planets may belong to the inner cloud, such as **Sedna**, first reported
in 2004, which never comes closer to the Sun than 76 AU.

**How much is out there.** The outer cloud may hold trillions of bodies more than 1 km across, and billions of 20 km.
If each is like Halley's comet, all together they weigh about **five Earths**. Older estimates were up to 380 Earths.

**How it got there.** The bodies probably did not form out there. They are thought to have formed much closer to the
Sun, among the young giant planets, and to have been flung onto huge, stretched orbits by the giants' gravity, Jupiter
above all. Passing stars, and clouds of gas in the Galaxy, then pulled their far ends about, so they no longer came back
near the planets. Some 50 to 100 Earth masses may have been thrown out in all; most of it was lost or broken up in
collisions.

**Held by a thread.** The Sun's gravity is weak out there. Its grip ends about **230,000 AU** out, where the pull of
the rest of the Galaxy takes over (book 3, [section 9.7](../orbits/09-Lagrange-Points-and-Trojans.md#hill)). A body at
50,000 AU moves round the Sun at less than 1 m/s (section 17.8). So small nudges change its orbit easily:

- the **galactic tide**, the stretching pull of the Milky Way, which works like the Moon's pull on the Earth's oceans;
- **passing stars**: **Scholz's Star** probably went through the outer cloud about 70,000 years ago, though it was too
  light and too fast to do much, and **Gliese 710** is the known star most likely to stir the cloud in the next 10
  million years.

A nudged comet whose new orbit dips close to the Sun takes about **2 million years** to fall in from 50,000 AU
(section 17.8).

**How far is that?** The nearest star, Proxima Centauri, is 4.25 light-years away, about **269,000 AU** (book 2,
[section 2.7](../light-and-telescopes/02-The-Speed-of-Light.md#lookback)). **Voyager 1**, the most distant spacecraft,
will take about **300 years** to reach the Oort cloud and about **30,000 years** to cross it.

## 17.3 What comets are made of { #makeup }

**Ices, dust and rock.** A comet's nucleus is a mix of rock, dust, water ice and frozen carbon dioxide, carbon
monoxide, methane and ammonia, with carbon compounds such as methanol, hydrogen cyanide, formaldehyde, ethanol and
ethane. The surface is usually dry and dusty. The ices lie under a crust several metres thick.

**Snowball or dirtball?** In 1950 **Fred Whipple** suggested that a comet is not a rock with some ice in it but ice with
some dust and rock in it, a **dirty snowball**. After a spacecraft hit a comet in 2005 (section 17.6),
"icy dirtball" seemed a better name. A study in 2014 compared comets to **deep-fried ice cream**: a hard crust of dense,
crystal ice mixed with carbon compounds, over colder, looser ice.

**Darker than a road.** Comet nuclei are among the darkest things in the solar system. The Giotto spacecraft found that
Halley's comet reflects only about **4%** of the light that falls on it. Comet Borrelly reflects less than 3%. A road
of asphalt reflects 7%.

**Lighter than water.** Comets have an average density of about **0.6 g/cm³**, 600 kg/m³, so a piece of comet would
float. They are too small and light for gravity to pull them round, so they have odd shapes. **67P** is two lobes
joined by a neck, about 4.3 by 4.1 km.

**Sizes.** Most short-period comets are 1.5 to 5 km across, and long-period ones usually 4 to 20 km. The largest known
nuclei are up to 30 km in radius. The smallest are tiny: 322P/SOHO may be only 100 to 200 m across. Comets smaller than
100 m seem to be rare.

**The coma.** Within 3 or 4 AU of the Sun, water makes up as much as 90% of the gas leaving a comet. A coma can
suddenly swell: after an outburst in October 2007, comet **17P/Holmes** briefly had a thin cloud of dust bigger than
the Sun.

**What Rosetta tasted.** The European spacecraft **Rosetta** spent two years beside 67P (section 17.6). Around the
comet it found:

- **glycine**, the simplest amino acid, and methylamine and ethylamine, molecules from which amino acids can form;
- free **oxygen** gas, 1% to 10% as much as the water, one of the mission's most striking finds;
- water with three times as much **heavy hydrogen**, deuterium, as the Earth's oceans. So the Earth's water probably
  did not come mostly from comets like this one.

Its lander, Philae, found sixteen carbon compounds, four of them never seen on a comet before. Glycine had already been
found in 2009 in dust from comet Wild 2 that NASA's Stardust brought home (section 17.6).

## 17.4 How comets end { #fates }

A comet cannot last for ever on an orbit that brings it close to the Sun. There are several ways for it to go.

**Fading.** Every pass near the Sun boils off some of its ice. A Jupiter-family comet stays active for about 10,000
years, or about 1,000 orbits. Long-period comets fade much faster: only 1 in 10 survives more than 50 close passes. A
comet that has lost its ice, or sealed it under its crust, can look just like an asteroid.

**Breaking up.** The **Kreutz sungrazers** are a family of comets that skim the Sun. They are pieces of one large comet
that broke up several centuries ago, and they keep breaking: the Great Comet of 1882 split into several pieces, and
**Ikeya–Seki** broke into three in 1965. Since 1995 the SOHO spacecraft, which watches the Sun, has found more than
**5,000** small members of the family. None of these small ones has survived its pass by the Sun.

**Crashing.** In 1994 the pieces of Comet Shoemaker–Levy 9 hit Jupiter (chapter 12,
[section 12.4](12-Jupiter.md#history)).

**Being thrown about.** A pass close to Jupiter can change a comet's orbit completely. On 4 February 1959, 67P passed
0.05 AU from Jupiter, and its nearest point to the Sun moved in from 2.7 AU to 1.28 AU, where it still is. In
November 2220 it will pass 0.14 AU from Jupiter again.

**Being thrown out.** Comet **C/1980 E1** took about 7.1 million years to go round the Sun. Then, in 1980, it passed
Jupiter and was sped up onto a **hyperbola**, with an eccentricity of 1.057 (book 3,
[section 4.3](../orbits/04-Ellipses.md#conics)). It is leaving the solar system for ever.

**Leaving a trail.** A comet's dust spreads out along its orbit. If the Earth passes through it, we see a meteor
shower (book 1, [section 15.2](../night-sky/15-Meteors-and-Comets.md#showers)). Comet 96P/Machholz, for example, is the
parent of two showers, the Quadrantids and the Arietids.

## 17.5 Who worked it out { #history }

Book 1 told the story of comets as sights, from Babylon and China to Halley's return (book 1,
[section 15.7](../night-sky/15-Meteors-and-Comets.md#history)). Here is how people worked out what comets are and where
they come from.

- **China.** Comets were noticed for thousands of years, as Chinese oracle bones show, and Chinese astronomers kept
  records of them for centuries (history book, [section 4.7](../history/04-China.md#guests)).
- **Greece and Rome.** **Aristotle**, in the 4th century BCE, thought comets were in the air, below the Moon. In the
  1st century CE **Seneca** argued that comets move too regularly and are not blown about by the wind, so they cannot
  be in the air.
- **1577.** **Tycho Brahe** and **Michael Maestlin** measured the parallax of the great comet of that year and showed
  that it was far beyond the Moon (history book, [section 14.2](../history/14-Tycho-and-Kepler.md#newstar)).
  **Taqi al-Din** watched the same comet from the new observatory in Istanbul (history book,
  [section 8.10](../history/08-The-Islamic-World-and-Persia.md#samarkand)).
- **1687 to 1705.** Newton showed that a body moving under gravity follows an ellipse, a parabola or a hyperbola, and
  Halley used this to show that comets come back (book 3,
  [section 10.2](../orbits/10-Wobbling-Orbits-and-the-Finding-of-Neptune.md#halley)).
- **1836.** The German mathematician **Friedrich Wilhelm Bessel**, after watching jets of vapour from Halley's comet in
  1835, suggested that the push of the escaping gas could change a comet's orbit. It can, and it does.
- **1888.** The German astronomer **Heinrich Kreutz** showed that the great comets of 1843, 1880 and 1882 were
  related: the Kreutz family.
- **1932 and 1950.** **Ernst Öpik** and then **Jan Oort** proposed the Oort cloud. Oort's puzzle was that comets that
  come close to the Sun cannot last: they boil away, break up or get thrown out. So they cannot have been on such orbits
  for 4.5 billion years, and must have been kept somewhere far away. Oort also noticed that many long-period comets
  have their far ends at around 20,000 AU.
- **1950.** **Fred Whipple** proposed the dirty snowball.
- **1965.** Two Japanese amateur astronomers, **Kaoru Ikeya** and **Tsutomu Seki**, found the sungrazer Ikeya–Seki on
  18 September 1965, independently and within 15 minutes of each other.
- **1969.** **Svetlana Gerasimenko** photographed a comet at the Alma-Ata Astrophysical Institute, in what was then the
  Kazakh Soviet Socialist Republic, on 11 September 1969. Back in Kyiv on 22 October, **Klim Churyumov** found another
  comet near the edge of the plate: 67P.
- **1981.** **Jack G. Hills** proposed the inner Oort cloud.
- **1992.** The Kuiper belt was found, the home of short-period comets (chapter 16,
  [section 16.5](16-Pluto-and-the-Kuiper-Belt.md#history)).
- **2010.** The 2,000th comet found in SOHO's pictures, which anyone can look through online, was found by the Polish
  amateur astronomer **Michał Kusiak** on 26 December 2010.
- **2025.** On 1 July 2025 the ATLAS survey telescope at Río Hurtado, Chile, found **3I/ATLAS**, the third known visitor
  from another star (section 17.7).

## 17.6 Visiting comets { #missions }

**The first visit.** The International Cometary Explorer, a spacecraft given a new job in 1982, flew past comet
Giacobini–Zinner in 1985.

**The Halley armada, 1986.** When Halley's comet came back, five spacecraft went to meet it: Europe's **Giotto**, two
Soviet and French probes, **Vega 1** and **Vega 2**, and two from Japan's space science institute. NASA sent none.
Giotto went closest, **596 km** from the nucleus. Without the measurements made by the other probes it would have had
to stay 4,000 km away.

**Stardust, 2004 to 2006.** NASA's Stardust flew 237 km from comet **Wild 2** on 2 January 2004 at 6.1 km/s, catching
dust from the coma. Its capsule came down in the Bonneville Salt Flats of Utah on 15 January 2006 with the first comet
dust ever brought to the Earth.

**Deep Impact, 2005.** NASA's Deep Impact was launched on 12 January 2005. On **4 July 2005** it put a 372 kg
impactor, much of it copper, a metal not expected in a comet, in the path of comet **Tempel 1**, which hit it at
**10.2 km/s**, with the energy of 4.8 tonnes of TNT. The main spacecraft watched the blast of dust. Its signals took 7
minutes 26 seconds to reach the Earth. Later, renamed EPOXI, it flew past comet Hartley 2.

**Rosetta, 2004 to 2016.** The European Space Agency's **Rosetta** was launched on an Ariane 5 from Kourou, French
Guiana, on **2 March 2004**. On the way it swung past Mars in 2007 (book 3,
[section 16.7](../orbits/16-Gravity-Assists.md#mars)) and flew past two asteroids, Šteins in 2008 and Lutetia in 2010.
On **6 August 2014** it became the first spacecraft to go into orbit round a comet, 67P, at 30 km and later 10 km.

- On **12 November 2014** its 100 kg lander, **Philae**, came down at about 1 m/s. It bounced twice and came to rest
  in shadow. Its battery ran out two days later. It woke briefly in June and July 2015.
- Rosetta stayed with the comet for two years, as it passed closest to the Sun on 13 August 2015 and went out again.
- On **30 September 2016** Rosetta was steered down onto the comet, in a region called Ma'at, and the mission ended.
  It cost about €1.3 billion.

The names come from Egypt. The **Rosetta Stone** and the **Philae obelisk** carry the same words in different
scripts, and comparing them helped people read Egyptian hieroglyphs. The hope was that the spacecraft would do the same
for comets.

**Still to come.** China's **Tianwen-2** plans to go on to 311P/PanSTARRS, a comet in the asteroid belt, after
returning its asteroid sample (chapter 9, [section 9.6](09-The-Asteroid-Belt-and-Ceres.md#visits)). **Comet
Interceptor**, from the European Space Agency with Japan's JAXA, is due for launch between August 2028 and July 2029,
on the same rocket as the ARIEL telescope. It will wait at the Sun–Earth L2 point (book 3,
[section 9.3](../orbits/09-Lagrange-Points-and-Trojans.md#parking)) for up to three years, until a suitable new comet
is found, and then dash out to meet it. A long-period comet gives too little warning to build a spacecraft for it; this
way, the spacecraft is ready first.

**Crossing paths.** Since 1959, 67P has come closer to the Sun than Mars ever does, and gone out a little beyond
Jupiter's orbit. Other Jupiter-family comets cross Mars's orbit too. Rosetta, on its way to 67P, swung past Mars itself
in 2007.

**Siding Spring, 2014.** On 19 October 2014 comet Siding Spring passed about 140,000 km from Mars, and the spacecraft
there sampled its dust raining into the Martian air (book 1,
[section 15.8](../night-sky/15-Meteors-and-Comets.md#mars)).

**A visitor from another star, 2025.** **3I/ATLAS** came in from beyond the solar system at **58 km/s**, too fast for
the Sun ever to hold it. On **3 October 2025** it passed **0.194 AU** from Mars, 29 million km. It may be very old: if
it comes from the thick disc of the Milky Way, it may be at least 7 billion years old, older than the Sun.

The spacecraft at Mars had the best seats.

- ESA's **ExoMars Trace Gas Orbiter** took pictures with its camera, CaSSIS. The comet was about 50,000 times fainter
  than the things it usually looks at, and needed exposures of 5 seconds.
- ESA's **Mars Express** also looked, but its shorter exposures could not show it.
- NASA's **Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter** observed it too, but its data were not released at the time, because NASA
  was shut down.
- China's **Tianwen-1** also took pictures of it.

It mattered because on **21 October 2025** the comet was almost behind the Sun as seen from the Earth, and on 29
October it passed closest to the Sun, at 1.36 AU. For those weeks, telescopes on the Earth could hardly see it.

**What it is made of.** The James Webb Space Telescope found 3I/ATLAS unusually rich in carbon dioxide, and the Very
Large Telescope saw cyanide and nickel gas, much as in the solar system's own comets. Its nucleus is less than 1 km
across.

## 17.8 Working it out { #working }

**Sizes of orbits.** By Kepler's third law, a period of $T$ years means an average distance of $T^{2/3}$ AU. Halley:
$76^{2/3} = 17.9$ AU. 67P: $6.45^{2/3} = 3.47$ AU. 67P's farthest point is then $2 \times 3.47 - 1.28 = 5.65$ AU,
close to Jupiter's 5.2 AU, which is why Jupiter keeps changing its orbit.

**Falling from the Oort cloud.** A comet that swings between 1 AU and 50,000 AU has an average distance of about
25,000 AU. Its period is $25{,}000^{1.5} = 3.95$ million years, and the fall in takes half of that, about 2 million
years.

**Speed at the far end.** From the speed formula of book 3,
[section 7.6](../orbits/07-Speed-Energy-and-Escape.md#visviva), the speed at the far end of that orbit is
$v^2 = 2GM(1/r_{far} - 1/(r_{far} + r_{near}))$, which gives $v = 0.84$ m/s, about 3 km/h, a slow stroll.

**Speed near the Sun.** At 1 AU, a body falling from very far away moves at $\sqrt{2GM/r} = 42.1$ km/s; the Earth, on
a round orbit, at $\sqrt{GM/r} = 29.8$ km/s. 3I/ATLAS came in at 58 km/s, so at 1.36 AU it was going
$\sqrt{58^2 + 2GM/r} = 68$ km/s.

**To the nearest star.** One light-year is $9.461 \times 10^{15} / 1.496 \times 10^{11} = 63{,}240$ AU, so 4.2465
light-years is 268,600 AU, about 269,000.

**Voyager 1.** At 17 km/s Voyager 1 covers 3.6 AU a year. From 172.5 AU in October 2026 (book 3,
[section 7.5](../orbits/07-Speed-Energy-and-Escape.md#bound)), 300 years carries it to about 1,250 AU, so the 300-year
figure takes the cloud's inner edge as nearer than 2,000 AU. To reach 2,000 AU would take about 510 years.

**Deep Impact's distance.** Radio signals took 446 s to arrive, so Tempel 1 was $446 \times 2.998 \times 10^8 =
1.34 \times 10^{11}$ m away, 0.89 AU.

**Floating comets.** 600 kg/m³ is 0.6 times the density of water, so a piece of comet would float, like most kinds of
wood.

    - Kepler's third law, the speed of light, $GM$ of the Sun, the astronomical unit and the light-year, and the
      vis-viva formula: standard (book 3, chapters 3 and 7). Halley's 76 years: book 3, chapter 10. 67P: section 17.7.
      Jupiter's 5.2 AU: chapter 12. 3I/ATLAS's 58 and 68 km/s: Wikipedia, 3I/ATLAS (above).

## 17.9 Sunlight against gravity { #derive }

Book 1 said that the dust tail is pushed by sunlight and curves. How strong is the push, and which grains does it
beat? The answer turns out not to depend on how far the grain is from the Sun.

**The push of light.** Light carries momentum: a beam of power $P$ pushes on something that soaks it up with a force
$P/c$. At distance $r$ from the Sun, each square metre gets $L/(4\pi r^2)$ watts, where $L = 3.828 \times 10^{26}$ W
is the Sun's power. A grain of radius $s$ catches $\pi s^2$ of it, so

$$F_{light} = \frac{Q L \pi s^2}{4 \pi r^2 c} = \frac{Q L s^2}{4 r^2 c},$$

where $Q$ says how well the grain catches light. We take $Q = 1$, a grain that soaks up everything.

**The pull of gravity.** A grain of density $\rho$ has mass $\tfrac43 \pi s^3 \rho$, so

$$F_{gravity} = \frac{G M \cdot \tfrac43 \pi s^3 \rho}{r^2}.$$

**The ratio.** Dividing,

$$\beta = \frac{F_{light}}{F_{gravity}} = \frac{3 Q L}{16 \pi G M c \rho s}.$$

Both forces fall off as $1/r^2$, so $r$ cancels: a grain has the same $\beta$ everywhere. It moves as if the Sun's
mass were only $(1 - \beta)M$.

**Numbers.** $3L/(16 \pi G M c) = 3 \times 3.828 \times 10^{26} / (16\pi \times 1.327 \times 10^{20} \times 2.998
\times 10^8) = 5.74 \times 10^{-4}$ kg/m². For fluffy comet dust of 1,000 kg/m³:

| Grain radius | $\beta$ |
| --- | --- |
| 1 mm | 0.00057 |
| 100 µm | 0.0057 |
| 10 µm | 0.057 |
| 1 µm | 0.57 |
| 0.57 µm | 1 |

For rocky grains of 3,000 kg/m³, $\beta$ reaches 1 at 0.19 µm. Grains smaller than that feel more push than pull, and
are blown straight out of the solar system.

**Which grains get away?** A grain leaves the comet at almost the comet's own speed $v$. It escapes if its energy is
not negative, now that the Sun pulls on it more weakly:

$$\tfrac12 v^2 \ge \frac{(1 - \beta) G M}{r}.$$

At the comet's nearest point $q$ on an orbit of eccentricity $e$, $v^2 = GM(1 + e)/q$ (book 3,
[section 7.6](../orbits/07-Speed-Energy-and-Escape.md#visviva)). Putting that in, $(1 + e)/2 \ge 1 - \beta$, or

$$\beta \ge \frac{1 - e}{2}.$$

| Orbit | $e$ | Lost if $\beta$ is at least | Lost if the radius is under (1,000 kg/m³) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| round | 0 | 1/2 | 1.1 µm |
| 67P | about 0.63 | 0.18 | 3 µm |
| Halley's comet | 0.967 | 0.0165 | 35 µm |
| fresh from the Oort cloud | 0.9999 | 0.00005 | about 1 cm |

So a comet on a short orbit keeps its bigger grains near its path, where they can make a meteor shower, while a comet
fresh from the Oort cloud loses almost all the dust it sheds near the Sun.

**Why the dust tail curves.** A grain that stays behind feels a weaker Sun but still has the comet's speed. It is going
too fast for its weaker pull, so it swings out onto a wider, slower orbit and drops behind the comet. The smallest
grains, with the biggest $\beta$, swing out furthest. The tail becomes a curved fan, bent back along the comet's path.
The gas in the ion tail is different: the solar wind (chapter 18) carries it straight out from the Sun.

**And Mars.** At Mars, 1.524 AU out, the Sun's pull is $GM/r^2 = 0.00255$ m/s² per kilogram, and sunlight pushes a 1 µm
grain with 0.57 times that, about 0.0015 m/s². Mars's own gravity, 3.73 m/s², is about 2,500 times stronger. So once
comet dust like Siding Spring's reaches Mars's air, sunlight has no say in it: the planet wins.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) What is the difference between a Jupiter-family comet and a long-period comet?

??? note "Answer"
    A Jupiter-family comet goes round the Sun in less than 20 years, on an orbit tilted by less than 30°, and comes
    from the Kuiper belt or scattered disc. A long-period comet takes more than 200 years, can come from any direction,
    and comes mostly from the Oort cloud.

**2.** (beginner) What is a comet's nucleus made of, and how dark is it?

??? note "Answer"
    Rock, dust and ices of water, carbon dioxide, carbon monoxide, methane and ammonia, with carbon compounds, under a
    dusty crust. It is darker than a road: Halley's nucleus reflects only about 4% of the light falling on it.

**3.** (beginner) What happened when Philae landed on comet 67P?

??? note "Answer"
    It came down at about 1 m/s on 12 November 2014, bounced twice and came to rest in shadow. Its battery ran out two
    days later, and it woke only briefly in June and July 2015.

**4.** (intermediate) Nobody has seen an object in the Oort cloud. Why do astronomers think it is there?

??? note "Answer"
    Long-period comets fall in from every direction on orbits reaching thousands of AU, and comets that pass close to
    the Sun cannot survive for billions of years. So there must be a store of comets far away, kept cold and safe,
    from which passing stars and the galactic tide send new ones in.

**5.** (intermediate) What did Rosetta find in the water of 67P, and what does it suggest?

??? note "Answer"
    The water had three times as much deuterium, heavy hydrogen, as the Earth's oceans. So the Earth's water probably
    did not come mostly from comets like 67P.

**6.** (intermediate) Why were the views of 3I/ATLAS from spacecraft at Mars so useful in October 2025?

??? note "Answer"
    The comet passed only 0.194 AU from Mars on 3 October, while from the Earth it was close to the Sun in the sky,
    almost behind it on 21 October. Spacecraft at Mars could see it when telescopes on the Earth could hardly do so.

**7.** (advanced) A long-period comet swings between 1 AU and 30,000 AU. How long does it take to go round, and how
long did it take to fall in?

??? note "Answer"
    Its average distance is about $(1 + 30{,}000)/2 = 15{,}000$ AU, so the period is $15{,}000^{1.5} = 1.84$ million
    years. The fall from the far end takes half of that, about 0.92 million years.

**8.** (expert) A comet on an orbit of eccentricity 0.9 sheds dust at its nearest point to the Sun. Which grains of
density 1,000 kg/m³ leave the solar system?

??? note "Answer"
    They need $\beta \ge (1 - 0.9)/2 = 0.05$. Since $\beta = 5.74 \times 10^{-4}/(\rho s)$, that means
    $s \le 5.74 \times 10^{-4}/(1{,}000 \times 0.05) = 1.15 \times 10^{-5}$ m. Grains smaller than about 11 µm in
    radius are lost.

## Summary

- Comets are sorted by their orbits: Jupiter-family and Halley-type comets from the Kuiper belt and scattered disc, and
  long-period comets mostly from the Oort cloud.
- The Oort cloud, never seen, runs from about 2,000 AU to perhaps 200,000 AU, and may hold trillions of comets; passing
  stars and the galactic tide send some of them in.
- A comet's nucleus is a dark, light, odd-shaped mix of ices, dust, rock and carbon compounds under a dusty crust.
- Comets fade, break up, crash into planets, or are thrown out of the solar system, and they leave dust trails that
  make meteor showers.
- From Seneca and Taqi al-Din to Öpik, Oort, Churyumov and Gerasimenko, people worked out that comets are distant,
  icy bodies stored at the edge of the solar system.
- Spacecraft have flown past comets, hit Tempel 1, brought home Wild 2's dust, and orbited and landed on 67P; Comet
  Interceptor will wait in space for a new comet.
- Comets cross Mars's orbit, and in 2025 the interstellar comet 3I/ATLAS passed Mars and was photographed from orbit
  there.
- For a dust grain, the push of sunlight against the Sun's pull, $\beta$, is the same at every distance; the smallest
  grains are blown out of the solar system.
