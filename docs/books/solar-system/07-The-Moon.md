# 7. The Moon

*The Moon is the only other world people have walked on, and the only one from which we have rock collected by
hand. It is a quarter of the Earth's width, airless and grey, its dark seas made of old lava and its bright highlands
of rock that floated to the top of a molten ocean. This chapter looks at what the Moon is made of, how it was
probably born in a giant collision, and how spacecraft from the Soviet Union, the United States, Japan, Europe,
China, India and others have explored it. Mars's two tiny moons make a strange comparison.*

## 7.1 The Earth's companion { #basics }

The Moon is **3,475 km** wide, about 27% of the Earth's width. Its mass is only 1.2% of the Earth's, about an 81st,
and its density is 3,344 kg/m³, much less than the Earth's 5,513. Its gravity is **1.62 m/s²**, about a sixth of
ours. It is only the fifth largest moon in the solar system, but no other planet has a moon so large compared with
itself.

It goes round the Earth once in **27.32 days**, at an average distance of **384,400 km**, about 30 Earth widths
away. The distance changes from about 357,000 to 407,000 km. The Moon turns once in exactly the same time as it
goes round, so it always shows us the same face (book 1,
[section 10.5](../night-sky/10-The-Moons-Phases.md#face); book 3,
[section 8.6](../orbits/08-Tides.md#locking)). The tides it raises are slowly pushing it away, by **3.8 cm a year**
(book 3, [section 8.5](../orbits/08-Tides.md#slowing)).

The Moon has almost no air. NASA puts the whole of it at about 25 tonnes of gas, with a pressure at night of 3 ×
10⁻¹⁵ bar. With no air to hold heat, the ground at the equator swings from about **117 °C** at midday to **−178 °C**
at night.

## 7.2 Seas and highlands { #surface }

Look at the Moon with your eyes alone and you see two kinds of ground: **dark patches** and **bright land**.

The dark patches are called **maria**, Latin for "seas" (one is a *mare*). Early observers thought they might be
water, but they are wide plains of **basalt**, dark iron-rich lava that flooded into huge old impact basins. They
cover about **16%** of the Moon. Almost all of them are on the side facing us, where they cover 31% of the surface,
against only 2% of the far side. The likely reason is that heat-making elements are concentrated under the near
side's crust, so more of the mantle there melted and erupted.

The bright land is the **highlands**, older, rougher and covered in craters. Its rock is mostly **anorthosite**, a
light rock made almost entirely of one mineral, plagioclase. The crust is about 50 km thick on average, and below
it the mantle is richer in iron than the Earth's. At the centre is a small core, about 350 km in radius or less,
probably iron with some sulphur and nickel, and at least partly molten.

The first person known to draw the Moon through a telescope was the English mathematician **Thomas Harriot**, on 5
August 1609. Galileo, a few months later, worked out that the shadows were cast by **mountains and craters** (book
11, [section 15.2](../history/15-Galileo-and-the-Telescope.md#galileo)). Later in the 17th century, Giovanni
Battista Riccioli and Francesco Maria Grimaldi drew up the system of names still used today, with seas such as Mare
Tranquillitatis, the Sea of Tranquillity.

Nobody saw the **far side** until 1959, when the Soviet probe Luna 3 photographed it. It turned out to be almost all
highlands. The far side also holds the largest known basin on the Moon, the South Pole–Aitken basin, and another
huge one, Orientale, nearly 1,000 km wide, sits on the edge between the two sides.

## 7.3 Craters as a clock { #craters }

The Moon has no weather, no water and no moving plates, so craters stay for billions of years. That makes the Moon
a **clock**. A surface that has been exposed for longer has been hit more often, so it has **more craters** in each
square kilometre. The highlands are crowded with craters; the maria have far fewer, so they must be younger.

Crater counts alone only say which surface is older. To turn them into ages in years, you need rocks of known age
from surfaces whose craters have been counted. The Apollo rocks did that. Dated by their radioactive elements,
the highland rocks are up to about **4.44 billion years** old and the mare basalts from about **3.16 billion** years
upwards.

In 2020 China's Chang'e 5 landed on some of the youngest-looking lava on the Moon, in the northern Ocean of Storms,
and brought back basalt less than **2.0 billion years** old, younger than any Apollo sample. That adds a new point
to the clock. Crater counts suggest that the youngest eruptions were about 1.2 billion years ago, but nobody has
samples of those yet.

The same clock is used far beyond the Moon. To date the surface of Mars, scientists count its craters and adjust the
Moon's calibration for Mars's place in the solar system (chapter 11).

??? note "Sources"
    - The ages of 3.16 and 4.44 billion years, crater counting giving about 1.2 billion years for the youngest lava,

    - Chang'e 5's site in the northern Oceanus Procellarum, its samples younger than 2.0 billion years, and their use

    - Older surfaces having more craters, and the Moon's clock being adapted for Mars: standard.

## 7.4 How the Moon formed { #origin }

In 1898 George Darwin suggested that a molten Moon had been spun off the young Earth, and for decades that was the
leading explanation. In 1946 the Canadian geologist **Reginald Daly**, at Harvard, proposed instead that the Moon
came from an **impact**.

The idea came back in 1975, when William K. Hartmann and Donald R. Davis proposed a **giant impact**, and the
Canadian astronomer **Alastair Cameron** and the American astronomer **William Ward** worked out a similar one. Near
the end of planet building, a body about the size of Mars, now called **Theia**, struck the young Earth a glancing blow.
Its iron core sank into the Earth, and rock from both was thrown into orbit, where it gathered into the Moon. This is
now the favourite explanation, because it fits several clues:

- **The same atoms.** The ratios of stable isotopes in lunar and Earth rock are identical, so they share a source.
- **Little iron.** The Moon has only a small iron core, as you would expect if it was made mostly from rocky outer
  layers.
- **Dry and baked.** The Moon has fewer of the substances that boil away easily, which a hot, violent birth would
  drive off.
- **Plenty of spin.** The Earth and Moon together have more spin and orbital motion than the other rocky planets,
  which a glancing blow could supply.

The impact is usually dated to about 4.5 billion years ago, though some dating of when the Moon's molten rock set
suggests 4.35 billion. Nobody yet has a computer model that follows the whole story, from the collision to a single
Moon.

**A molten start.** In 1970 two groups studying chips of anorthosite in the Apollo 11 samples proposed that the
young Moon was covered by a **magma ocean**. As it cooled, light crystals of plagioclase **floated** to the top and
made the bright highland crust, while heavier minerals sank. A typical model starts with a molten layer about 1,000 km
deep. Section 7.9 shows why a quickly built Moon would be hot enough.

## 7.5 Water at the poles { #water }

After Apollo, the Moon was thought to be bone dry. Any water vapour on the surface should be broken up by sunlight
and lost to space. But the Moon's axis is tilted only about 1.5° to its path round the Sun, so the floors of some
craters near the poles never see sunlight at all.
Like the dark craters at Mercury's poles (chapter 4, [section 4.3](04-Mercury.md#ice)), they can trap ice.

- **1994.** The American Clementine probe bounced radio waves into dark craters near the south pole. The echoes
  hinted at ice, but the result was not clear.
- **1998.** Lunar Prospector found extra hydrogen at both poles, which was read as a sign of water ice.
- **2009.** NASA's Moon Mineralogy Mapper, flying on India's **Chandrayaan-1**, found hydroxyl, hydrogen joined to
  oxygen, bound to the soil over large areas. Later that year NASA's LCROSS flew through the plume thrown up when an
  impactor hit a south polar crater, and found signs of water.
- **2010.** Chandrayaan-1's radar found more than 40 dark craters near the north pole that might hold about **600
  million tonnes** of ice.
- **2018.** Water ice was confirmed in several places, not as sheets but probably as small chunks mixed into the
  soil.
- **2020.** Several teams, including the SOFIA airborne telescope, found water molecules even on sunlit ground, 100
  to 400 parts per million.
- **2020 and after.** Tiny glass beads in the Chang'e 5 samples hold up to 1,909 millionths of their mass as water.
  Spread over the Moon, beads like these could hold about 2.7 × 10¹⁴ kg of it.

Water matters because astronauts could use it. It could be drunk, or split into hydrogen and oxygen for rocket fuel.
China's **Chang'e 7** is meant to land near the south pole with a small "hopper" that will jump into dark craters to
look for ice. It was due to launch in August 2026, but China postponed it to 2027.

## 7.6 Missions from around the world { #missions }

- **1959, Luna 2** (Soviet Union) hit the Moon on 14 September, the first human-made object to reach another world.
  Later that year **Luna 3** sent back the first pictures of the far side.
- **1966, Luna 9** (Soviet Union) made the first soft landing, on 3 February, and sent back pictures from the
  surface. The Soviet authorities held the pictures back, but scientists at Jodrell Bank Observatory in England,
  tracking the probe, noticed that the signal used the same format as newspaper picture machines. The *Daily
  Express* brought a receiver, and the pictures were decoded and printed around the world. **Luna 10** became the
  first spacecraft to orbit the Moon the same year.
- **1968 and 1969, Apollo** (United States). Apollo 8 carried the first crew into lunar orbit on 24 December 1968,
  and Apollo 11 landed on **20 July 1969**. By 1972 six Apollo missions had landed **twelve people**, who stayed for
  up to three days.
- **1970 to 1976, Luna** (Soviet Union). Luna 16 was the first robot to bring back Moon soil, and Luna 17 landed
  **Lunokhod 1**, the first remote-controlled rover on another world. Three Luna landers returned 0.3 kg in all.
- **1990, Hiten** (Japan), the first lunar mission from any country other than the Soviet Union and the United
  States, after a gap of 14 years with no missions at all.
- **2004 to 2008.** Europe's **SMART-1** made the first detailed survey of the surface's chemical elements, China's
  **Chang'e 1** made a full map, and India's **Chandrayaan-1** went into orbit and dropped an impact probe.
- **2013, Chang'e 3** (China) landed with the rover **Yutu**, "Jade Rabbit", the first soft landing since 1976.
- **2019, Chang'e 4** (China) made the first landing on the **far side**. Israel's privately built Beresheet crashed
  the same year.
- **2020, Chang'e 5** (China) brought back 1,731 g, the first Moon samples returned since 1976.
- **2023, Chandrayaan-3** (India) landed on 23 August near the south pole, at 69° S, making India the fourth country
  to land on the Moon and the first to land near its south pole.
- **2024.** Japan's **SLIM** landed on 19 January, aiming within 100 m of its target; it came to rest on its side but
  made Japan the fifth country to land. In February the American **IM-1** became the first commercially built lander
  on the Moon. China's **Chang'e 6** landed on the far side on 1 June, inside the South Pole–Aitken basin, and
  brought back **1,935 g**, the first rock from the far side; Pakistan's small orbiter ICUBE-Q rode along with it.
- **2026, Artemis II** (United States, with Canada). From 1 to 10 April, Reid Wiseman, Victor Glover, Christina Koch
  and the Canadian astronaut Jeremy Hansen flew round the Moon in the Orion capsule, the first crew to leave low Earth
  orbit since 1972. At their farthest they were **252,756 miles** (406,771 km) from the Earth, farther than any
  people before them. Koch became the first woman, Glover the first person of colour, and Hansen the first
  non-American to fly round the Moon.

## 7.7 The Moon and Mars's moons { #mars }

| | Phobos | Deimos | Moon |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Size | 26 × 23 × 18 km | 16 × 12 × 10 km | 3,475 km across |
| Distance from the planet's centre | 9,378 km | 23,459 km | 384,400 km |
| In radii of its planet | 2.8 | 6.9 | 60.3 |
| Time to go round | 7.65 hours | 30.3 hours | 27.32 days |
| Mass | 1.06 × 10¹⁶ kg | 2.4 × 10¹⁵ kg | 7.35 × 10²² kg |
| Share of its planet's mass | 1 in 60 million | 1 in 270 million | 1 in 81 |
| Density | 1,900 kg/m³ | 1,750 kg/m³ | 3,344 kg/m³ |
| Moving | inwards | outwards | outwards, 3.8 cm a year |

Mars's moons are tiny, dark lumps. Phobos is a 26 km potato; the Moon weighs about **7 million times** as much. Both
of Mars's moons reflect only 7 to 8% of the light that falls on them, darker than the Moon's 12%, and they are much
less dense. They also hug their planet: Phobos is less than three Mars radii from the centre, while the Moon is 60
Earth radii out (Figure B).

That closeness decides their fate. Phobos goes round faster than Mars turns, so the tide it raises drags it inwards,
and one day it will be torn apart. Deimos and the Moon go round more slowly than their planets turn, so they
drift outwards (book 3, [section 8.7](../orbits/08-Tides.md#mars)).

Where Mars's moons came from is still argued over: they may be captured asteroids, or they may have gathered from
rubble thrown up by a giant impact on Mars (book 3, [section 19.2](../orbits/19-Orbits-at-Mars.md#origin)). If it
was an impact, Mars's moons and ours were born the same way, and the difference is just in the size of the crash.
Japan's **MMX** probe, launched from Tanegashima on **20 October 2026** (Japanese time), is due at Mars next summer.
It is to be the first spacecraft to orbit Phobos, to collect samples from its surface and to return from the Mars
system, which should settle the question.

The Moon is also practice for Mars. NASA says that Artemis is meant partly to prepare for future human missions to
Mars, and the Moon's dust, cold nights, radiation and water ice are rehearsals for what crews will meet there.

## 7.8 Working it out { #working }

**The Moon's gravity.** With $GM = 4.90 \times 10^{12}$ m³/s² and $R = 1.7374 \times 10^6$ m,

$$g = \frac{GM}{R^2} = \frac{4.90 \times 10^{12}}{(1.7374 \times 10^6)^2} = 1.62 \text{ m/s}^2,$$

which is $1.62/9.82 = 0.165$ of the Earth's, about a sixth. The escape speed is

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R}} = \sqrt{2 \times 2.82 \times 10^6} = 2{,}380 \text{ m/s},$$

less than a quarter of the Earth's 11.2 km/s. That is one reason the Moon could not hold on to air.

**A message to the Moon.** At 384,400 km, light takes $384{,}400/299{,}792 = 1.28$ s each way, so a laser pulse
fired at a mirror on the Moon comes back after about 2.6 s.

**Artemis II's record.** 252,756 miles × 1.609 km = 406,771 km, a little farther than the Moon's average distance,
because the crew swung round behind the Moon.

**The maria.** 16% of the Moon's 38 million km² is about **6 million km²**, more than half the area of Canada.

**Apollo against the robots.** Apollo brought back 381 kg and Chang'e 6 1.935 kg, so Apollo brought about $381/1.935
= 200$ times as much. All the Moon rock ever brought back is about 385 kg, not counting meteorites from the Moon.

**Shares of mass.** The Moon has $0.07346/5.9724 = 0.0123$ of the Earth's mass, 1 part in 81. Phobos has $1.06 \times
10^{16}/6.42 \times 10^{23} = 1.65 \times 10^{-8}$ of Mars's, 1 part in 60 million.

      [Earth](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/earthfact.html) Fact Sheets (above). Phobos's mass and
      Mars's mass: NASA, Mars Fact Sheet (above).
    - The surface area of 38 million km²: Wikipedia, Moon (above). Canada's area, just under 10 million km²: standard.
    - The speed of light, $g = GM/R^2$ and $v = \sqrt{2GM/R}$: standard. Everything else: our arithmetic.

## 7.9 Heat from coming together { #derive }

When a world gathers itself together, its pieces fall in and speed up, and when they hit they turn that energy into
heat. How much heat is there?

Build a ball of uniform density $\rho$ one shell at a time. When it has grown to radius $r$, its mass is $m =
\tfrac43\pi\rho r^3$. A new shell of thickness $dr$ has mass $dm = 4\pi\rho r^2\,dr$, and falling from far away onto
the surface it releases energy $Gm\,dm/r$. Adding up all the shells from $r = 0$ to $R$:

$$E = \int_0^R \frac{G}{r}\left(\tfrac43\pi\rho r^3\right)\left(4\pi\rho r^2\right)dr = \frac{16\pi^2 G\rho^2}{3}
\cdot\frac{R^5}{5} = \frac{3}{5}\,\frac{GM^2}{R}.$$

So each kilogram gets $\tfrac35\,GM/R$. If none of it escaped, rock with specific heat $c \approx 1{,}000$ J/(kg·K)
would warm by

$$\Delta T = \frac{3}{5}\,\frac{GM}{Rc}.$$

| | Moon | Mars | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $GM/R$ | 2.82 × 10⁶ J/kg | 1.26 × 10⁷ J/kg | 6.26 × 10⁷ J/kg |
| $\tfrac35\,GM/R$ | 1.69 × 10⁶ J/kg | 7.6 × 10⁶ J/kg | 3.75 × 10⁷ J/kg |
| $\Delta T$ if none escapes | 1,700 K | 7,600 K | 37,500 K |

Rock starts to melt at roughly 1,300 to 1,500 K, so even the small Moon gets enough heat to melt much of itself, if
it is built fast enough that the heat cannot leak away. The Moon seems to have been: models of the giant impact have
it gathering from the debris in about **a month to a year**, which is why it started with a magma ocean (section
7.4). The Earth and Mars got far more than enough to melt. Real worlds lose some of this heat as they grow, and
melting itself soaks up energy, so these numbers are upper limits, but they show why young rocky worlds were molten.

The same sum, $\tfrac35\,GM^2/R$, is the energy you would need to take a world apart again. For the Earth it is
about $0.6 \times 3.986 \times 10^{14}
\times 5.97 \times 10^{24}/(6.371 \times 10^6) = 2.2 \times 10^{32}$ J.

    - The uniform-sphere energy $\tfrac35\,GM^2/R$ and the shell-by-shell sum: standard.
    - $GM$ and the radii: NASA, Moon, Mars and Earth Fact Sheets (above). For Mars, $GM = 42{,}828$ km³/s² and
      $R = 3{,}389.5$ km.
    - $c \approx 1{,}000$ J/(kg·K) and rock melting at roughly 1,300 to 1,500 K: standard.
    - The Moon gathering in about a month to a year, trapping its heat: Wikipedia, Lunar magma ocean (above).
    - Everything else: our arithmetic.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) How wide is the Moon compared with the Earth, and how strong is its gravity?

??? note "Answer"
    About 27% of the Earth's width (3,475 km), with about a sixth of the Earth's gravity (1.62 m/s²).

**2.** (beginner) What are the dark patches on the Moon made of?

??? note "Answer"
    Basalt, dark iron-rich lava that flooded old impact basins. They are called maria, "seas".

**3.** (beginner) Which mission first landed on the far side of the Moon, and when?

??? note "Answer"
    China's Chang'e 4, in early 2019.

**4.** (intermediate) Why are the maria thought to be younger than the highlands?

??? note "Answer"
    They have far fewer craters in each square kilometre, so they have been exposed to impacts for less time. Dated
    Apollo rocks agree: mare basalts from about 3.16 billion years, highland rocks up to 4.44 billion.

**5.** (intermediate) Give two clues that support the giant-impact idea for the Moon's origin.

??? note "Answer"
    Any two of: lunar and Earth rocks have identical stable isotope ratios; the Moon has only a small iron core; it
    is short of substances that boil off easily; the Earth–Moon system has a lot of spin and orbital motion.

**6.** (intermediate) Why can there be ice on the Moon when its days reach 117 °C?

??? note "Answer"
    The Moon's axis is barely tilted, so the floors of some craters near the poles never get sunlight. They stay
    cold enough to keep ice.

**7.** (advanced) The Moon is 384,400 km away. How long does a laser pulse take to get there and back?

??? note "Answer"
    $2 \times 384{,}400/299{,}792 = 2.56$ s, about two and a half seconds.

**8.** (expert) Using $\Delta T = \tfrac35\,GM/(Rc)$ with $c = 1{,}000$ J/(kg·K), how much would Mercury warm as it
formed? Take $GM = 2.203 \times 10^{13}$ m³/s² and $R = 2.440 \times 10^6$ m.

??? note "Answer"
    $GM/R = 9.03 \times 10^6$ J/kg, so $\Delta T = 0.6 \times 9.03 \times 10^6/1{,}000 = 5{,}400$ K, if none of the
    heat escaped.

## Summary

- The Moon is 3,475 km wide, with 1.2% of the Earth's mass and a sixth of its gravity, and it is drifting away.
- Its dark maria are basalt lava, mostly on the near side; its bright highlands are anorthosite.
- Crater counts, checked against dated rocks from Apollo, Luna and Chang'e, give ages for whole landscapes.
- It probably formed from the debris of a giant impact about 4.5 billion years ago and began with a magma ocean.
- Ice hides in craters near the poles that never see the Sun.
- The Soviet Union, the United States, Japan, Europe, China, India and companies have all reached it; Artemis II
  took four people round it in April 2026.
- Phobos and Deimos are tiny and close to Mars; Phobos is falling in, while Deimos and the Moon drift out.
