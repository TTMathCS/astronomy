# 1. A tour of the solar system

*The solar system is the Sun and everything held by its pull: eight planets, hundreds of moons, more than a million
known asteroids, thousands of comets, and a cloud of icy bodies reaching a good part of the way to the next star.
This first chapter is a quick tour. It shows how big things are, how far apart, which kinds of world there are, what
counts as a planet, and where Mars fits in.*

## 1.1 The Sun and its family { #family }

The solar system formed about **4.6 billion years ago** from a collapsing cloud of gas and dust (chapter 2). Almost
all of that cloud ended up in the middle, as the **Sun**. Today the Sun holds **99.86%** of the mass of the whole
solar system. Everything else, every planet, moon, asteroid and comet, shares the last 0.14%.

Of that small share, most is in **Jupiter**, which has more than twice the mass of all the other planets put
together. Then come Saturn, Neptune and Uranus. The four rocky planets, Mercury, Venus, the Earth and Mars, together
have less than 1% of the planets' mass.

Going out from the Sun, the family is:

- **Four rocky planets:** Mercury, Venus, the Earth and Mars.
- **The asteroid belt,** a ring of rocky rubble between Mars and Jupiter.
- **Four giant planets:** Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus and Neptune.
- **The Kuiper belt,** a ring of icy bodies beyond Neptune, home of Pluto.
- **The Oort cloud,** a vast, unseen shell of icy bodies, where many comets come from.

## 1.3 How far { #distances }

Distances in the solar system are so large that astronomers use their own unit, the **astronomical unit** (AU), the
Earth's average distance from the Sun, about **150 million km** (book 3, chapter 12). Light covers 1 AU in about
8.3 minutes, so we see the Sun as it was 8.3 minutes ago.

Mars is at 1.52 AU, half as far again as the Earth: sunlight takes 12.7 minutes to reach it. Jupiter is at 5.2 AU,
Saturn at 9.6, Uranus at 19.2 and Neptune at 30.2. Sunlight takes over 4 hours to reach Neptune.

## 1.4 Two kinds of planet { #kinds }

The two sizes go with two makeups.

- **Rocky planets** are made of rock and iron. They are dense: 3,900 to 5,500 kg/m³, four to five and a half times
  as dense as water. They have solid surfaces, few or no moons and no rings.
- **Giant planets** are mostly hydrogen and helium (Jupiter and Saturn) or ices of water, ammonia and methane
  (Uranus and Neptune) round small rocky cores. They have no solid surface to stand on. Their densities are low,
  690 to 1,640 kg/m³; Saturn's average is less than water's. Every one has rings and many moons.

Why the split? Close to the young Sun it was too warm for water to freeze, so only rock and metal could gather into
planets, and there was not much of them. Farther out, beyond the **snow line**, ice joined the rock, giving big cores
that could pull in gas from the cloud. Chapter 2 tells the story and chapter 3 compares the two kinds in detail.

??? note "Sources"
    - Densities, rings, moons and magnetic fields: NASA, Planetary Fact Sheet (above).
    - What the planets are made of, and the snow line: standard; chapters 2 and 3 give sources. Saturn's density being
      below water's (1,000 kg/m³): our comparison with the fact sheet's 687 kg/m³.

## 1.5 Moons, rings and small bodies { #small }

**Moons.** NASA's fact sheet, last updated in March 2025, lists one moon for the Earth, two for Mars, **95** for
Jupiter, **274** for Saturn, 28 for Uranus and 16 for Neptune. Mercury and Venus have none. New small moons of the
giants are found most years, so these numbers keep growing.

**Rings.** All four giants have rings. Saturn's are bright and wide; the others' are dark and thin. No rocky planet
has a ring today.

**Asteroids and comets.** The Minor Planet Center, which keeps the world's list, counted about **1.58 million**
minor planets (mostly asteroids) and about **4,600 comets** by November 2026. Over 43,000 of the minor planets are
**near-Earth objects**, whose orbits come close to ours.

**The far edge.** Beyond Neptune, the **Kuiper belt** runs from about 30 to 50 AU. Much farther out, the **Oort
cloud** is thought to stretch from about 2,000 to 200,000 AU, over three light-years. The Dutch astronomer Jan Oort
proposed it in 1950 to explain where long-period comets come from. Nobody has seen an object in the Oort cloud
itself.

## 1.6 What is a planet? { #planet }

**The wanderers.** For thousands of years people all over the world knew five planets, the bright "stars" that wander
among the others: Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn (book 1,
[section 13.1](../night-sky/13-Finding-the-Planets.md#wanderers)). In China, Mars was *Huoxing*, the Fire Star
(history book, [section 4.8](../history/04-China.md#mars)); in India it was *Mangala*
([section 5.6](../history/05-India.md#brahmagupta)).

**New worlds.** William Herschel found Uranus in 1781 (history book,
[section 17.1](../history/17-Herschel-and-the-Canals-of-Mars.md#herschel)). Ceres, found in 1801, was at first
called a planet, until many more like it turned up and it became the first known asteroid (history book,
[section 17.4](../history/17-Herschel-and-the-Canals-of-Mars.md#rule)). Neptune was found in 1846 from its pull on
Uranus (book 3, [section 10.3](../orbits/10-Wobbling-Orbits-and-the-Finding-of-Neptune.md#neptune)). On 18 February
1930 Clyde Tombaugh, an American astronomer at the Lowell Observatory, found **Pluto**, and it was counted as the
ninth planet.

**The 2006 rules.** Pluto turned out to be small, and from the 1990s many more icy bodies were found near it, one of
them, Eris, about as large. So in August 2006 the International Astronomical Union voted on what a planet is. A
**planet** is a body that

1. goes round the Sun,
2. is massive enough for its own gravity to pull it nearly round, and
3. has **cleared the neighbourhood** round its orbit.

A body that meets the first two but not the third, and is not a moon, is a **dwarf planet**. Pluto shares its zone
with many Kuiper belt objects, so it became a dwarf planet. Astronomers generally agree that at least nine bodies are
dwarf planets: Pluto, Eris, Haumea, Makemake, Gonggong, Quaoar, Sedna, Ceres and Orcus. Ceres is the only one in the
asteroid belt; the rest are out beyond Neptune.

## 1.7 Mars in the family { #mars }

Mars is the **fourth planet** from the Sun and the outermost rocky one. Beyond it lie the asteroid belt and then
Jupiter.

| | Mars | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Distance from the Sun | 228 million km, 1.52 AU | 150 million km, 1 AU |
| Width | 6,792 km | 12,756 km |
| Mass | 0.107 Earths | 1 |
| Density | 3,934 kg/m³ | 5,514 kg/m³ |
| Gravity at the surface | 3.7 m/s² (38%) | 9.8 m/s² |
| Length of day | 24.7 hours | 24.0 hours |
| Length of year | 687 days | 365 days |
| Moons | 2 | 1 |
| Global magnetic field | no | yes |
| Mean temperature | −65 °C | 15 °C |

So Mars is a small rocky planet, with about a tenth of the Earth's mass and the lowest density of the four. Its day
is almost the same as ours, its year nearly twice as long. It has thin air, no global magnetic field and two tiny
moons. A radio message between the Earth and Mars takes from about **3 to 22 minutes** each way, depending on where
the two planets are in their orbits.

Each chapter of this book ends its tour of a world by comparing it with Mars. The next book tells Mars's own story.

## 1.8 Working it out { #working }

**Light times.** Light travels 299,792 km every second. For Mars, at 228.0 million km:

$$t = \frac{228.0 \times 10^6}{299{,}792} = 761 \text{ s} = 12.7 \text{ minutes}.$$

For Neptune, at 4,515 million km: 15,060 s, 4.2 hours. Between the Earth and Mars the distance runs from about
$206.7 - 152.1 = 54.6$ million km, when Mars is near perihelion and the Earth near aphelion on the same side, to about
$249.3 + 152.1 = 401.4$ million km on opposite sides: **3.0 to 22.3 minutes**.

**A scale model.** Divide every size and distance by the Sun's width, 1,391,400 km, and multiply by 1 m.

| | Ball | Distance from the Sun |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Earth | 9.2 mm | 108 m |
| Mars | 4.9 mm | 164 m |
| Jupiter | 103 mm | 560 m |
| Neptune | 36 mm | 3.2 km |
| Alpha Centauri | | about 30,000 km |

For Alpha Centauri, 4.4 light-years is $4.4 \times 9.46 \times 10^{12} = 4.2 \times 10^{13}$ km, and
$4.2 \times 10^{13} / 1{,}391{,}400 = 3.0 \times 10^7$ m.

**Shares of the mass.** The planets add up to $2{,}667 \times 10^{24}$ kg; the Sun is $1{,}988{,}400 \times
10^{24}$ kg, 746 times as much. So the planets are $1/747 = 0.13\%$ of the total. Jupiter is $1{,}898/2{,}667 = 71\%$
of the planets; the four rocky planets, $11.8 \times 10^{24}$ kg, are 0.4%.

**Kepler's third law as a check.** In years and AU, $T^2 = a^3$ (book 3,
[section 3.4](../orbits/03-Keplers-Three-Laws.md#third)). For Mars, $1.524^{1.5} = 1.88$ years, 687 days, as in the
table.

??? note "Sources"
    - Masses, distances and sizes: NASA, Planetary Fact Sheet and Sun Fact Sheet (above). The speed of light: standard.
    - Everything else: our arithmetic.

## 1.9 Where the spin is { #derive }

The Sun has 99.86% of the mass. You might expect it to have most of the solar system's **spin**, its angular
momentum, too. It does not.

**A planet's orbit.** For a body of mass $m$ on an orbit with semi-major axis $a$ and eccentricity $e$ round a star
with $GM$, the angular momentum is

$$L = m\sqrt{GM\,a\,(1 - e^2)}.$$

For Jupiter, $m = 1.898 \times 10^{27}$ kg, $a = 7.785 \times 10^{11}$ m, $e = 0.049$, and $GM = 1.327 \times
10^{20}$ m³/s²: $L = 1.93 \times 10^{43}$ kg m²/s.

**The Sun's spin.** For a spinning ball, $L = I\omega$ with $I = 0.070\,MR^2$ for the Sun, which is much denser in
the middle. With $M = 1.988 \times 10^{30}$ kg, $R = 6.957 \times 10^8$ m and one turn in 609 hours,
$\omega = 2.87 \times 10^{-6}$ rad/s, and $L = 1.93 \times 10^{41}$ kg m²/s, a hundredth of Jupiter's.

**The shares.** Adding all eight planets gives $3.13 \times 10^{43}$:

| | Share of the angular momentum |
| --- | --- |
| Jupiter | 61% |
| Saturn | 25% |
| Neptune | 8% |
| Uranus | 5% |
| The Sun's spin | 0.6% |
| Earth | 0.08% |
| Mars | 0.01% |

Our simple sum gives the Sun under 1%; Wikipedia, using other assumptions, says about 2%. Either way, the Sun has
nearly all the mass and almost none of the spin. A collapsing cloud spins faster as it shrinks, like a skater
pulling in their arms, so a young Sun should have spun very fast. Explaining where its spin went is one of the tests
of any story of how the solar system formed, and chapter 2 takes it up.

??? note "Sources"
    - $L = m\sqrt{GMa(1 - e^2)}$ for an orbit, and $L = I\omega$: standard.
    - The Sun's moment of inertia factor 0.070 and its rotation in 609.12 hours (the adopted value at 16° latitude):
      NASA, Sun Fact Sheet (above). The planets' values: NASA, Planetary Fact Sheet (above).
    - The Sun's share of about 2%, and a collapsing cloud spinning up: Wikipedia, Solar System (above).
    - The shares in the table: our arithmetic. It treats the Sun as turning all at once at the 16° rate.

**2.** (beginner) Name the four rocky planets and the four giant planets, in order from the Sun.

??? note "Answer"
    Rocky: Mercury, Venus, the Earth, Mars. Giants: Jupiter, Saturn, Uranus, Neptune.

**3.** (beginner) How long does sunlight take to reach Mars?

??? note "Answer"
    About 12.7 minutes, against 8.3 minutes for the Earth.

**4.** (intermediate) Give two differences between the rocky planets and the giants.

??? note "Answer"
    Any two: the giants are much bigger; they are much less dense; they are mostly gas or ice instead of rock and
    metal; they have no solid surface; they all have rings and many moons.

**5.** (intermediate) What are the three rules for a planet, and which one does Pluto fail?

??? note "Answer"
    It goes round the Sun, its gravity has pulled it nearly round, and it has cleared the neighbourhood of its
    orbit. Pluto fails the third: it shares its zone with many other Kuiper belt objects.

**6.** (intermediate) Why does a message to Mars sometimes take 3 minutes and sometimes 22?

??? note "Answer"
    The distance between the two planets changes as they go round the Sun, from about 55 million km when they are on
    the same side to about 400 million km when they are on opposite sides.

**7.** (advanced) In the 1 m Sun model, how big is Saturn (120,536 km across) and how far is it (1,432 million km)?

??? note "Answer"
    $120{,}536/1{,}391{,}400 = 0.087$ m, a ball 8.7 cm across, and $1{,}432 \times 10^6 / 1{,}391{,}400 = 1{,}029$ m,
    about 1 km from the Sun.

**8.** (expert) Find the angular momentum of Mars's orbit, with $m = 6.42 \times 10^{23}$ kg, $a = 2.28 \times
10^{11}$ m and $e = 0.094$. How does it compare with the Sun's spin?

??? note "Answer"
    $L = 6.42 \times 10^{23} \times \sqrt{1.327 \times 10^{20} \times 2.28 \times 10^{11} \times 0.991} = 6.42
    \times 10^{23} \times 5.48 \times 10^{15} = 3.5 \times 10^{39}$ kg m²/s, about 1/55 of the Sun's spin.

## Summary

- The Sun holds 99.86% of the solar system's mass; Jupiter has most of the rest.
- There are four small, dense rocky planets and four big, light giants, with the asteroid belt between them, the
  Kuiper belt beyond Neptune and the Oort cloud far beyond that.
- The Sun is 109 Earths wide and Jupiter 11; Mars is half the Earth's width.
- 1 AU is 150 million km, 8.3 light-minutes. Mars is at 1.52 AU, Neptune at 30.
- Hundreds of moons, about 1.58 million minor planets and 4,600 comets are known.
- Since 2006 a planet must also have cleared its neighbourhood, so Pluto and eight or more others are dwarf planets.
- Mars is the outermost rocky planet: a tenth of the Earth's mass, a similar day, a 687-day year and thin air.
- The planets, mostly Jupiter, carry over 99% of the solar system's spin.
