# 12. Jupiter

*Jupiter has more than twice the mass of all the other planets put together. It has no surface to stand on, a storm
wider than the Earth that has raged for nearly two centuries at least, a magnetic field that reaches almost to Saturn,
and more than a hundred moons. People have tracked it across the sky for thousands of years, and nine spacecraft have
flown past or orbited it. This chapter covers its clouds and storms, its history, its heat and magnetism, the missions
sent to it, how it shaped Mars, and how its shape tells us what is inside.*

## 12.1 The giant { #basics }

**Big and heavy.** Jupiter is the fifth planet from the Sun, on average **778 million km** away, 5.2 times the
Earth's distance. Its mass is **318 Earths**, and it is so big that **1,321 Earths** would fit inside it. Yet its
average density is only **1,326 kg/m³**, a quarter of the Earth's, because it is made mostly of hydrogen and helium.
What is inside, from the cloud tops down to a fuzzy core, is in chapter 3
([section 3.5](03-Rocky-Worlds-and-Giant-Worlds.md#gasgiants)).

**No ground.** Jupiter has no solid surface. The gas just gets thicker with depth until it behaves like a liquid. So
heights are measured from the level where the pressure is **1 bar**, about the air pressure at sea level on the
Earth. There the temperature is **165 K** (−108 °C), and gravity is about **2.6 times** the Earth's.

**A fast spin.** Jupiter turns once in **9 hours 55 minutes**, faster than any other planet. Its equator moves at
about **12.6 km/s**, 27 times as fast as the Earth's (section 12.8). The spin flattens it: it is **71,492 km** in
radius at the equator but only 66,854 km at the poles, 6.5% less. You can see the squashed shape in a small
telescope. Its year is **11.86** Earth years, and its axis is tilted only 3°, so it has almost no seasons.

**Too big to grow.** Jupiter is about as wide as a planet can be. Models show that if it had more than about 40% more
mass, its inside would be squeezed so hard that it would get **smaller**, not bigger. It is also slowly shrinking,
by about 1 mm a year, as it cools; when it formed it was about twice as wide.

**In the sky.** At its brightest Jupiter reaches magnitude **−2.94**, brighter than any star; of the planets only
Venus outshines it, and Mars at its closest can match it. Binoculars held steady show its four big moons as tiny stars
in a line, and a small telescope shows the cloud belts. Book 1 explains when and where to look (book 1,
[section 13.4](../night-sky/13-Finding-the-Planets.md#outer)).

## 12.2 Belts, zones and clouds { #clouds }

**Stripes.** Through a telescope Jupiter is striped. The light bands are called **zones** and the dark ones
**belts**. There are about a dozen, parallel to the equator. In the zones, gas rises; it is colder, and ammonia
freezes into white ice clouds. In the belts, gas sinks, and we see deeper, darker layers. What makes the belts brown
and orange is still not certain.

**Jet streams.** Between the bands blow steady winds called **jets**, alternately east and west. The strongest, at
23° north, reaches about **150 m/s**, more than 500 km/h. The Galileo probe found that the winds do not die away
with depth: they were still blowing at 22 bar, far below the clouds.

**Three decks of cloud.** Jupiter's air is about 90% hydrogen and 10% helium by volume, with a little methane,
ammonia, water and other gases. As you go down, it gets warmer, and different gases condense into clouds at
different levels (Figure A):

- **ammonia ice**, the white clouds at the top, at 0.6 to 0.9 bar;
- **ammonium hydrosulfide**, made from ammonia and hydrogen sulfide, at about 1 to 2 bar;
- **water**, as ice and drops, at 3 to 7 bar.

**Lightning.** Jupiter's storms flash with lightning hundreds of times more powerful than on the Earth, mostly in the
belts. It was thought to come from the deep water clouds, but Juno also found **shallow lightning** higher up, in
clouds of ammonia and water mixed. Strong storms may make "**mushballs**", slushy hail of water ice and liquid
ammonia that falls and carries ammonia down to deeper layers.

**Storms at the poles.** Seen from above, Jupiter's poles look nothing like its bands. Juno found a **cluster of
cyclones** at each pole: at the north, one in the centre with eight around it; at the south, one in the centre with
five large ones and one small one around it.

## 12.3 The Great Red Spot { #spot }

**A giant storm.** The **Great Red Spot** is a high-pressure storm 22° south of Jupiter's equator, the largest storm of
its kind in the solar system. It turns anticlockwise once every **4.5 days** or so, with winds at its edge up to
about **430 km/h**, while the middle is almost still. Its cloud tops stand about 8 km above the clouds around it.
Any telescope with a lens or mirror of 12 cm or more can show it.

**How old?** In 1665 Giovanni Cassini saw "a permanent spot" and watched it until 1713. Then there is a gap. The
spot seen today was first recorded in **September 1831**, and Heinrich Schwabe, a pharmacist, drew the first known
picture showing its details that year; it has been watched without a break since 1878. If Cassini's spot was the same
storm, it is more than 360 years old. A study in 2024 of the old records concluded that it probably was not: the
first spot faded and the present one formed later. Even so, it has lasted at least 195 years.

**Shrinking.** A little over a century ago the spot was about **40,000 km** long, three Earths wide. By 2004 it was
half that, and on 3 April 2017 it measured **16,350 km**, 1.3 times the Earth's width. If it keeps shrinking at this
rate, it will be round by about 2040. Nobody knows whether it will disappear.

**How deep?** Juno flew over it on 11 July 2017. Juno's gravity measurements and the way the winds die off with depth
put its bottom about **200 to 500 km** down: deep for a storm on the Earth, but shallow for Jupiter, whose radius is
71,000 km. Its red colour is thought to be a kind of sunburn: chemicals carried high up are changed by sunlight.

**Red Spot Junior.** Three white storms, first seen in 1938, drew together over the decades; two merged in 1998, and
in **March 2000** they took in the third and became one storm, **Oval BA**. It later turned reddish, and is called the
Little Red Spot or "Red Jr."

## 12.4 Jupiter round the world { #history }

Jupiter takes about twelve years to go round the sky, moving roughly one constellation of the zodiac each year. Many
peoples built calendars and stories on that slow, steady walk.

- **Babylon.** Babylonian astronomers were recording Jupiter by the 8th or 7th century BC. To them it was the planet
  of **Marduk**, chief of their gods, and its twelve-year round helped them divide the zodiac. Before 50 BC they
  worked out how far it moved by adding up the area under a graph of its speed, a method later reinvented in Europe
  (book 11, [section 2.11](../history/02-Mesopotamia.md#jupiter)).
- **China.** Chinese astronomers called it the **year star**, *suìxīng*, because it moved one constellation a year.
  The twelve earthly branches, used to count years, and the twelve animals of the East Asian zodiac go back to its
  cycle. The historian **Xi Zezong** argued that the ancient astronomer **Gan De** recorded a small star "in
  alliance" with Jupiter, which may have been one of its moons, seen without a telescope nearly two thousand years
  before Galileo.
- **East Asia.** In China, Japan, Korea and Vietnam it is the **wood star**, after the wood of the five elements.
- **India.** In Vedic astrology it is named after **Brihaspati**, teacher of the gods, and called **Guru**, "the
  teacher".
- **Central Asia.** In Turkic stories it is **Erendiz** or Erentüz. Turkic astronomers put its period at 11 years and
  300 days, about two weeks short of the true value (section 12.8).
- **Greece, Rome and Alexandria.** The Greeks named it after **Zeus** and the Romans after **Jupiter**, the chief
  god. In the 2nd century, in Alexandria, Ptolemy's *Almagest* gave its period as 4,332.38 days, or 11.86 years.

**The telescope.** In January 1610 the Italian polymath **Galileo Galilei** found four moons going round Jupiter, the
first moons seen going round another world. **Simon Marius** found them one day later and published in 1614, and it is
his names that stuck: Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto (book 11,
[section 15.3](../history/15-Galileo-and-the-Telescope.md#jupiter)). In 1665 Cassini measured the spin, about 9 hours
56 minutes, and in 1692 he saw that different bands turn at slightly different rates. By the 1670s Cassini found that
the moons' eclipses came about **17 minutes** later when Jupiter was on the far side of the Sun, and **Ole Rømer**
explained it: light takes time to cross the Earth's orbit (book 11,
[section 16.3](../history/16-Newton-Halley-and-Romer.md#light)). In **1892** E. E. Barnard, at Lick Observatory in
California, found a fifth moon, Amalthea, the last moon of any planet found by eye at a telescope.

**New kinds of light.** In 1932 Rupert Wildt found ammonia and methane in Jupiter's spectrum. In **1955** Bernard
Burke and Kenneth Franklin picked up bursts of radio waves from Jupiter, which repeated with its spin: the first sign
of its magnetic field (section 12.5).

**A comet hits.** In July 1992 Comet Shoemaker–Levy 9 passed so close to Jupiter that it was torn apart (book 3,
[section 8.6](../orbits/08-Tides.md#locking)). Between **16 and 22 July 1994** its pieces, up to 2 km wide, hit
Jupiter's southern half at about **60 km/s**. Observatories round the world, the Hubble Space Telescope and the
Galileo spacecraft watched. The fireballs reached about 24,000 K, plumes rose more than 3,000 km, and the dark scars
were easier to see than the Great Red Spot. It was the first collision of two solar system bodies ever watched
(chapter 11).

## 12.5 Heat, magnetism, moons and rings { #magnet }

**More heat out than in.** Jupiter gives out more heat than it gets from the Sun. It is still slowly shrinking, and
as it shrinks, gravity squeezes the gas and warms it, the way a bicycle pump gets warm. This inner heat helps drive
the weather.

**The biggest magnet.** Deep inside, at more than about 10,000 km down, hydrogen is squeezed until it conducts
electricity like a metal (chapter 3). Currents in this **metallic hydrogen** make Jupiter's magnetic field, about
**20 times** as strong as the Earth's at the cloud tops, with a total strength, its magnetic moment, about **20,000
times** the Earth's. Pioneer 10 measured it directly in 1973.

**The magnetosphere.** The field carves out a bubble in the solar wind, the **magnetosphere**. Towards the Sun it
reaches up to **7 million km**; away from the Sun it stretches almost to the orbit of Saturn. If you could see it, it
would look bigger in our sky than the full Moon. It is the largest structure in the solar system after the Sun's own
bubble, the heliosphere (chapter 18).

**Fed by a moon.** Io's volcanoes throw sulfur dioxide into space, up to about **1,000 kg every second**. Jupiter's
field strips the atoms of electrons and drags them round with the planet's spin, making a ring of charged gas, the
**Io plasma torus**, along Io's orbit. The field is stretched into a flat disc by this load. Jupiter's magnetosphere
is mostly powered by its own spin and by Io, not by the solar wind as the Earth's is. Currents along the field make
permanent **auroras** round both poles, and the intense radio bursts that Burke and Franklin heard.

**Moons.** Wikipedia counted **115** known moons in 2026, of which only 16 are wider than 10 km; NASA's fact sheet,
last updated in October 2024, gives 95. Most are small and far out, many going round backwards. The four big
**Galilean moons** are worlds in their own right: Ganymede is larger than the planet Mercury, and Io is the most
volcanic place known. They are the subject of chapter 15, and the 1:2:4 rhythm of Io, Europa and Ganymede is in book 3
([section 11.4](../orbits/11-Resonances.md#io)).

**Rings.** Jupiter has faint rings of dust, found by Voyager 1 in 1979, the third ring system known after Saturn's and
Uranus's. There is a thick inner **halo**, a thin **main ring**, and two faint **gossamer rings** made of dust knocked
off the small moons Amalthea and Thebe.

## 12.6 Visitors { #missions }

**Flybys.** Jupiter is the most visited of the outer planets.

| Spacecraft | Closest approach | What it found |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pioneer 10 | 3 December 1973 | first close-up pictures, the magnetosphere |
| Pioneer 11 | 4 December 1974 | came within 34,000 km, then went on to Saturn |
| Voyager 1 | 5 March 1979 | volcanoes on Io, the rings, new moons |
| Voyager 2 | 9 July 1979 | more of the moons, ice on Europa |
| Ulysses | 8 February 1992, 4 February 2004 | the magnetosphere, on the way to the Sun's poles |
| Cassini | 30 December 2000 | pictures on the way to Saturn |
| New Horizons | 28 February 2007 | a boost on the way to Pluto |

Many of these used Jupiter's gravity to fling themselves on to other places (book 3,
[section 16.3](../orbits/16-Gravity-Assists.md#tour)). The volcanoes of Io, found by the Voyagers, were the first
active volcanoes seen anywhere beyond the Earth.

**Galileo.** NASA's Galileo was the first spacecraft to orbit Jupiter, arriving on **7 December 1995**. The same day
its 340 kg **probe** dived into the atmosphere, parachuted through 150 km of it and sent data for **57.6 minutes**,
until the pressure reached about 22 bar and the temperature 153 °C. The orbiter made 35 orbits over almost eight
years, flew close to all four big moons, found hints of liquid water under the surfaces of some of them, and
watched Shoemaker–Levy 9 hit. On **21 September 2003** it was steered into Jupiter on purpose, so that it could never
crash onto Europa and bring microbes from the Earth to a moon that might have life.

**Juno.** NASA's Juno was launched on 5 August 2011 and went into a **polar orbit** on 5 July 2016 (4 July in North
American time). Passing low over the poles keeps it out of the worst of the radiation belts. It found the fuzzy core,
the polar cyclones and the shallow lightning, and from 2021 flew past Ganymede, Europa and Io. Its extended mission
ran to September 2025, and its future then looked uncertain because of budget cuts, but it kept working: on **1 May
2026** it passed about 5,000 km from the small moon Thebe and photographed it.

**On the way.** Two more spacecraft are heading to Jupiter's moons. ESA's **JUICE**, launched on 14 April 2023, is
due in 2031 and will end up orbiting Ganymede. NASA's **Europa Clipper**, launched on 14 October 2024, swung past
Mars in March 2025 and is due in April 2030 to study Europa. China plans **Tianwen-4**, an orbiter for Jupiter and its
moon Callisto; sources give launch dates from about 2029 to 2035.

**Jupiter made Mars small.** Mars has only a tenth of the Earth's mass, and Jupiter is the likely reason. In the
Grand Tack idea, the young Jupiter drifted in towards the Sun and back out, clearing away much of the rock that
might have built a bigger Mars (chapter 2, [section 2.7](02-How-the-Solar-System-Formed.md#mars)).

**Jupiter aims rocks at Mars.** Jupiter's pull clears gaps in the asteroid belt and pushes asteroids onto paths that
cross Mars's orbit (chapter 9, [section 9.3](09-The-Asteroid-Belt-and-Ceres.md#gaps)). Mars, being next door to the
belt, is hit more often than the Moon (chapter 11).

**A bulge too big.** Spin flattens a planet, and Figure B in section 12.9 shows how much. Jupiter and the Earth fit
the rule for a fluid planet. Mars does not: it is flatter than even a planet of even density could be at its spin.
The extra bulge is held up by rock, mostly the huge volcanic rise of **Tharsis** (chapter 8,
[section 8.3](08-Mars-among-the-Planets.md#features)). So for Mars, the shape cannot tell us how the mass is packed
inside. Instead, its moment of inertia, **0.366**, was found by timing radio signals from landers and orbiters to see
how slowly its spin axis wobbles. A uniform ball would give 0.4, so Mars has a dense core, but less concentrated than
the Earth's (0.331) or Jupiter's (0.254).

**Jupiter seen from Mars.** Mars is closer to Jupiter, so at opposition Jupiter looks about 30% brighter from Mars
than from the Earth (section 12.8). Book 1 describes the planets in Mars's sky (book 1,
[section 21.5](../night-sky/21-The-Night-Sky-from-Mars.md#planets)).

**Mars helps the way to Jupiter.** NASA's Europa Clipper swung past Mars on 1 March 2025 to gain the speed it needed
(book 3, [section 16.7](../orbits/16-Gravity-Assists.md#mars)).

## 12.8 Working it out { #working }

**Density.** Jupiter's mass of $1.898 \times 10^{27}$ kg in a ball of mean radius 69,911 km gives

$$\rho = \frac{M}{\tfrac43 \pi R^3} = \frac{1.898 \times 10^{27}}{\tfrac43 \pi \times (6.991 \times 10^7)^3} = 1{,}326
\text{ kg/m}^3.$$

**Speed of the equator.** In one turn of 9.925 hours the equator travels $2\pi \times 71{,}492 = 449{,}200$ km, so

$$v = \frac{449{,}200 \text{ km}}{9.925 \times 3{,}600 \text{ s}} = 12.6 \text{ km/s}.$$

The Earth's equator moves at 0.465 km/s, 27 times slower.

**Flattening.** $(71{,}492 - 66{,}854)/71{,}492 = 4{,}638/71{,}492 = 0.0649$. The poles are 4,638 km closer to the
centre than the equator, more than a third of the Earth's width.

**The year from Kepler.** Kepler's third law, with the distance in AU and the period in years, gives
$T = a^{3/2} = 5.204^{1.5} = 11.87$ years, against the measured 11.86. The Turkic value of 11 years 300 days is
$11 + 300/365.25 = 11.82$ years, 15 days short.

**Sunlight.** At 5.204 AU the sunlight is $1/5.204^2 = 1/27$ as strong as at the Earth: 50 W/m² against 1,361 W/m².

**Rømer's 17 minutes.** Between Jupiter seen across the Earth's orbit and seen from the near side, light has an extra
2 AU to travel: $2 \times 1.496 \times 10^{11} \text{ m} / 3.00 \times 10^8 \text{ m/s} = 997$ s, or 16.6 minutes.

**The Red Spot in Earths.** The Earth is 12,742 km wide. Then $40{,}000/12{,}742 = 3.1$ and
$16{,}350/12{,}742 = 1.3$.

**Jupiter from Mars.** At opposition Jupiter is about $5.20 - 1.52 = 3.68$ AU from Mars but $5.20 - 1.00 = 4.20$ AU
from the Earth. Brightness falls as the square of the distance, so from Mars it is
$(4.20/3.68)^2 = 1.30$ times as bright, about 0.3 magnitudes brighter.

## 12.9 How spin flattens a planet { #derive }

Why is Jupiter flattened by 6.5% and the Earth by only 0.3%? And what does the amount tell us about the inside?

**The spin number.** Compare the pull outwards from the spin at the equator, $\omega^2 R$, with gravity,
$GM/R^2$. Their ratio is

$$q = \frac{\omega^2 R^3}{GM}.$$

For Jupiter, $\omega = 2\pi/(9.925 \times 3{,}600) = 1.759 \times 10^{-4}$ per second, $R = 7.149 \times 10^7$ m and
$GM = 1.267 \times 10^{17}$ m³/s², so $q = 0.0892$: the spin cancels almost 9% of gravity at the equator. For the
Earth $q = 0.00346$.

**All the mass at the centre.** Suppose the planet's mass is all at its centre and the outside is a fluid. A fluid
surface settles where the energy per kilogram, gravity plus spin, is the same everywhere. At a distance $r$ from the
centre, at the equator, that energy is $-GM/r - \tfrac12 \omega^2 r^2$; at the pole there is no spin term. Setting the
equator, radius $R_e$, equal to the pole, radius $R_p$:

$$\frac{GM}{R_e} + \tfrac12 \omega^2 R_e^2 = \frac{GM}{R_p}.$$

Rearranged, $GM (R_e - R_p)/(R_e R_p) = \tfrac12 \omega^2 R_e^2$. With $R_p \approx R_e$, the **flattening**
$f = (R_e - R_p)/R_e$ is

$$f = \frac{\omega^2 R_e^3}{2GM} = \frac{q}{2}.$$

**Even density.** If instead the mass is spread evenly, the bulge itself pulls outwards, and the planet flattens more.
The full calculation, done by Colin Maclaurin in 1742, gives, for small spin,

$$f = \frac54 q.$$

These are the two lines in Figure B. Real planets, denser in the middle but not points, must lie between them.

*Figure B. Flattening against spin for six planets.*

**Huygens and Newton.** These two limits are old. Christiaan Huygens took the first one and predicted a flattening
of 1/578 for the Earth, which is $q/2$. Isaac Newton took the second and predicted 1/230, which is $5q/4$. The Earth's
real flattening is 1/298, in between, which is how we know the Earth is denser in the middle.

**Reading the inside.** How far a planet sits between the lines measures how concentrated its mass is. The
**moment of inertia factor**, $C/(MR^2)$, says the same thing in another way: 0.4 for an even ball, less for one with
a dense middle. For a fluid planet the two are linked by the **Darwin–Radau equation**:

$$\frac{C}{MR^2} \approx \frac23 \left(1 - \frac25 \sqrt{\frac{5q}{2f} - 1}\right).$$

Try it on Jupiter. $5q/(2f) = 5 \times 0.0892/(2 \times 0.06487) = 3.437$, so the square root is $\sqrt{2.437} = 1.561$,
and

$$\frac{C}{MR^2} \approx \frac23 (1 - 0.4 \times 1.561) = \frac23 \times 0.376 = 0.250.$$

NASA's fact sheet gives **0.254**. So, from a stopwatch and a ruler, the spin and the shape, we
learn that Jupiter's mass is strongly packed towards its centre. The same sum gives 0.209 for Saturn, against 0.210
measured, and 0.331 for the Earth, against 0.3308.

**Where it fails.** For Mars, $5q/(2f) = 1.95$, which gives 0.407, more than an even ball could have. The answer is
impossible because the formula assumes a fluid shape, and Mars's bulge is partly held up by rock (section 12.7).

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Jupiter has 318 times the Earth's mass and 1,321 times its volume. Why is its density so low?

??? note "Answer"
    It is made mostly of hydrogen and helium, the lightest gases, so its mass is spread through a huge volume. Its
    density is 1,326 kg/m³, a quarter of the Earth's.

**2.** (beginner) What is the Great Red Spot, and how wide is it now?

??? note "Answer"
    A giant high-pressure storm 22° south of the equator. In 2017 it was 16,350 km wide, about 1.3 times the Earth's
    width, and shrinking.

**3.** (beginner) What did Galileo see around Jupiter in 1610, and why did it matter?

??? note "Answer"
    Four moons going round it. It was the first time anything was seen going round another world, which showed that
    not everything goes round the Earth.

**4.** (intermediate) Why are Jupiter's zones lighter than its belts?

??? note "Answer"
    In the zones gas rises and cools, and ammonia freezes into white ice clouds high up. In the belts gas sinks, the
    high clouds clear, and we see deeper, darker layers.

**5.** (intermediate) Where do the sulfur and oxygen in Jupiter's magnetosphere come from?

??? note "Answer"
    From the volcanoes of Io, which throw out sulfur dioxide, up to about a tonne a second. Jupiter's field breaks
    it into charged atoms and drags them round as the Io plasma torus.

**6.** (intermediate) Why was the Galileo spacecraft crashed into Jupiter on purpose in 2003?

??? note "Answer"
    So that it could never hit Europa and carry microbes from the Earth to a moon that might have an ocean and life.

**7.** (advanced) Saturn turns once in 10.656 hours, its equatorial radius is 60,268 km and its
$GM = 3.7931 \times 10^{16}$ m³/s². Find $q$, and the flattening for the two limits. Its real flattening is 0.098.

??? note "Answer"
    $\omega = 2\pi/38{,}362 = 1.638 \times 10^{-4}$ per second, so
    $q = (1.638 \times 10^{-4})^2 \times (6.027 \times 10^7)^3 / 3.793 \times 10^{16} = 0.155$. The limits are
    $q/2 = 0.077$ and $5q/4 = 0.194$. The real 0.098 is between them, near the lower one.

**8.** (expert) Use the Darwin–Radau equation to estimate Saturn's moment of inertia factor from question 7. What does
it say about Saturn compared with Jupiter?

??? note "Answer"
    $5q/(2f) = 5 \times 0.155/(2 \times 0.098) = 3.95$, so $\sqrt{2.95} = 1.72$ and
    $C/(MR^2) = \tfrac23 (1 - 0.4 \times 1.72) = 0.208$. NASA gives 0.210. It is lower than Jupiter's 0.254, so
    Saturn's mass is even more packed towards its centre.

## Summary

- Jupiter has 318 Earth masses, a quarter of the Earth's density, a 9 h 55 min day and no solid surface.
- Light zones of rising gas and dark belts of sinking gas are divided by jets up to 150 m/s; clouds of ammonia,
  ammonium hydrosulfide and water lie at different depths.
- The Great Red Spot has been watched since 1831 and is shrinking; it is about 1.3 Earths wide and a few hundred
  kilometres deep.
- Babylon, China, India, Central Asia and Alexandria all followed Jupiter's twelve-year round; Galileo and Marius found
  its big moons in 1610.
- Jupiter gives out more heat than it receives, and its metallic hydrogen makes the largest magnetosphere of any
  planet, fed by Io's volcanoes.
- Nine spacecraft have flown past or orbited it; JUICE and Europa Clipper are on the way.
- Jupiter probably kept Mars small and still sends asteroids its way; Mars's bulge is held up by rock, not spin.
- A planet's spin and shape give its moment of inertia: Jupiter's 0.25 shows a mass packed towards the centre.
