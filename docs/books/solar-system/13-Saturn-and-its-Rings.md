# 13. Saturn and its rings

*Saturn is the farthest planet that is easy to see with the naked eye, and the first look at it through a telescope
makes most people gasp. It is a giant like Jupiter, but lighter: a ball of hydrogen and helium less dense than water,
spinning so fast that it bulges by a tenth. Round it lie rings of ice wider than 20 Earths and in most places only a
few tens of metres thick. This chapter covers its clouds and storms, the rings and the argument over their age, how
people round the world followed it, its heat and magnetism, the spacecraft sent to it, how it compares with Mars, and
why the rings are so thin.*

## 13.1 The ringed planet { #basics }

**Big but light.** Saturn is the sixth planet from the Sun, on average **1.43 billion km** away, 9.5 times the
Earth's distance. Its mass is **95 Earths**, and **764 Earths** would fit inside it. Yet its average density is only
**687 kg/m³**, about 30% less than water. It is the only planet less dense than water: in a bath big enough, it would
float. What is inside, a deep layer of liquid metallic hydrogen and a fuzzy core, is in chapter 3
([section 3.5](03-Rocky-Worlds-and-Giant-Worlds.md#gasgiants)).

**The flattest planet.** Saturn turns once in about **10 hours 39 minutes**. It is **60,268 km** in radius at the
equator but only 54,364 km at the poles, almost 10% less, so it is the most flattened of the planets. Its equator moves
at **9.9 km/s** (section 13.8). The spin flings the equator outwards so hard that gravity there, at the 1 bar level, is
**8.96 m/s²**, a little less than the Earth's 9.78, while at the poles it is 12.14 m/s². Chapter 12 shows how the
flattening tells us how the mass is packed inside
([section 12.9](12-Jupiter.md#derive)).

**How long is a day?** Nobody knows exactly. Saturn has no surface to watch, and its clouds go round at different
speeds at different latitudes. Its magnetic field is lined up almost perfectly with its spin axis, so, unlike
Jupiter's tilted field, it does not swing round once a turn to give a clock. Radio bursts timed by the Voyagers gave
10 h 39 min 22 s, but in 2004 Cassini found that the radio period had stretched to about 10 h 45 min 45 s, far too big
a change for the planet itself. Gas from the moon Enceladus may drag on the magnetic field and slow the radio clock.
Waves in the C ring, shaken by vibrations inside Saturn, give **10 h 33 min 38 s**. So the day lies somewhere between
about 10½ and 10¾ hours.

**Year and seasons.** Saturn takes **29.4 Earth years** to go round the Sun, and its axis is tilted **26.7°**, a little
more than the Earth's 23.4°. So it has seasons like ours, each about seven years long. Sunlight there is only a
ninety-second as strong as at the Earth: **14.8 W/m²**.

**In the sky.** Saturn looks like a steady, pale yellow star, usually around magnitude **0.5**. When the rings are wide
open it can reach **−0.55**. Very large binoculars or a small telescope magnifying 30 times or more show the rings. It
comes to opposition on 18 October 2027 and 30 October 2028 (book 1,
[section 13.4](../night-sky/13-Finding-the-Planets.md#outer)).

**Moons.** NASA counts **274** moons, and another count gives at least **293**, more than any other planet. The
largest, **Titan**, is bigger than the planet Mercury and is the only moon with a thick atmosphere. Chapter 15 visits
Titan, Enceladus and the rest.

## 13.2 Clouds, storms and a hexagon { #weather }

**Paler bands.** Saturn has belts and zones like Jupiter's, but they are much fainter, and wider near the equator. Its
pale yellow colour comes from ammonia ice high up. The clouds come in the same three kinds as on Jupiter (chapter 12,
[section 12.2](12-Jupiter.md#clouds)), but lie deeper down, because Saturn is colder and the right temperatures are
reached lower in the atmosphere:

| Cloud | Pressure | Temperature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| ammonia ice | 0.5 to 2 bar | 100 to 160 K |
| ammonium hydrosulfide ice | 3 to 6 bar | 190 to 235 K |
| water ice | 2.5 to 9.5 bar | 185 to 270 K |
| water drops with ammonia | 10 to 20 bar | 270 to 330 K |

**Fast winds.** At the equator the Voyagers measured winds of up to **500 m/s**, 1,800 km/h, more than three times
Jupiter's fastest jets. Only Neptune's winds are faster (chapter 14).

**Great White Spots.** About once a Saturn year, near the northern summer, a huge storm bursts out of the depths. Its
bright head grows to more than 10,000 km across in a few days, and its tail can stretch right round the planet. Six
are known:

- **1876**, seen by Asaph Hall, who used it to time Saturn's spin;
- **1903**, seen by Edward Barnard;
- **1933**, seen by Will Hay, a comic actor and amateur astronomer, for a long time the most famous;
- **1960**, seen by J. H. Botham in South Africa;
- **1990**, seen by Stuart Wilber;
- **2010**, first seen by Anthony Wesley and followed by Cassini into 2011.

The 2010 storm carried up water ice from about 200 km down, rose 40 to 50 km above the other clouds, and gave off
about ten bursts of lightning a second. Saturn's lightning is about a thousand times as powerful as the Earth's. Why no
white spot was recorded before 1876 is a mystery. None has been seen in the south yet; if one comes, it may be after
12 May 2032, when the south pole leans most towards the Sun.

**A hexagon at the pole.** Around the north pole, at about 78°N, a jet stream runs in a **six-sided** loop. Each side
is about **14,500 km**, longer than the Earth is wide. It was first noted in Voyager pictures from 1981, worked out by
David Godfrey in 1987 by piecing those views together, and watched closely by Cassini from 2006. It turns once in
10 h 39 min 24 s, the same as the old radio period. Between 2012 and 2016 it changed from mostly blue to more golden.
Most scientists think it is a standing wave in the atmosphere, and spinning tanks of fluid in laboratories make
similar polygons. The south pole has no hexagon, but a warm vortex with a storm 8,000 km across that has an eye wall
like a hurricane, and a ten-sided wave pattern that formed around 2023.

## 13.3 The rings { #rings }

**Ice in orbit.** The rings are not solid. They are made of countless pieces of ice, from specks of dust to boulders,
each going round Saturn in its own orbit. In the main rings most pieces are smaller than 10 m, and the ice is
**99.9%** pure water. The main rings reach from **7,000 km to 80,000 km** above Saturn's cloud tops, yet they are only
**10 m to 1 km** thick. In its last months Cassini flew between the rings and the planet and weighed them by their
pull: **1.54 × 10¹⁹ kg**, about two-fifths of the small moon Mimas, or two-thirds of the ice in Antarctica, spread
over 80 times the Earth's surface area.

**Rings with letters.** The rings are lettered in the order they were found:

- **A and B** by Giovanni Domenico Cassini in 1675, with the dark **Cassini Division**, 4,800 km wide, between them;
- **C** in 1850 by William Cranch Bond and his son George, a faint ring that William Lassell called the "crepe ring";
- **D** in 1933 by Nikolai Barabashov and Boris Semeykin;
- **E** in 1967 by Walter Feibelman;
- **F** in 1979 by Pioneer 11, and **G** in 1980 by Voyager 1.

So, going out from Saturn, they run D, C, B, A, F, G, E (Figure A).

**Gaps and shepherds.** Moons carve the rings. The Cassini Division is not empty, only thinner, and its inner edge is
kept sharp by the moon Mimas: a particle there goes round twice for each orbit of Mimas, and the repeated tugs clear
it (book 3, [section 11.2](../orbits/11-Resonances.md#gaps)). The Encke Gap in the A ring, found by James Keeler in
1888, is swept by the tiny moon Pan, and the 42 km Keeler Gap by Daphnis. The thin F ring, which looks braided, is held
in place by two small "shepherd" moons, Prometheus and Pandora.

**Faint rings.** The E ring is made of ice blown out of geysers at the south pole of **Enceladus**, which orbits inside
it, and it is more than 2,000 km thick. Far beyond, the huge, faint **Phoebe ring**, found in infrared light by NASA's
Spitzer Space Telescope, is made of dust from the moon Phoebe. Like Phoebe it goes round backwards, and it is tilted
about 27° to the other rings, because it lies in the plane of Saturn's orbit rather than its equator.

**Spokes and propellers.** The Voyagers saw dark streaks, called **spokes**, lying across the B ring like the spokes
of a wheel. They go round with Saturn's magnetic field, not at the speed of the ring orbits, and are probably fine
dust lifted off the ring by electric charges. Cassini found **propellers**: moonlets about a hundred metres wide, too
small to see, each stirring a propeller-shaped wake several kilometres long. By 2008 more than 150 were known. One,
tracked for years, is nicknamed Blériot.

**Rain from the rings.** The rings are slowly falling into Saturn. Charged grains of ice slide down along the magnetic
field lines in a **ring rain** of 432 to 2,870 kg every second, measured from the Earth with the Keck telescopes. That
alone would remove the rings in about 300 million years. In 2017, diving through the gap between the rings and the
planet, Cassini found a much bigger flow along the equator, **4,800 to 44,000 kg** a second. If that lasts, the rings
could be gone in less than 100 million years (section 13.8).

**How old?** For a long time the rings were thought to be as old as Saturn. Cassini raised doubts. Dust from outside
the Saturn system rains onto them all the time and should slowly darken them, yet they are almost pure ice. From the
measured dust flow, and from their small mass, many scientists concluded the rings are young, a few hundred million
years old or even less, so dinosaurs walked the Earth under a Saturn that may have had no rings. In December 2024,
Ryuki Hyodo, Gustavo Madeira and Hidenori Genda argued in *Nature Geoscience* that this cleanness is misleading. Dust
hits the rings at about **20 km/s**, so fast that it turns into a gas hotter than 10,000 °C. The charged atoms are
then caught by Saturn's magnetic field and carried into the planet or out beyond the rings, so little of the dirt
stays. If they are right, clean rings could still be **4.5 billion years** old. The question is open.

**Where they came from.** In the 19th century Édouard Roche suggested that the rings were once a moon that came too
close and was torn apart by tides. Simulations in 2022 supported this and named the lost moon **Chrysalis**. Other
ideas are two icy moons colliding, which would explain why there is so little rock, or, in a picture of R. M. Canup's,
the icy outer layers stripped from a Titan-sized moon as it spiralled in while Saturn was young. In that picture the
rings began about a thousand times heavier, and their outer edge gave birth to the inner moons, out to Tethys.

## 13.4 Saturn round the world { #history }

**The slow wanderer.** Saturn is the slowest of the planets seen with the naked eye, taking almost thirty years to go
once round the zodiac. **Babylonian** astronomers watched and recorded its movements systematically. In **India** it
is **Shani**, one of the nine navagrahas, who judges everyone by their good and bad deeds. In **China** and **Japan**
it is the "earth star", 土星, from the Five Elements (book 1,
[section 13.7](../night-sky/13-Finding-the-Planets.md#world)). In **Hebrew** it is Shabbathai, and in **Arabic**
Zuhal, a name also used in Ottoman Turkish, Urdu and Malay. The **Greeks** called it Phainon, "the shining one", and
linked it with the god Kronos; the Romans named it after Saturnus, and gave its name to Saturday, *Sāturni
diēs*. Its symbol began in Greek papyri from Oxyrhynchus in Egypt as the first two letters of "Kronos" joined
together. **Ptolemy** worked out its orbit in Alexandria from its oppositions.

**Ears and a vanishing act.** In 1610 Galileo saw Saturn as three bodies, a big one with two small ones almost
touching it, and called them its "ears". In 1612 they vanished. "Has Saturn swallowed his children?" he wondered,
thinking of the myth. Then they came back. We now know why: every 13.7 to 15.7 years the Earth passes through the
plane of the rings, and they are so thin that edge on they all but disappear (Figure B).

**A thin, flat ring.** In London, Christopher Wren had been watching Saturn since about 1652 and came close to
suggesting a ring, but dropped his idea when he saw a better one. Christiaan Huygens, in the Netherlands, ground his
own lenses and in 1655, with a telescope magnifying 43 times, saw what the shape really was. He also found the big
moon **Titan**. He hid his ring idea in a jumble of letters and in 1659 revealed the sentence: *Annulo cingitur,
tenui, plano, nusquam coherente, ad eclipticam inclinato*, "it is surrounded by a thin, flat ring, nowhere touching,
tilted to the ecliptic". Robert Hooke saw shadows cast on the rings.

**More moons and gaps.** At the Paris Observatory, Giovanni Domenico Cassini found four more moons, Iapetus, Rhea,
Tethys and Dione, and in 1675 the gap that bears his name, using a lens only 2.5 inches across with a focal length of
20 feet. William Herschel found Mimas and Enceladus in 1789 (history book,
[chapter 17](../history/17-Herschel-and-the-Canals-of-Mars.md#herschel)). Hyperion followed in 1848, and in 1899
William Henry Pickering found Phoebe, which goes round Saturn backwards and takes more than a year to do it.

**What are the rings made of?** Pierre-Simon Laplace showed that a single solid ring could not survive and suggested
many narrow ringlets. In 1859 James Clerk Maxwell proved that neither solid ringlets nor a fluid ring would be stable:
the rings must be made of countless small pieces, each in its own orbit. Sofia Kovalevskaya later showed that they
could not be liquid either. In 1895 **James Keeler** at the Allegheny Observatory and **Aristarkh Belopolsky** at the
Pulkovo Observatory, working separately, measured the rings' light and found that the inner parts go round faster
than the outer ones, just as separate small moons would. Maxwell was right. In 1944 Titan was found to have a thick
atmosphere.

## 13.5 Heat, magnetism and moons { #inside }

**A fuzzy core.** Saturn's mass is packed more towards its centre than Jupiter's: its moment of inertia factor is
**0.210**, against Jupiter's 0.254 (chapter 12, check question 8). Waves in the C ring, set off by Saturn's own
vibrations, show a **diffuse core** of about 17 Earth masses that blends into the layers above and reaches about 60%
of the way out (chapter 3, [section 3.5](03-Rocky-Worlds-and-Giant-Worlds.md#gasgiants)). Near the centre the
temperature may be **11,700 °C**.

**Heat from helium rain.** Saturn gives out about **2.5 times** as much energy as it gets from the Sun. Jupiter's
extra heat comes from slow shrinking, but Saturn is too small for that to be enough. The likely extra source is
**helium rain**: deep inside, drops of helium separate from the hydrogen and sink, and as they fall through it they
heat it by friction. That would leave the upper layers short of helium, and they are: Saturn's upper atmosphere is
**3.25%** helium by volume, against Jupiter's 10.2%.

**A field with no tilt.** Currents in Saturn's metallic hydrogen make a magnetic field. At the cloud tops it is a
little weaker than the Earth's field at our surface, but Saturn is so big that its magnetic moment is about **580
times** the Earth's (section 13.8). It is about a twentieth of Jupiter's. The strange thing is its tilt: NASA gives
**0.0°**. The Earth's field is tilted about 10° to its spin axis and Jupiter's about 10° too. A well-known result of
physics, Cowling's theorem, says that a dynamo cannot keep up a field that is perfectly symmetric about the spin axis,
so Saturn must hide some lopsidedness deep down; how it does so is not known.

**Magnetosphere.** Saturn's magnetic bubble is far smaller than Jupiter's. When the solar wind was strong, Voyager 2
found it reaching only 19 Saturn radii, 1.1 million km. Titan orbits in its outer part and feeds it with charged
atoms from its air, and Enceladus feeds the inner part with water. Like the Earth's, it makes **aurorae**.

**Moons.** Most of Saturn's hundreds of moons are small, but seven are round. Titan has lakes and seas of liquid
methane and ethane, and Enceladus sprays water from an ocean under its ice through more than 100 geysers. Chapter 15
covers them.

## 13.6 Visitors { #missions }

Four spacecraft have reached Saturn. The first three flew past:

| Spacecraft | Arrived | Closest | Highlights |

| Voyager 1 (NASA) | November 1980 | 64,200 km | first sharp pictures; Titan's haze hid its surface |
| Voyager 2 (NASA) | August 1981 | | more moons up close; the hexagon in its views |

**Cassini–Huygens.** The fourth was the biggest. It was a joint mission of NASA, the European Space Agency and the
Italian Space Agency, and people from 27 countries built and flew it. It was launched on 15 October 1997 and swung
past Venus twice, the Earth and Jupiter on the way, picking up speed from each (book 3,
[chapter 16](../orbits/16-Gravity-Assists.md)). Because it carried plutonium for power, protesters tried to stop the
launch. On **1 July 2004** it became the first spacecraft to orbit Saturn. Radio signals take 67 to 92 minutes to get
there (section 13.8), so it had to do most things on its own.

**Landing on Titan.** On 25 December 2004 Cassini released ESA's **Huygens** probe, which parachuted through Titan's
haze and landed on **14 January 2005**, sending data for about 90 minutes. It was the first landing in the outer solar
system, and the first on any moon but ours.

**What Cassini found.** Over 13 years in orbit it found geysers of water ice at the south pole of Enceladus, lakes and
seas of liquid hydrocarbons near Titan's north pole, one almost as big as the Caspian Sea, giant lightning, the
hurricane-like storm at the south pole, the propeller moonlets and new moons. On 19 July 2013, from behind Saturn, it
photographed the Earth as a dot, and people were asked to wave.

**The Grand Finale.** In 2017, low on fuel, Cassini made 22 dives through the gap between the rings and the cloud
tops, measuring the rings' mass and the rain of ring material. On **15 September 2017** it dived into Saturn and burned
up; the last signal arrived at 11:55:46 UTC. It was destroyed on purpose so that it could never crash onto Titan or
Enceladus and bring microbes from the Earth to places that might be habitable.

**Next.** NASA's **Dragonfly**, a nuclear-powered drone the size of a car, is due to launch in July 2028 and reach
Titan in 2034, to fly from place to place studying its chemistry.

**The same tilt.** Mars and Saturn are tilted almost the same amount, 25° and 27°, so both have seasons much like the
Earth's. But a season on Mars lasts about half an Earth year and on Saturn about seven years, so each Saturn season is
longer than a whole Martian year three times over.

**Storms round the planet.** Both planets have storms that can wrap all the way round. On Mars they are dust storms,
which can grow to cover the whole planet (chapter 8, [section 8.3](08-Mars-among-the-Planets.md#features)); they are
driven by sunlight warming the dusty air. Saturn's Great White Spots get their energy from below, from heat rising out
of the planet.

**A ring for Mars?** Mars's inner moon **Phobos** is sinking towards Mars by about 2 m a century. In 30 to 50 million
years it is expected to break up at about 2.1 Mars radii, inside the Roche limit, and may spread into a ring (book 3,
[section 8.7](../orbits/08-Tides.md#mars)). That is just the story Roche told for Saturn's rings, played forwards. So
Mars may one day have a ring, as Saturn may once have had none. Japan's MMX, which left for Phobos in October 2026,
will bring back samples of the moon (chapter 7, [section 7.7](07-The-Moon.md#mars)).

**Saturn from Mars.** At opposition Saturn is about 8.05 AU from Mars but 8.57 AU from the Earth, so from Mars it looks
about **13%** brighter (section 13.8).

## 13.8 Working it out { #working }

**Floating.** Saturn's mass of $5.683 \times 10^{26}$ kg in a ball of mean radius 58,232 km gives

$$\rho = \frac{M}{\tfrac43 \pi R^3} = \frac{5.683 \times 10^{26}}{\tfrac43 \pi \times (5.823 \times 10^7)^3} = 687
\text{ kg/m}^3,$$

31% less than water's 1,000 kg/m³.

**Flattening and spin.** $(60{,}268 - 54{,}364)/60{,}268 = 5{,}904/60{,}268 = 0.098$. In one turn of 10.656 hours the
equator travels $2\pi \times 60{,}268 = 378{,}700$ km, so it moves at $378{,}700/38{,}362 = 9.87$ km/s.

**The year from Kepler.** With Saturn's mean distance of 9.537 AU, Kepler's third law gives
$T = 9.537^{1.5} = 29.45$ years, as measured.

**Where an orbit lasts one Saturn day.** An orbit has period $T$ where $GM/r^2 = (2\pi/T)^2 r$, so

$$r = \left(\frac{GM T^2}{4\pi^2}\right)^{1/3}
= \left(\frac{3.7931 \times 10^{16} \times 38{,}362^2}{4\pi^2}\right)^{1/3} = 1.122 \times 10^8 \text{ m},$$

112,200 km, or 1.86 Saturn radii, in the outer B ring (Figure A). Ring particles inside it go round faster than
Saturn turns; those outside go round slower.

**The Roche limit for ice.** With Saturn's density of 687 kg/m³ and ice's 917 kg/m³, the fluid Roche limit of
book 3 ([section 8.9](../orbits/08-Tides.md#roche)) is
$2.44 \times (687/917)^{1/3} = 2.44 \times 0.908 = 2.22$ Saturn radii, or 133,600 km. The A ring ends at 2.27 and
the F ring is at 2.32, right at the edge; Mimas, at 3.08, is well outside.

**How long the rings can last.** The rings' mass divided by the rate at which they lose it gives their lifetime. With
ring rain alone,

$$t = \frac{1.54 \times 10^{19} \text{ kg}}{2{,}870 \text{ kg/s}} = 5.4 \times 10^{15} \text{ s} = 170 \text{ million
years},$$

and with 432 kg/s, 1,130 million years. Adding the flow Cassini found, the total is 5,232 to 46,870 kg/s, which gives
93 million down to 10 million years.

**Thinner than paper.** The main rings are about 273,560 km across and in places only 10 m thick. Shrink them to a
disc 1 km across and they would be $10 \times 1{,}000/273{,}560{,}000 = 3.7 \times 10^{-5}$ m thick: 37 micrometres,
about a third of the thickness of a sheet of paper.

**The magnetic moment.** A dipole's moment goes as its field at the equator times the radius cubed. Saturn's field is
0.215 gauss at 60,330 km and the Earth's 0.306 gauss at 6,378 km, so the ratio is
$(0.215/0.306) \times (60{,}330/6{,}378)^3 = 0.703 \times 846 = 595$, close to the 580 quoted.

**Light time.** Saturn is 1,205 to 1,659 million km from the Earth. Radio waves cross that in
$1.205 \times 10^{12}/3.00 \times 10^8 = 4{,}020$ s to $1.659 \times 10^{12}/3.00 \times 10^8 = 5{,}530$ s, or 67 to 92
minutes.

**Saturn from Mars.** At opposition Saturn is $9.57 - 1.52 = 8.05$ AU from Mars and $9.57 - 1.00 = 8.57$ AU from the
Earth, so from Mars it is $(8.57/8.05)^2 = 1.13$ times as bright.

**White spots.** The gaps between the six Great White Spots are 27, 30, 27, 30 and 20 years, an average of 26.8,
against Saturn's year of 29.4.

## 13.9 Why the rings are so thin { #derive }

The main rings are more than 270,000 km across but in places only 10 m thick. Why so flat, and why in Saturn's
equator?

**Collisions flatten a cloud.** Imagine a swarm of ice pieces going round Saturn on orbits tilted every which way.
Pieces on different tilts cross each other's paths twice an orbit and collide. Each collision between lumps of ice
wastes some energy as heat, but it cannot destroy their total spin, the angular momentum that keeps them going round.
The up-and-down motions cancel out, the round-and-round motion stays, and the swarm settles into a flat disc.

**Why the equator.** Saturn's bulge pulls a little extra on anything passing over its equator, and this slowly twists
a tilted orbit round, faster close in and slower farther out. So pieces on tilted orbits at different distances drift
apart in tilt, cross and collide, until all of them lie in the one plane where the bulge has no twist to give: the
equator. That is why the rings share Saturn's 26.7° tilt to its orbit, and why we see them tip towards and away from
us over its year (Figure B).

**Bobbing up and down.** Now take one piece at distance $r$ from Saturn's centre, a small height $z$ above the ring
plane. Saturn's pull, $GM/r^2$, points at the centre. Most of it holds the piece in orbit; a small part points back
down towards the plane. By similar triangles that part is the pull times $z/r$:

$$g_z = -\frac{GM}{r^2} \cdot \frac{z}{r} = -\frac{GM}{r^3}\, z = -\Omega^2 z,$$

where $\Omega = \sqrt{GM/r^3}$ is the angular speed of the orbit, because a circular orbit needs $\Omega^2 r = GM/r^2$.
A pull back proportional to the distance is a spring. So the piece bobs up and down through the plane, with angular
frequency $\Omega$: once each orbit. (This is just another way of saying that a slightly tilted orbit crosses the ring
plane twice each time round.)

**How high it bobs.** If the piece crosses the plane with a sideways, random speed $c$ upwards, its height is
$z = (c/\Omega)\sin \Omega t$. It rises to $c/\Omega$ above the plane and sinks as far below, so the ring is about

$$h \approx \frac{2c}{\Omega}$$

thick. At 100,000 km from Saturn's centre, $\Omega = \sqrt{3.7931 \times 10^{16}/(10^8)^3} = 1.95 \times 10^{-4}$ per
second, an orbit of about 9 hours. For a ring 10 m thick, $c = h\Omega/2 = 10 \times 1.95 \times 10^{-4}/2 = 0.001$
m/s. The pieces' random speeds are only about **1 millimetre a second**, a snail's pace, while they race round Saturn
at 19.5 km/s.

**What sets the random speed.** Neighbouring pieces at slightly different distances go round at slightly different
speeds. Seen from a piece riding along with the ring, a neighbour a distance $d$ farther out drifts backwards at about
$\tfrac32 \Omega d$. (The orbit speed $v = \sqrt{GM/r}$ falls by $\tfrac12 \Omega d$ over the distance $d$, and the
moving frame, turning at $\Omega$, adds another $\Omega d$, which together give $\tfrac32 \Omega d$.) Collisions with
neighbours a particle's width $d$ away keep stirring the random speeds up to about $c \approx \tfrac32 \Omega d$, and
every bump wastes some of it, so the stirring and the losses balance near that value. Then

$$h \approx \frac{2c}{\Omega} \approx \frac{2 \times \tfrac32 \Omega d}{\Omega} = 3d.$$

The spin $\Omega$ cancels. A ring of colliding pieces settles to only **a few pieces thick**. With pieces up to a few
metres across, that is about 10 m, as measured. The rings are thin because they are made of ice lumps that bump
gently, and nothing keeps stirring them faster.

**Thicker where something stirs.** Where a moon tugs the ring, the pieces are stirred faster and the ring is thicker.
At the edge of the Keeler Gap the tilted orbit of Daphnis lifts ring material up to 1.5 km out of the plane, and
the E ring, puffed up by fresh ice from Enceladus, is more than 2,000 km thick.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Why could Saturn float in water, if there were a bath big enough?

??? note "Answer"
    Its average density is 687 kg/m³, about 30% less than water's 1,000 kg/m³, because it is mostly hydrogen and
    helium.

**2.** (beginner) What are Saturn's rings made of?

??? note "Answer"
    Countless pieces of almost pure water ice, from dust to boulders a few metres across, each going round Saturn in
    its own orbit.

**3.** (beginner) Why did Saturn's "ears" vanish for Galileo in 1612?

??? note "Answer"
    The Earth passed through the plane of the rings, so he saw them edge on. They are so thin that edge on they all but
    disappear. This happens every 13.7 to 15.7 years.

**4.** (intermediate) Why is the length of Saturn's day still uncertain?

??? note "Answer"
    There is no surface to watch, the clouds at different latitudes go round at different speeds, and the magnetic
    field is lined up with the spin axis, so it gives no regular wobble. The radio period has even changed, perhaps
    because of gas from Enceladus. Estimates run from about 10 h 33 min to 10 h 46 min.

**5.** (intermediate) Why do many scientists think the rings are young, and what is the main argument against?

??? note "Answer"
    Dust from outside should slowly darken the rings, yet they are 99.9% clean ice, which suggests they are only a
    few hundred million years old. Against this, Hyodo and colleagues showed that dust hitting at 20 km/s turns to
    hot charged gas that Saturn's magnetic field carries away, so the rings could stay clean for 4.5 billion years.

**6.** (intermediate) Where does the E ring come from, and why is it so much thicker than the main rings?

??? note "Answer"
    From ice sprayed out of geysers at the south pole of Enceladus. The fresh particles are tiny and stirred up by the
    moon, so they have large random speeds and spread more than 2,000 km thick.

**7.** (advanced) Suppose the rings, of mass $1.54 \times 10^{19}$ kg, lose 10,000 kg every second. How long would they
last?

??? note "Answer"
    $1.54 \times 10^{19}/10^4 = 1.54 \times 10^{15}$ s. A year is $3.156 \times 10^7$ s, so this is
    $4.9 \times 10^7$ years, about 49 million years.

**8.** (expert) Phobos may break up into a ring at 2.1 Mars radii. Mars's radius is 3,396 km and its
$GM = 4.2828 \times 10^{13}$ m³/s². If the particles' random speeds are 1 mm/s, how thick would the ring be?

??? note "Answer"
    $r = 2.1 \times 3{,}396 = 7{,}132$ km, so $\Omega = \sqrt{4.2828 \times 10^{13}/(7.132 \times 10^6)^3} =
    3.44 \times 10^{-4}$ per second, an orbit of about 5 hours. Then $h \approx 2c/\Omega = 2 \times 0.001/3.44 \times
    10^{-4} = 5.8$ m. A ring of Phobos would be just as thin as Saturn's.

## Summary

- Saturn has 95 Earth masses, a density less than water's, a day of about 10½ hours that is hard to pin down, and is
  the most flattened planet.
- Its clouds are fainter and deeper than Jupiter's; winds reach 500 m/s, Great White Spots erupt about once a Saturn
  year, and a hexagon circles the north pole.
- The rings are 99.9% water ice, 10 m to 1 km thick and about 0.4 Mimas masses; moons carve their gaps, and they are
  slowly raining into Saturn.
- Whether the rings are a few hundred million or 4.5 billion years old is still argued over.
- Babylon, India, China and Greece followed the slow wanderer; Galileo saw "ears", Huygens a ring, and Maxwell proved
  it was made of separate pieces, confirmed by Keeler and Belopolsky in 1895.
- Saturn gives out 2.5 times the energy it gets, probably partly from helium rain, and its magnetic field has no
  measurable tilt.
- Pioneer 11, the Voyagers and Cassini–Huygens have visited; Huygens landed on Titan in 2005, and Dragonfly is next.
- Mars and Saturn share a tilt; Phobos may one day give Mars a ring, as a torn-up moon may once have given Saturn its
  rings.
- Colliding ice settles into a disc only a few pieces thick, because random speeds are kept down to about $\Omega$
  times a particle's size.
