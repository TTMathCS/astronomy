# 6. The Earth as a planet

*We usually see the Earth from the ground, one landscape at a time. This chapter steps back and looks at it the way a
visitor from space would: a blue world, mostly ocean, whose outer shell is broken into plates that keep moving and
renewing it, wrapped in a magnetic shield and in air that living things have changed. Mars, half as wide and long
since quiet, makes the contrast.*

## 6.1 A visitor's view { #view }

Of the four rocky planets, the Earth is the biggest and the densest: **12,756 km** wide, with an average density of
5,513 kg/m³ (chapter 3). It is the third planet from the Sun, at 1 AU, and the only place known to have life. From
space the first thing you notice is the colour. The ocean covers **70.8%** of the surface, and clouds cover most of
the planet.

Two photographs changed the way people see it. On **24 December 1968**, during Apollo 8, the first crewed voyage to
orbit the Moon, the astronaut William Anders photographed the Earth rising over the Moon's grey horizon. The nature
photographer Galen Rowell later called that picture, *Earthrise*, "the most influential environmental photograph
ever taken".

On **14 February 1990**, Voyager 1 was more than 6 billion km away (40.5 AU) when it turned its camera back to take
a "Family Portrait" of the planets. In that picture the Earth is smaller than a pixel, a speck caught in a band of
scattered sunlight. The astronomer Carl Sagan, who had pushed for the picture to be taken, wrote about it in his
1994 book *Pale Blue Dot*, and the name stuck.

Ten months later another spacecraft did something stranger. It looked at the Earth as if it were an unknown planet,
and asked whether it could find life there (section 6.5). And from Mars, the Earth is a bright evening or morning
star with the Moon beside it (book 1, [section 21.4](../night-sky/21-The-Night-Sky-from-Mars.md#earth)).

## 6.2 Moving plates { #plates }

The Earth's outer shell, the **lithosphere**, is the crust together with the cold, rigid top of the mantle. It is
broken into **seven or eight big plates** and many small ones, which ride on a softer layer underneath, the
**asthenosphere**. The plates move at anything from nothing up to about **10 cm a year**. The Atlantic, for example,
widens by about 2.5 cm a year.

Where plates pull apart, at the **mid-ocean ridges**, hot rock wells up and new sea floor forms. Where they push
together, the ocean floor of one plate dives under the other and sinks back into the mantle; this is **subduction**,
and it makes deep ocean trenches. Where plates slide past each other, they jerk along in earthquakes. Most
earthquakes, volcanoes and mountain ranges are along plate edges.

Because the ocean floor keeps being recycled, it is young. Most of it is less than 100 million years old, and the
oldest, in the western Pacific, is about **200 million years**. The continents do not sink, so they keep their old
rock. The oldest rock dated so far is 4.03 billion years old, and tiny crystals called **zircons**, found in Western
Australia, go back 4.4 billion years. Plates have been moving for 3 to 4 billion years.

The engine is heat. About **47 TW** leaks out of the Earth's interior, roughly half made by radioactive elements and
half left over from the Earth's formation. It keeps the solid mantle slowly churning, hot rock rising and cooler
rock sinking, and the plates move on top.

**How the idea won.**

- **1912.** The German scientist **Alfred Wegener** proposed that the continents drift. Others, from Abraham
  Ortelius onwards, had had similar ideas. Wegener noticed that the continents fit together best not along today's
  coasts but along the edges of their shelves, 200 m below the sea.
- **1920s.** Early supporters included **Alexander du Toit** from South Africa, **Arthur Holmes** in England and
  **Milutin Milanković** in Serbia, and the Swiss geologist **Émile Argand** backed the idea in 1922. Many European
  researchers accepted its main points, but it took longer in North America. Critics asked what could push
  continents through the solid ocean floor, and Wegener's speed of 250 cm a year was a hundred times too fast.
- **1950s.** Studies of old magnetism in rocks, led by S. K. Runcorn at Cambridge and P. M. S. Blackett at Imperial
  College, showed by 1953 that India had once been in the southern hemisphere, as Wegener had said.
- **Late 1950s and 1960s.** Maps of the ocean floor by **Marie Tharp**, working with Bruce Heezen, showed a ridge
  running round the globe, and Harry Hess proposed that new sea floor forms there and spreads outwards. In 1963
  Frederick Vine and Drummond Matthews, and separately the Canadian L. W. Morley, explained the magnetic stripes on
  the sea floor (section 6.4). Earthquakes deep under the trenches, in the Wadati–Benioff zones, showed where old sea
  floor goes down. By the end of the 1960s, plate tectonics was accepted.
- **1993 onwards.** GPS made it possible to measure the drift directly.

## 6.3 A water world { #oceans }

The Earth is the only world in the solar system with liquid water lying open on its surface. The ocean covers 70.8%
of the surface, and its floor is on average about **3.8 km** down. All the Earth's water together weighs about
**1.4 × 10²¹ kg**. If the Earth were smoothed into a perfect ball, it would cover it about 2.7 km deep (section 6.8).
Even so, it is only about 0.02% of the Earth's mass.

About **97.5%** of the water is salty, with about 35 g of salt in each kilogram of sea water. Most of that salt came
from volcanoes or was washed out of rock. Of the 2.5% that is fresh, about 69% is ice in ice caps and glaciers and
30% is underground. Rivers and lakes hold only about 1% of the fresh water.

The water is old. The 4.4-billion-year zircons show that there was already liquid water, and some continental
crust, within about 150 million years of the Earth's formation.

Water matters to the whole planet, not just to living things. It helps lock carbon dioxide away in rocks such as
limestone (chapter 5, [section 5.4](05-Venus.md#greenhouse)), which is one reason the Earth did not go the way of
Venus. It carries heat round the world in ocean currents, and its vapour is the Earth's strongest greenhouse gas
(section 6.6).

## 6.4 The magnetic shield { #magnetic }

Deep inside the Earth, the liquid iron of the outer core keeps moving as heat escapes from it. Moving molten metal,
stirred by the heat and twisted by the Earth's spin, works as a **dynamo** and makes the Earth's magnetic field
(chapter 3, [section 3.2](03-Rocky-Worlds-and-Giant-Worlds.md#layers)). At the ground the field is weak, 24 to 66
millionths of a tesla. Its poles are not at the geographic poles: the best-fitting magnet through the centre is
tilted by about **9°** from the spin axis. So a compass does not point exactly north, and the difference changes
from place to place and from year to year.

Out in space, the field makes a bubble called the **magnetosphere**. It turns aside most of the solar wind and of
the cosmic radiation that would otherwise hit the air. Some charged particles get caught in doughnut-shaped zones
round the Earth, the **Van Allen belts**. The inner belt is usually from about 1,000 to 12,000 km up.

Rocks keep a record of the field. When lava cools, its magnetic minerals freeze in the direction of the field at
that moment. That record shows that the field **flips**, north and south magnetic poles swapping over. There have
been at least **183 reversals** in the last 83 million years, on average one every 450,000 years. The last one, the
Brunhes–Matuyama reversal, was **780,000 years** ago. A flip seems to take a few thousand years, and the field gets
weaker during it but does not vanish.

New sea floor records the field as it forms at a ridge and then moves away. So a spreading ridge leaves matching
**stripes** of normal and reversed rock on each side, which is what Vine, Matthews and Morley explained in 1963.

**A worldwide story.**

- **China, from about 206 BC.** The magnetic compass was first used for fortune-telling in the Han dynasty. By
  1088, in the Song dynasty, **Shen Kuo** described compasses used to find the way, with iron needles magnetised by
  stroking them on lodestone.
- **India.** Some historians think a compass made of an iron fish floating in oil was used in southern India in the
  4th century, though others doubt it.
- **Europe and the Islamic world.** The first recorded uses are from about 1190 in Western Europe and from the 13th
  century in the Islamic world.
- **1600.** The English physician **William Gilbert**, in *De Magnete*, argued from experiments on a small magnetic
  globe, his *terrella*, that the Earth itself is a magnet.
- **Early 1900s.** **Bernard Brunhes** and others found volcanic rocks magnetised the opposite way to today's
  field.
- **Late 1920s.** **Motonori Matuyama** gave the first systematic evidence and time scale for reversals: the rocks
  with reversed fields were all from the early Pleistocene or older.
- **1958.** The Soviet satellite Sputnik 2, with detectors designed by **Sergei Vernov**, and then the American
  Explorer 1 and Explorer 3 confirmed the radiation belts, later named after **James Van Allen** of the University
  of Iowa. Explorer 4, Pioneer 3 and Luna 1 then mapped them.

## 6.5 Living air { #air }

Dry air is, by volume, **78.08% nitrogen**, **20.95% oxygen** and 0.93% argon, with about **420 parts per million of
carbon dioxide** and small traces of other gases, such as methane at 1.9 parts per million. Water vapour varies a lot
and is typically about 1%. The whole atmosphere weighs about 5.1 × 10¹⁸ kg, less than a 270th of the ocean.

That much oxygen should not be there. It is a very reactive gas, and left alone it would combine with rocks and
other gases and disappear. The air keeps it only because living things keep making more.

It was not always like this (Figure A). For about the first half of the Earth's history the air had almost no free
oxygen. Then, in the **Great Oxidation Event**, from about **2.46 to 2.06 billion years ago**, oxygen built up in the
air and the shallow seas. It was made by **cyanobacteria**, microbes whose photosynthesis gives off oxygen. The rise
was slow, because the oxygen first had to use up the iron, sulphur and methane that soaked it up. The deep sea got
its oxygen by about 1.85 billion years ago, when layers of iron-rich rock stopped forming on the sea floor. Oxygen
high in the air also made the **ozone layer**, which blocks harmful ultraviolet light and helped life move onto the
land.

**Air as a test for life.** The English scientist **James Lovelock**, while designing instruments for NASA, thought
about how to tell whether Mars has life. Living things would have to use the air and so change it. But Mars's air
turned out to be close to chemical balance: mostly carbon dioxide, with very little oxygen, methane or hydrogen. To
Lovelock, the contrast with the Earth's restless mixture strongly suggested that Mars has no life. The Viking
landers still searched for it, without success. The same line of thought led him to the Gaia idea, that the Earth
works as a self-regulating system.

In December 1990 the Galileo spacecraft, swinging past the Earth on its way to Jupiter, put that idea to the test.
Carl Sagan and his colleagues treated the Earth as an unknown planet. Galileo found plenty of oxygen; methane far
out of chemical balance with it; a pigment spread over the land that absorbs strongly at the red end of the
spectrum, the green of plants; and narrow, pulsed radio signals. In *Nature* in 1993 they wrote that the first three
together strongly suggest life, and that the radio signals seemed to come only from intelligence. Astronomers hope to
look for life on planets round other stars in much the same way (chapter 20).

## 6.6 Seasons and climate { #climate }

The Earth's spin axis is tilted **23.44°** to its orbit, so each hemisphere leans towards the Sun for half the year
and away from it for the other half. That, not the distance from the Sun, makes the seasons (book 1,
[section 8.2](../night-sky/08-The-Suns-Path-and-the-Seasons.md#tilt)). The orbit is nearly round. The Earth is
147.1 million km from the Sun in early January and 152.1 million km in early July, so the sunlight is only about 7%
stronger in January (section 6.8). That is why the northern winter comes when the Earth is closest to the Sun.

The tilt is steady. Calculations find that it varies by only about ±1.3°, and the Moon seems to be the reason
(book 3, [section 20.2](../orbits/20-Mars-Changing-Orbit-and-Tilt.md#moon)): without it, the tilt might swing widely
over millions of years, as Mars's does, though some scientists dispute this. The small, regular changes in the tilt
and the orbit still pace the ice ages, as Milanković worked out (book 3,
[section 20.4](../orbits/20-Mars-Changing-Orbit-and-Tilt.md#history)), the same Milanković who backed Wegener.

The average surface temperature is **15 °C**, about 34 °C warmer than it would be with no greenhouse, mostly thanks
to water vapour and carbon dioxide (chapter 5, [section 5.9](05-Venus.md#derive)). The air and the ocean carry heat
from the tropics towards the poles, which keeps the extremes in check. The air can reach about 55 °C in hot deserts
such as Death Valley and fall to −89 °C in Antarctica, but on a typical day the temperature swings by only about
10 °C between night and day. On Mars, with thin air and no ocean, it swings by nearly 60 °C (section 6.7).

Mars has the same ingredients as the Earth, rock, iron, water and carbon dioxide, but today it has none of the four
things that make the Earth special: moving plates, open oceans, a magnetic field and oxygen in the air.

**One plate.** The whole outer shell of Mars is a single piece. That helps explain its giant volcanoes. On the
Earth, the Pacific plate drifts over a hot spot under Hawaii, an idea put forward by John Tuzo Wilson in 1963. Each
volcano is carried away from the hot spot and a new one starts, leaving the **Hawaiian–Emperor chain**: more than 80
volcanoes, most now under the sea, stretching 6,200 km, the oldest dated at 81 million years. On Mars, with no
drift, lava kept piling up in one place, and with Mars's weaker gravity, **Olympus Mons** grew to about 21.3 km high
and 600 km wide, nearly two and a half times as high as Everest stands above the sea.

**No field.** Mars once had a dynamo. Crust in its southern half, 4.2 to 4.3 billion years old, is strongly
magnetised, in alternating stripes, while younger ground is not. About 4 billion years ago its field was as strong as
the Earth's is now, and it was gone by about 3.8 billion years ago. The InSight lander measured a field of about 2
millionths of a tesla, from old magnetised rock underground; the Earth's is 24 to 66. Some scientists argue that
with no field to protect it, Mars's air was worn away by the solar wind (chapter 19). But Venus has no field either
and kept a huge atmosphere, so the field cannot be the whole story.

**No open water.** At 0.006 bar, the air is too thin for liquid water to last long: it quickly freezes or boils
away. Mars keeps its water as ice, in the polar caps and underground.

**Why Mars went quiet.** The deepest reason is size (Figure B). A planet stores heat in its volume and loses it
through its surface, and Mars has only about half as much planet under each square metre of surface as the Earth. So
it cooled faster (section 6.9). As it cooled, its core stopped stirring and the dynamo died, its outer shell
thickened into a single rigid lid, and its volcanoes slowed. Mars is not completely dead: InSight felt marsquakes
(chapter 3, [section 3.3](03-Rocky-Worlds-and-Giant-Worlds.md#quakes)). The Earth, bigger and still hot inside,
keeps its plates moving and its dynamo running.

## 6.8 Working it out { #working }

**A smooth Earth's ocean.** Spread $M = 1.4 \times 10^{21}$ kg of sea water, of density 1,025 kg/m³, over the whole
surface, $A = 4\pi R^2 = 4\pi (6.371 \times 10^6\ \text{m})^2 = 5.10 \times 10^{14}$ m²:

$$h = \frac{M}{\rho A} = \frac{1.4 \times 10^{21}}{1{,}025 \times 5.10 \times 10^{14}} = 2{,}680 \text{ m},$$

close to Wikipedia's 2.7 to 2.8 km. The ocean weighs $1.4 \times 10^{21} / 5.1 \times 10^{18} = 275$ times as much as
the air.

**Stripes on the sea floor.** At 2.5 cm a year, the Atlantic widens by 25 km every million years. Since the last
reversal, 780,000 years ago, it has widened by $0.025 \times 780{,}000 = 19{,}500$ m, so the youngest stripe down
its middle is about 19.5 km wide. At Wegener's 250 cm a year it would be 1,950 km wide.

**The Pacific plate over Hawaii.** The chain is 6,200 km long and its oldest dated volcano is 81 million years old:

$$\frac{6.2 \times 10^8\ \text{cm}}{8.1 \times 10^7\ \text{years}} = 7.7 \text{ cm a year}.$$

The far end is probably a little older than 81 million years, so the true average is a little less.

**The magnet's tilt.** The north pole of the centre-fitting magnet is at 80.65° N, so it is tilted $90° - 80.65° =
9.35°$ from the spin axis.

**Heat from inside.** 47 TW spread over $5.10 \times 10^{14}$ m² is 0.092 W/m², or 92 mW/m²: one 100 W light bulb's
worth for every 1,100 m² of ground. The sunlight the Earth absorbs, averaged over day and night and the whole globe,
is $1{,}361 \times (1 - 0.294)/4 = 240$ W/m², about 2,600 times as much. Wikipedia gives the inner heat as 0.03% of
the 173,000 TW of sunlight arriving.

**Sunlight in January and July.** Sunlight falls off as the square of the distance, so

$$\left(\frac{152.100}{147.095}\right)^2 = 1.069,$$

about 7% more in January. For Mars, $(249.261/206.650)^2 = 1.455$, 45% more at its closest, so its orbit matters
much more to its seasons (book 1, [section 8.7](../night-sky/08-The-Suns-Path-and-the-Seasons.md#mars)).

## 6.9 Why big planets stay hot longer { #derive }

A planet of radius $R$ holds heat in its whole volume, $\tfrac43\pi R^3$, and loses it through its surface, $4\pi R^2$.
Say its rock has density $\rho$ and specific heat $c$, the energy needed to warm 1 kg by 1 K, and it loses heat at
$F$ watts through each square metre. Cooling the whole planet by $\Delta T$ needs the energy $\rho c\,\Delta T \cdot
\tfrac43\pi R^3$, delivered at the rate $F \cdot 4\pi R^2$, so it takes

$$t = \frac{\rho c\,\Delta T \cdot \tfrac43\pi R^3}{F \cdot 4\pi R^2} = \frac{\rho c\,\Delta T}{F} \cdot \frac{R}{3}.$$

For worlds of similar rock losing heat at similar rates, the time goes with $R/3$, the volume divided by the surface
area (Figure B):

| | Moon | Mercury | Mars | Venus | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| $R/3$ | 579 km | 813 km | 1,132 km | 2,017 km | 2,126 km |
| Compared with the Earth | 0.27 | 0.38 | 0.53 | 0.95 | 1 |

The Earth takes 1.9 times as long as Mars to cool by the same amount, and 3.7 times as long as the Moon. The order
matches what we see: the Moon and Mercury have been quiet for a very long time, Mars was busy early and has slowed
down, and Venus and the Earth are still active.

**A number for the Earth.** Take $\rho = 5{,}513$ kg/m³, $c \approx 1{,}000$ J/(kg·K), about right for rock,
$F = 0.092$ W/m² (section 6.8) and $R = 6.371 \times 10^6$ m. Cooling the whole Earth by 100 K at today's rate would
take

$$t = \frac{5{,}513 \times 1{,}000 \times 100}{0.092} \times \frac{6.371 \times 10^6}{3} = 1.3 \times 10^{16}\ \text{s}
\approx 400 \text{ million years}.$$

The real Earth cools more slowly than that, because about half of the heat coming out is being made fresh by
radioactive elements (section 6.2).

**Heat flow and size.** Radioactive elements make heat in proportion to volume too. If a planet makes $H$ watts in
each cubic metre and loses heat as fast as it makes it, the same volume-over-area argument gives

$$F = \frac{HR}{3}.$$

So a smaller planet made of the same rock also has a weaker flow of heat through its surface. If all of the Earth's
heat came from radioactivity, $H = 3F/R = 3 \times 0.092/(6.371 \times 10^6) = 4.3 \times 10^{-8}$ W/m³. The same $H$
in Mars would give $F = 4.3 \times 10^{-8} \times 3.39 \times 10^6/3 = 0.049$ W/m², about half the Earth's. InSight
carried a probe, nicknamed the mole, to measure Mars's real heat flow. It needed to dig at least 3 m down, but after
nearly two years of trying it never got deep enough, so the measurement is still waiting for a future mission.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) What did Voyager 1's *Pale Blue Dot* picture show, and from how far away?

??? note "Answer"
    The Earth as a speck smaller than a pixel, from more than 6 billion km (40.5 AU), on 14 February 1990.

**2.** (beginner) How much of the Earth's surface is ocean, and how much of the Earth's water is fresh?

??? note "Answer"
    About 70.8% of the surface is ocean. Only about 2.5% of the water is fresh, and most of that is ice.

**3.** (beginner) What are the two main gases in the Earth's air?

??? note "Answer"
    Nitrogen, about 78%, and oxygen, about 21%.

**4.** (intermediate) Why is no ocean floor older than about 200 million years, while some continental rock is over 4
billion years old?

??? note "Answer"
    Ocean floor is made at the ridges and sinks back into the mantle at subduction zones, so it keeps being renewed.
    Continents do not sink, so they keep their old rock.

**5.** (intermediate) How do magnetic stripes on the sea floor show that it spreads?

??? note "Answer"
    New rock at a ridge freezes in the direction of the field at the time. As the field flips and the floor moves
    away on both sides, it leaves matching bands of normal and reversed rock on each side of the ridge.

**6.** (intermediate) Why did Lovelock think Mars had no life?

??? note "Answer"
    Its air is close to chemical balance, mostly carbon dioxide with very little oxygen, methane or hydrogen. Life
    would have changed the air, as it has on the Earth.

**7.** (advanced) Olympus Mons grew in one place, while Hawaii's volcanoes form a chain 6,200 km long. What does that
tell you about the two planets?

??? note "Answer"
    The Pacific plate moves over the hot spot, at up to about 7.7 cm a year, so each volcano is carried away and a new
    one starts. Mars has a single plate that does not move, so its lava kept piling up in the same place.

**8.** (expert) Using $t \propto R$, how much longer than Mercury should the Earth take to cool? Use widths of 4,879
km and 12,756 km.

??? note "Answer"
    $12{,}756/4{,}879 = 2.6$ times as long.

## Summary

- From far away the Earth is a blue dot, mostly ocean and cloud; *Earthrise* and *Pale Blue Dot* made people see it so.
- Its outer shell is seven or eight big plates, moving up to 10 cm a year, which keep the ocean floor young.
- Water covers 70.8% of the surface; 97.5% of it is salty, and most fresh water is ice.
- A dynamo in the liquid core makes the magnetic field, which flips on average every 450,000 years.
- Oxygen arrived 2.46 to 2.06 billion years ago, made by microbes, and life keeps it in the air.
- A steady tilt of 23.44° makes the seasons; oceans and air keep the extremes in check.
- Mars has one plate, no field, no open water and no oxygen, mostly because it is smaller and cooled faster.
