# 10. Meteorites

*Every year a few thousand stones and lumps of iron from space reach the ground. Most come from the asteroid belt, a
few from the Moon and Mars, and some are older than any rock on the Earth. They are the cheapest space probes we
have: they come to us. This chapter looks at how people came to accept that stones fall from the sky, what kinds there
are, where they come from, and how a straight line drawn through a few measurements gives the age of the solar
system.*

## 10.1 From the sky to the ground { #falls }

A **meteoroid** is a piece of rock or metal in space; the streak of light it makes in the air is a **meteor**; any part
that reaches the ground is a **meteorite**. Book 1 covers meteors, fireballs and some famous falls, such as Hoba,
Tagish Lake and Chelyabinsk (book 1, [section 15.5](../night-sky/15-Meteors-and-Comets.md#fireballs)).

**Falls and finds.** A meteorite seen coming down and then picked up is a **fall**; any other is a **find**. By January
2019 the Meteoritical Bulletin Database listed 1,180 confirmed falls. Finds are far more common: more than 70,000
meteorites are known in all, and the Natural History Museum in London puts the number at almost 80,000.

**How often.** Each year, on every million square kilometres, about 8 or 9 meteorites land with more than 1 kg
surviving. Over the whole Earth that is about 4,300 a year (section 10.8), but most fall into the sea or where nobody
sees them.

**How to recognise one.** Racing through the air melts a meteorite's surface into a thin glassy **fusion crust**,
usually black. The heat does not get far in: only a few millimetres into a stone, and up to about 1 cm into iron.

**Where they are found.** In 1969 a Japanese Antarctic expedition found nine meteorites on a field of blue ice near the
Yamato Mountains. They realised that the moving ice sheet carries meteorites along and gathers them where the ice
is pushed up against mountains and worn away. A Japanese expedition in 1974 picked up nearly 700. The United States
began its Antarctic Search for Meteorites (ANSMET) soon after, and European, Italian, Chinese (from 2000) and Korean
(from 2007) teams have searched too. Deserts work almost as well: dark stones stand out, and little rain falls to rot
them. A German team prospecting for oil in Libya in 1986 and 1987 found about 65 on one desert plain, and the gravel
plains of Oman had given about 5,000 by mid-2009.

**Caught on camera.** In 1959 two cameras photographed a fireball over Czechoslovakia, and the photographs were used
both to find the stones, the **Příbram** meteorite, and to work out for the first time the orbit that had brought a
meteorite to the Earth. Other countries then set up camera networks of their own.

## 10.2 Stones from the sky { #history }

People used meteorites long before anyone agreed where they came from.

**Iron from the sky.** Before people could smelt iron from ore, meteorites were almost the only source of the metal.
The iron blade of the dagger buried with **Tutankhamun**, who ruled Egypt in about 1334 to 1325 BC, is about 11%
nickel and 0.6% cobalt, which matches meteoritic iron. In north-west **Greenland** the Inughuit knew the great Cape
York iron, also called **Innaanganeq**, for centuries, and used it as a source of metal for tools and harpoons. Its
eight large pieces weigh 58 tonnes together, the biggest 31 tonnes. In North America, beads of meteoritic iron have
been found in **Hopewell** burial mounds, and in 1915 a 61 kg iron meteorite was found in a **Sinagua** burial of
about AD 1100 to 1200 near Camp Verde, Arizona, wrapped with care in a feather cloth.

**Falls written down.** The oldest meteorite whose fall was seen and which still survives is the **Nōgata** meteorite
of Japan. A boy saw it fall on 19 May 861 and led people to it; it has been kept in a Shinto shrine at Nōgata ever
since. In 1979, after it was studied by Masako Shima of the National Science Museum in Tokyo, the Meteoritical Society
accepted it as a meteorite. In China in 1064, during the Song dynasty, the scholar **Shen Kuo** wrote of a fall near
Changzhou: "a loud noise that sounded like a thunder was heard in the sky; a giant star, almost like the moon,
appeared in the southeast". People later found a pit with the still-hot stone in it. In Europe, a stone that fell
into a wheat field outside **Ensisheim** in Alsace on 7 November 1492 can still be seen in the town's museum.

**Scientists catch up.** Even so, most European scientists of the 1700s did not believe that stones fell from the sky.
In 1794 **Ernst Chladni**, born in Wittenberg in Saxony, published a short book arguing that meteorites come from
space: that explained their high speeds, and why they arrive with fireballs. Then on 26 April 1803 thousands of stones
fell on **L'Aigle** in northern France. The French government sent the physicist **Jean-Baptiste Biot** to look into it,
and his lively report, unlike Chladni's book, was widely read and changed people's minds.

## 10.3 Kinds of meteorite { #kinds }

Meteorites are sorted first into three big groups:

- **Stones**, made mostly of silicate minerals, like the rocks of the Earth's crust and mantle.
- **Irons**, made mostly of iron and nickel. Iron, nickel and cobalt make up more than 95% of them, and the nickel is
  nearly always more than 5%, which is how meteoritic iron is told apart from iron made by people.
- **Stony-irons**, a mixture of the two.

**Chondrites.** About 86% of all meteorites are **chondrites**, stones named after the tiny round grains in them,
**chondrules**. Chondrules were once droplets of molten rock that cooled while floating free in space, and they make
up 20% to 80% of a chondrite. Chondrites were never melted as a whole, so they are samples of the material the planets
were built from.

- **Ordinary chondrites** are about 80% of all falls. They come in three groups, H, L and LL, with high, low and very
  low amounts of iron.
- **Carbonaceous chondrites** are only 4.6% of falls, but they are the most primitive. They hold up to 3% carbon,
  including organic compounds, and some hold 3% to 22% water, locked into their minerals.
- **Enstatite chondrites** are rare, about 2% of the chondrites that fall.

**Achondrites** are stones without chondrules: rock from a body that melted, like lava. They are about 8% of
meteorites, and about two thirds of them are the HED meteorites from Vesta (chapter 9,
[section 9.6](09-The-Asteroid-Belt-and-Ceres.md#visits)).

**Why irons look common.** Irons are only about 5.7% of the meteorites seen to fall, yet they hold almost 90% of the
mass of all known meteorites, about 500 tonnes, and all the largest ones are irons. They are dense, they last a long
time on the ground, and anyone can tell a lump of metal from the rocks around it. Before the 1900s more than 80% of
the few hundred finds were irons or stony-irons. In Antarctica, where nearly everything that falls can be recovered,
irons are only 0.4% of the finds.

Irons and stony-irons come from bodies that melted and let their metal sink: irons are pieces of the metal cores of
broken asteroids, and some stony-irons, the **pallasites**, may come from where core met mantle.

## 10.4 Where they come from { #sources }

**Out of the gaps.** Most meteorites start as pieces knocked off asteroids in collisions. A piece that drifts into one
of the Kirkwood gaps has its orbit stretched until it crosses the Earth's path (chapter 9,
[section 9.3](09-The-Asteroid-Belt-and-Ceres.md#gaps)). After a long time in space, a few of them hit us.

**Which asteroids?** Until recently only about 6% of meteorites, those from the Moon, Mars and Vesta, could be traced
to where they came from. Samples have helped: Itokawa's grains from Hayabusa match the LL chondrites (chapter 9,
[section 9.4](09-The-Asteroid-Belt-and-Ceres.md#types)). Then in 2024 a team from France's CNRS, the European Southern
Observatory and Charles University in Prague showed that about **70%** of all known falls come from just three young
asteroid families, **Karin**, **Koronis** and **Massalia**, made by collisions in the main belt about 5.8, 7.5 and 40
million years ago. The Massalia family alone has given **37%** of known meteorites. Young families still have plenty
of small pieces to spare; old ones have used theirs up. With this work the source of more than 90% of meteorites is
now known.

**An asteroid caught in the act.** On 6 October 2008 Richard Kowalski of the Catalina Sky Survey in Arizona found a
small asteroid, **2008 TC3**, about 4 m across. Within hours it was clear that it would hit the Earth, the first time
that had ever been predicted. Twenty hours after it was found, on 7 October, it blew up about 37 km above the Nubian
Desert in Sudan. Peter Jenniskens of the SETI Institute and Muawia Shaddad of the University of Khartoum led a search
with 45 students and staff of the university, and in the end about 600 meteorites, 10.5 kg in all, were picked up.
They are called **Almahata Sitta**, Arabic for "Station Six", after a railway station nearby. Many are a rare kind of
achondrite, the **ureilites**, which contain tiny diamonds.

## 10.5 The oldest things we can hold { #age }

**The first solids.** Some chondrites, mostly carbonaceous ones, hold small white lumps called **calcium-aluminium-rich
inclusions**, or **CAIs**. They are made of minerals that condensed from hot gas, above 1,300 K, in the disc round the
young Sun. The first to be described, by Mireille Christophe Michel-Lévy in 1968, was in the Vigarano meteorite.
Dating four CAIs by the decay of uranium to lead gave **4,567.30 ± 0.16 million years**. Since they are the oldest
solids that have been dated, that number is usually taken as the **age of the solar system** (chapter 2,
[section 2.1](02-How-the-Solar-System-Formed.md#clues)).

**Allende.** At 01:05 on 8 February 1969 a fireball broke up over the state of Chihuahua in **Mexico**. More than 2
tonnes of stones were picked up, the largest carbonaceous chondrite ever found. It is full of large CAIs, and because
there was so much of it to share, it is often called "the best-studied meteorite in history".

**Older than the Sun.** Some meteorites hold grains that are older still. Tiny grains of silicon carbide in the
Murchison meteorite (section 10.6) were made round other stars before the Sun was born, and the oldest have been
dated to about **7 billion years**, some 2.5 billion years older than the solar system.

**Ages on Vesta.** The HED meteorites crystallised between 4.43 and 4.55 billion years ago, so Vesta had a crust within
about the first 150 million years of the solar system.

## 10.6 Water and the stuff of life { #organics }

**Murchison.** In 1969 a carbonaceous chondrite fell near the town of **Murchison** in Victoria, **Australia**. More
than 100 kg was collected, and since it was a fall, picked up quickly, it is one of the most studied meteorites of
all. More than 15 **amino acids**, the building blocks of proteins, have been found in it. So have uracil, one of the
bases of RNA, and the related xanthine, with carbon whose mix of isotopes shows they were made in space, not added
later on the Earth. In November 2019 the sugar **ribose**, part of RNA, was reported in it too.

None of this is life. It shows that the chemistry that leads towards life went on in the early solar system, and that
meteorites and dust delivered such compounds to the young Earth.

**Catching them clean.** A meteorite starts to change as soon as it lands: air and water attack it, and Earth's own
living things contaminate it. On 28 February 2021 a fireball over Gloucestershire, England, dropped a carbonaceous
chondrite near the village of **Winchcombe**. Thanks to an appeal to the public, pieces were picked up quickly, so
they could be studied before they changed much. The samples brought back from Ryugu and Bennu (chapter 9) are cleaner
still, since they never touched the Earth's air at all.

**Water.** Carbonaceous chondrites can be up to a fifth water, locked into clay minerals. That water formed far from
the Sun, beyond the snow line (chapter 2). Some of the Earth's own water may have arrived this way.

## 10.7 Meteorites from Mars and the Moon { #mars }

**Pieces of Mars.** Most Martian meteorites belong to three groups named after three falls: the **shergottites**,
after a stone that fell at Sherghati in **India** in 1865; the **nakhlites**, after one that fell at El-Nakhla near
Alexandria in **Egypt** in 1911; and the **chassignites**, after one that fell at Chassigny in **France** in 1815.
Together they are the **SNC** meteorites. Their chemistry looked like the Martian rocks the Viking landers measured in
1976, and in 1983 gases trapped in glass in the shergottite EET79001 turned out to match the Martian air that Viking
had measured. That settled it. The nakhlites formed from lava in at least four eruptions between 1,416 and 1,322
million years ago, so Mars still had volcanoes then.

The Natural History Museum in London says there are just under 400 known Martian meteorites, out of almost 80,000
meteorites in all. The famous ALH84001 and how rocks escape Mars are in book 3
([section 7.7](../orbits/07-Speed-Energy-and-Escape.md#mars)), and the Martian meteorites of the Sahara, from Tissint
to Black Beauty, are in book 11 (history book, [section 10.9](../history/10-Africa.md#mars)).

**Pieces of the Moon.** In January 1982 John Schutt, leading an ANSMET team in Antarctica, found an unusual stone,
Allan Hills 81005. Brian Mason of the Smithsonian Institution saw that it was unlike any known meteorite and looked
like rocks brought back from the Moon: the first lunar meteorite to be recognised. Japanese scientists then realised
that they had already collected one, Yamato 791197, in 1979. By July 2019, 371 lunar meteorites were known, more than
1,090 kg, perhaps from more than 30 separate falls, all found in deserts, mostly in Antarctica, northern Africa and
Oman. Some probably come from the far side, where no Apollo or Luna mission went. Finding them also helped the case
for Mars: if impacts could throw rocks off the Moon, they could throw them off Mars.

**Meteorites on Mars.** It works the other way too. The rovers have found iron meteorites lying on Mars, starting with
Heat Shield Rock in 2005 (book 1, [section 15.8](../night-sky/15-Meteors-and-Comets.md#mars)).

## 10.8 Working it out { #working }

**How many land each year.** About 8.5 falls of more than 1 kg per million km² each year, over the Earth's 510 million
km², is

$$8.5 \times 510 = 4{,}300 \text{ a year},$$

about 12 a day. Only 29% of the surface is land, so about $8.5 \times 149 = 1{,}300$ land on dry ground. Compare that
with the 1,180 falls confirmed in the whole of history: almost all are never seen.

**The energy of 2008 TC3.** It had a mass of about 80 tonnes and hit the air at 12.8 km/s, so its energy was

$$E = \tfrac12 m v^2 = \tfrac12 \times 80{,}000 \times 12{,}800^2 = 6.6 \times 10^{12} \text{ J}.$$

A kiloton of TNT is $4.184 \times 10^{12}$ J, so this is 1.6 kilotons, inside the 0.9 to 2.1 kilotons estimated from
the flash. Of its 80 tonnes, 10.5 kg was found: $10.5/80{,}000 = 0.013\%$. Most of it burned up or broke into dust.

**Leaving Mars, the Moon or Vesta.** To escape, a rock needs at least the escape speed $v$, which takes $\tfrac12 v^2$
of energy for each kilogram. For Mars that is $\tfrac12 \times 5{,}030^2 = 12.7$ million joules per kilogram; for the
Moon $\tfrac12 \times 2{,}380^2 = 2.8$ million; for Vesta $\tfrac12 \times 364^2 = 66{,}000$. Throwing a rock off Vesta
takes 190 times less energy than throwing it off Mars, which is one reason there are so many more pieces of Vesta.

**How slow is rubidium?** Rubidium-87 decays with $\lambda = 1.42 \times 10^{-11}$ per year (section 10.9), so its
half-life is

$$t_{1/2} = \frac{\ln 2}{\lambda} = \frac{0.693}{1.42 \times 10^{-11}} = 48.8 \text{ billion years},$$

more than ten times the age of the solar system. In 4.55 billion years only $1 - e^{-0.0646} = 6.3\%$ of it has decayed.

    - 8 to 9 falls per million km² per year: Wikipedia, Meteorite (above); 8.5 is the middle. The Earth's area of 510
      million km² and land of 149 million km²: standard.
    - 2008 TC3's 80 tonnes, 12.8 km/s, 0.9 to 2.1 kilotons and 10.5 kg: Wikipedia, 2008 TC3 (above). A kiloton is

    - Escape speeds: section 10.7. The decay constant of rubidium-87: standard (the value of $1.42 \times 10^{-11}$

    - Everything else: our arithmetic.

## 10.9 Ages from a straight line { #derive }

How do we get a number like 4.55 billion years from a rock? The trick is to compare several minerals from the same
rock.

Take a radioactive **parent** isotope, rubidium-87, which turns into a **daughter**, strontium-87. Each atom has the
same small chance of decaying in each year, so the number of parent atoms falls as

$$P(t) = P_0 e^{-\lambda t},$$

where $\lambda$ is the decay constant. Every parent atom that decays becomes a daughter, so the daughters made so far
number $P_0 - P = P\,(e^{\lambda t} - 1)$, written in terms of the parents left today. If there were $D_0$ daughter
atoms at the start,

$$D = D_0 + P\,(e^{\lambda t} - 1).$$

We do not know $P_0$ or $D_0$. The way round this is to divide everything by the amount of a third isotope,
strontium-86, which is not made by decay and so stays the same, call it $S$:

$$\frac{D}{S} = \left(\frac{D}{S}\right)_0 + \frac{P}{S}\,(e^{\lambda t} - 1).$$

When a rock crystallises from melt, its different minerals take in different amounts of rubidium, so $P/S$ is
different in each. But chemistry cannot tell strontium-87 from strontium-86, so every mineral starts with the
**same** $(D/S)_0$. If we measure $D/S$ and $P/S$ in several minerals today and plot one against the other, the points
lie on a straight line, the **isochron**:

- its **slope** is $m = e^{\lambda t} - 1$, so the age is $t = \ln(1 + m)/\lambda$;
- it meets the left-hand axis at the starting ratio $(D/S)_0$, which we get for free.

For a slope of $m = 0.0667$,

$$t = \frac{\ln 1.0667}{1.42 \times 10^{-11}} = \frac{0.0646}{1.42 \times 10^{-11}} = 4.55 \times 10^9 \text{ years}.$$

**Why it is trusted.** The method needs no guess about how much strontium-87 the rock began with. And the line is its
own check: if the rock was heated or soaked in water later, so that atoms moved in or out, the points would not fall
on a straight line. **How precise?** Changing $t$ by $dt$ changes the slope by $\lambda(1 + m)\,dt$. A slope measured to
0.0003 gives the age to about $0.0003/(1.42 \times 10^{-11} \times 1.0667) = 20$ million years.

Rubidium decays slowly, which suits very old rocks. The 4,567.30 million years of the CAIs came from a related method
using two decays at once, uranium-238 to lead-206 and uranium-235 to lead-207, which can reach a fraction of a million
years.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) What is the difference between a meteorite fall and a meteorite find?

??? note "Answer"
    A fall is a meteorite seen coming down and then picked up; a find is any other meteorite, found later lying on the
    ground.

**2.** (beginner) Why are so many meteorites found in Antarctica?

??? note "Answer"
    The moving ice carries meteorites along and gathers them where it is pushed up against mountains and worn away,
    and dark stones are easy to see on the ice.

**3.** (beginner) What are chondrules, and what are chondrites?

??? note "Answer"
    Chondrules are tiny round grains that were once molten droplets floating in space. Chondrites are stony meteorites
    full of them, about 86% of all meteorites.

**4.** (intermediate) Irons are only about 6% of falls. Why do they make up almost 90% of the mass in collections?

??? note "Answer"
    They are dense, they last a long time on the ground, the biggest meteorites are all irons, and a lump of metal is
    easy to tell from ordinary rock, so people notice and keep them.

**5.** (intermediate) What did the 2024 work find about where most meteorites come from?

??? note "Answer"
    About 70% of falls come from three young asteroid families, Karin, Koronis and Massalia, formed 5.8, 7.5 and about
    40 million years ago; Massalia alone gave 37%.

**6.** (intermediate) How do we know the SNC meteorites came from Mars?

??? note "Answer"
    Their chemistry matches Martian rocks measured by the Viking landers, and gases trapped in glass in EET79001 match
    the Martian air.

**7.** (advanced) 2008 TC3 had a mass of 80 tonnes and hit the air at 12.8 km/s. What was its energy, in kilotons of
TNT ($4.184 \times 10^{12}$ J each)?

??? note "Answer"
    $\tfrac12 \times 80{,}000 \times 12{,}800^2 = 6.6 \times 10^{12}$ J, which is $6.6/4.184 = 1.6$ kilotons.

**8.** (expert) An isochron for rubidium-87 ($\lambda = 1.42 \times 10^{-11}$ per year) has a slope of 0.0500. How old
is the rock?

??? note "Answer"
    $t = \ln(1.05)/\lambda = 0.0488/(1.42 \times 10^{-11}) = 3.44 \times 10^9$ years, about 3.4 billion years.

## Summary

- A few thousand meteorites over 1 kg land each year; most are never seen. Antarctica and deserts are where most are
  found.
- People used meteoritic iron for thousands of years, and falls were written down in Japan in 861 and China in 1064;
  scientists accepted them after Chladni in 1794 and L'Aigle in 1803.
- About 86% are chondrites, full of once-molten droplets; ordinary chondrites are 80% of falls.
- About 70% of falls come from three young asteroid families; a few come from Vesta, the Moon and Mars.
- CAIs, 4,567.30 million years old, are the oldest solids from the solar system; some grains are older than the Sun.
- Carbonaceous chondrites carry water, amino acids and sugars made in space.
- An isochron gives a rock's age from the slope of a straight line, without knowing how it started.
