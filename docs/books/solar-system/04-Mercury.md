# 4. Mercury

*Mercury is the smallest planet and the closest to the Sun. It is a ball of iron with a thin skin of rock, baked to
430 °C by day and frozen to −180 °C by night, with ice hiding in craters at its poles. Its day lasts two of its years,
and from some places on it the Sun stops in the sky and goes backwards. This chapter visits Mercury and compares it
with Mars, which has almost exactly the same surface gravity.*

## 4.1 The innermost planet { #basics }

Mercury is **4,879 km** wide, about two-fifths the width of the Earth and a little smaller than Mars. It goes round
the Sun in **88 days**, faster than any other planet, at an average of 47 km/s.

Its orbit is the most stretched of all the planets, with an eccentricity of 0.21. At its closest, its **perihelion**,
it is 46.0 million km from the Sun; at its farthest, its **aphelion**, 69.8 million km. Its orbit is also tilted 7° to
the Earth's, more than any other planet's.

Because it never gets far from the Sun in our sky, Mercury is only seen low in the twilight, just after sunset or
just before sunrise (book 1, [section 13.3](../night-sky/13-Finding-the-Planets.md#inner)). In English it is named
after the Roman god Mercurius, the fast-moving messenger of the gods. Other peoples' names for it are in book 1,
[section 13.7](../night-sky/13-Finding-the-Planets.md#world).

## 4.2 A long, strange day { #day }

Mercury spins once every **58.6 days**. That is exactly two-thirds of its year: it turns three times for every two
trips round the Sun, a **3:2 spin–orbit resonance**. How this was found, by radar in 1965, is told in book 3,
[section 11.5](../orbits/11-Resonances.md#spin).

The result is a very long day (Figure A). Take a spot that has the Sun overhead, noon, when Mercury is closest to the
Sun. After one orbit, 88 days, Mercury has turned one and a half times, so it is midnight there. After a second orbit
it is noon again. **From one sunrise to the next takes 176 Earth days**, two Mercury years.

*Figure A. Mercury's spin over one orbit, at eleven-day steps.*

The spin and the orbit together make the Sun do strange things in Mercury's sky. Near perihelion Mercury goes round the
Sun faster than it spins, so for a few days the Sun stops and moves backwards. At some places it rises a little,
goes back down and then rises again. At two places on the equator it crosses overhead three times in a row
(section 4.9).

Mercury's axis is tilted only 0.03°, so it has no seasons. At its poles the Sun always skims the horizon, and the
floors of some craters there never see sunlight at all.

## 4.3 Fire and ice { #ice }

Mercury has almost no air to hold heat or carry it around. By day, its equator reaches about **430 °C**, hot enough to
melt lead. At night it falls to about **−180 °C**. Sunlight there is on average 6.7 times as strong as on the Earth.

What little gas there is forms a thin **exosphere** of atoms knocked off the surface by the solar wind and by small
meteoroids: mostly oxygen, sodium, hydrogen, helium and potassium. Its pressure at the surface is less than a
hundred-million-millionth of the Earth's.

Yet there is **ice** on Mercury. Near the poles, the Sun never rises high, and deep craters have floors in permanent
shadow. The poles never get warmer than about −93 °C, and those shadowed floors are colder still, a trap where ice can
last. In the early 1990s radar observations with the 70 m Goldstone dish in California and the Very Large Array in New
Mexico found patches near the poles that reflect radar strongly, as ice does. In 2012 NASA's **MESSENGER** spacecraft
confirmed water ice in the craters at the north pole. The icy craters hold an estimated $10^{14}$ to $10^{15}$ kg of
ice.

## 4.4 Craters, cliffs and names { #surface }

At first sight Mercury looks like the Moon: grey and covered in craters. The largest, the **Caloris basin**, is
1,550 km across, about a third of the planet's width. The impact that made it was so strong that it set off lava
eruptions and raised a ring of mountains around it.

Mercury also has long, curving **cliffs**, called rupes, some hundreds of kilometres long. They formed when Mercury's
core and mantle cooled and shrank after the crust had already set hard. The crust had to wrinkle to fit the smaller
planet, like the skin of a drying apple.

**Names from around the world.** Mercury's craters are named after artists, musicians, painters and writers who are
no longer living. As of 2025, 444 craters had names, among them:

## 4.5 The iron planet { #iron }

Mercury's density, 5,429 kg/m³, is almost as high as the Earth's, even though it is far smaller and less squeezed
(chapter 3). It is about **70% metal and 30% rock** by mass. Its iron core is about **2,020 to 2,070 km** in radius,
about 85% of the way out, leaving only some 400 km of mantle and crust on top (chapter 3, Figure A).

Why so much iron? There are three main ideas:

- **A giant impact** early on stripped away much of the young planet's rocky mantle, leaving the core behind.
- **The young Sun's heat** boiled away rock from its surface, which the solar wind then blew off.
- **Sorting in the disc:** drag in the gas of the young disc (chapter 2) let lighter grains drift away before they
  could join Mercury.

MESSENGER found more potassium and sulphur on the surface than expected. Both are easily boiled off, so their
presence argues against the two hot ideas. The question is still open.

**A magnetic field.** Mercury has a weak magnetic field, about 1% as strong as the Earth's at the surface, but enough
to push the solar wind around the planet. Like the Earth's, it lines up roughly with the spin axis, and it is
probably made the same way: by molten iron flowing in the core. Mars, a bigger planet, has no global field today
(chapter 19).

??? note "Sources"
    - 70% metal and 30% rock, the three ideas, and MESSENGER's potassium and sulphur arguing against the giant impact
      and the vaporisation: Wikipedia, Mercury (above).
    - The core radius of 2,020 km: Wikipedia, Mercury (above); 2,074 km and 85% of the radius: NASA, Mercury facts
      (above). The 400 km left over: our arithmetic.
    - The field at 1% of the Earth's: NASA, Mercury facts (above). Deflecting the solar wind, nearly lined up with the
      spin axis, and a dynamo like the Earth's: Wikipedia, Mercury (above).

## 4.6 Getting there { #missions }

Mercury is close, but it is the hardest planet to reach. A spacecraft falling inwards towards the Sun speeds up, and
to stop at Mercury instead of shooting past, it must lose that speed again. Mercury needs the largest change of
speed of any planet. So missions go the long way round, slowing down with many flybys (book 3,
[chapter 16](../orbits/16-Gravity-Assists.md)).

- **Mariner 10** (NASA, launched 3 November 1973) flew past Venus and then Mercury three times, from 29 March 1974 to
  16 March 1975. Its looping orbit was worked out with the help of the Italian scientist Giuseppe Colombo. It mapped
  40 to 45% of the surface.
- **MESSENGER** (NASA, launched August 2004) flew past the Earth once, Venus twice and Mercury three times before it
  became the first spacecraft to orbit Mercury, on 18 March 2011. It mapped the whole planet by March 2013, measured
  the magnetic field and confirmed the polar ice. Out of fuel, it crashed into Mercury on 30 April 2015.
- **BepiColombo**, a joint mission of ESA and Japan's JAXA named after Colombo, was launched on 20 October 2018. It
  is due to enter orbit round Mercury on **21 November 2026**, after a thruster problem pushed its arrival back from
  December 2025. Its two orbiters, ESA's MPO and JAXA's Mio, are due to separate on 9 to 10 December 2026, and
  science is due to start in April 2027.

Mars has twice Mercury's mass, but it is much wider, so its pull at the surface is the same: you would weigh the
same on both, about 38% of your weight on the Earth. Mercury is denser because it is mostly iron; Mars has a
smaller core and more rock (chapter 3).

Both planets hold ice in their polar regions, but in very different amounts. Mars's south polar cap alone holds
about $10^{16}$ kg of water, 10 to 100 times all the ice in Mercury's shadowed craters. And Mars has thin air,
seasons and a day much like ours; Mercury has none of these.

## 4.8 Working it out { #working }

**The same gravity.** Surface gravity is $g = GM/R^2$. With $GM$ in km³/s² and $R$ in km:

- Mercury: $22{,}032/2{,}439.7^2 = 0.00370$ km/s² $= 3.70$ m/s².
- Mars: $42{,}828/3{,}389.5^2 = 0.00373$ km/s² $= 3.73$ m/s².

Mars has $0.642/0.330 = 1.94$ times the mass, but $(3{,}389.5/2{,}439.7)^2 = 1.93$ times the radius squared, so the
two cancel.

**Sunlight.** Sunlight falls off as $1/r^2$. At Mercury's average distance, 57.9 million km, it is 9,083 W/m². At
perihelion, 46.0 million km, it is $9{,}083 \times (57.9/46.0)^2 = 14{,}400$ W/m²; at aphelion, 69.8 million km,
$9{,}083 \times (57.9/69.8)^2 = 6{,}250$ W/m². That is 4.6 to 10.6 times the sunlight on the Earth, and 11 to 25
times the sunlight on Mars.

**The long day.** In one Earth day Mercury turns $360/58.646 = 6.139°$ and moves $360/87.969 = 4.092°$ round the
Sun. The Sun moves across its sky at the difference, $2.046°$ a day, so a full day is

$$\frac{360}{6.139 - 4.092} = 176 \text{ Earth days}.$$

**How much ice.** Mercury's polar craters hold $10^{14}$ to $10^{15}$ kg. Spread over a square 10 km on a side, that is
$10^{14}/(1{,}000 \text{ kg/m³} \times 10^8 \text{ m²}) = 1$ m to 10 m deep.

??? note "Sources"
    - $GM$, radii, masses, distances and sunlight: NASA, Mercury and Mars Fact Sheets (above). The ice: section 4.3.
    - Everything else: our arithmetic.

## 4.9 When the Sun stands still { #derive }

How fast does Mercury go round the Sun, in degrees a day? By Kepler's second law (book 3,
[section 3.3](../orbits/03-Keplers-Three-Laws.md#second)), a planet moves fastest at perihelion. For an orbit of
eccentricity $e$, the angle $\theta$ measured from perihelion grows at

$$\frac{d\theta}{dt} = n\,\frac{(1 + e\cos\theta)^2}{(1 - e^2)^{3/2}},$$

where $n = 360°/88$ days $= 4.092°$ a day is the average rate. This comes from the angular momentum $r^2\,d\theta/dt =
\sqrt{GMa(1 - e^2)}$ and the ellipse $r = a(1 - e^2)/(1 + e\cos\theta)$.

**At perihelion,** $\theta = 0$ and $e = 0.2056$:

$$\frac{d\theta}{dt} = 4.092 \times \frac{1.2056^2}{0.9577^{3/2}} = 4.092 \times 1.551 = 6.346° \text{ a day}.$$

Mercury spins at $1.5\,n = 6.139°$ a day. Near perihelion it goes round the Sun **faster than it spins**, so the Sun
slips backwards across its sky (Figure B).

**When does it start?** Set the two rates equal:

$$(1 + e\cos\theta)^2 = 1.5\,(1 - e^2)^{3/2} = 1.406, \qquad 1 + e\cos\theta = 1.186, \qquad \cos\theta = 0.903,$$

so $\theta = 25.4°$. To turn that angle into time, use the eccentric anomaly $E$ and Kepler's equation:

$$\tan\frac{E}{2} = \sqrt{\frac{1 - e}{1 + e}}\,\tan\frac{\theta}{2} = 0.812 \times 0.225 = 0.183,
\qquad E = 20.7° = 0.362 \text{ rad},$$

$$M = E - e\sin E = 0.362 - 0.2056 \times 0.354 = 0.289 \text{ rad}, \qquad t = \frac{0.289}{2\pi} \times 88.0 = 4.0
\text{ days}.$$

So the Sun goes backwards from about **4 Earth days before perihelion to 4 days after**, the same four days that
Wikipedia gives. The backward step is small, only about half a degree, which is why the Sun seems to hang almost still
for a couple of weeks over the two hot spots, at its brightest because Mercury is at its closest.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) How long is Mercury's year, and how long is its day from sunrise to sunrise?

??? note "Answer"
    Its year is 88 Earth days. Its day, sunrise to sunrise, is 176 Earth days, two of its years.

**2.** (beginner) How can there be ice on the planet closest to the Sun?

??? note "Answer"
    Mercury's axis is hardly tilted, so near the poles the Sun stays low, and the floors of deep craters are always
    in shadow. With no air to carry heat in, they stay far below freezing.

**3.** (beginner) Whom are Mercury's craters named after? Give two examples from different continents.

??? note "Answer"
    Artists, musicians, painters and writers who have died. For example Bashō, a Japanese poet; Mofolo, a writer
    from Lesotho; Sor Juana, a Mexican writer; Chopin, a Polish composer.

**4.** (intermediate) Why does Mercury have long cliffs across its surface?

??? note "Answer"
    Its core and mantle cooled and shrank after the crust had set, so the crust wrinkled and broke into long cliffs
    to fit the smaller planet.

**5.** (intermediate) Give one idea for why Mercury is mostly iron, and one finding that argues against it.

??? note "Answer"
    A giant impact, or the young Sun's heat, may have stripped off much of its rock. But MESSENGER found more
    potassium and sulphur than expected, and these are easily boiled off, which argues against both hot ideas.

**6.** (intermediate) Why is Mercury so hard to reach, even though it is close?

??? note "Answer"
    A spacecraft falling towards the Sun speeds up, and it must lose that speed to stay at Mercury. It needs the
    largest change of speed of any planet, so missions use many flybys to slow down.

**7.** (advanced) Mars has 1.94 times Mercury's mass and 1.39 times its radius. Show that their surface gravity is
almost the same.

??? note "Answer"
    $g$ goes as $M/R^2$, so Mars has $1.94/1.39^2 = 1.94/1.93 = 1.005$ times Mercury's gravity: the same to within
    1%.

**8.** (expert) At aphelion, how fast does Mercury go round the Sun in degrees a day? Does the Sun go backwards
there?

??? note "Answer"
    $\theta = 180°$: $4.092 \times (1 - 0.2056)^2/0.9577^{3/2} = 4.092 \times 0.6311/0.9373 = 2.76°$ a day. That is
    much less than the spin, 6.14°, so the Sun moves forwards quickly, at $6.14 - 2.76 = 3.4°$ a day.

## Summary

- Mercury is the smallest planet, 4,879 km wide, and the closest to the Sun, with an 88-day, very stretched orbit.
- It spins three times for every two orbits, so its day lasts 176 Earth days, and near perihelion the Sun goes
  backwards in its sky.
- Its surface swings from 430 °C to −180 °C, yet shadowed polar craters hold water ice.
- It is cratered like the Moon, with cliffs from shrinking, and its craters are named after artists from all over
  the world.
- It is about 70% metal, with a core reaching 85% of the way out and a weak magnetic field.
- Mariner 10 and MESSENGER have visited it; BepiColombo is due to arrive on 21 November 2026.
- Mercury and Mars have the same surface gravity, but Mercury is iron-rich, airless and nearly untilted.
