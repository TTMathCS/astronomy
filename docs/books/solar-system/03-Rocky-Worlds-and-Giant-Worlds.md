# 3. Rocky worlds and giant worlds

*Nobody has ever drilled through the Earth's crust, let alone into another planet. Yet we know that the Earth
has a solid iron ball at its centre, that Mars has a liquid core wrapped in molten rock, and that Jupiter is mostly
hydrogen squeezed into a metal. This chapter shows what is inside the two kinds of planet and how we found out: by
weighing them, by timing quakes, and by watching how they spin.*

## 3.1 What density tells us { #density }

The first clue to what a planet is made of is its **density**: its mass divided by its volume. Water has a density of
1,000 kg/m³, ordinary rock about 3,000 and iron about 7,900.

| Planet | Mean density, kg/m³ |
| --- | --- |
| Mercury | 5,429 |
| Venus | 5,243 |
| Earth | 5,513 |
| Mars | 3,934 |
| Jupiter | 1,326 |
| Saturn | 687 |
| Uranus | 1,270 |
| Neptune | 1,638 |

The four rocky planets are denser than rock, so they must hold a lot of metal as well. The giants are much less dense
than rock. Saturn is even less dense than water. They must be made mostly of light stuff: hydrogen and helium, and in
Uranus and Neptune, water, ammonia and methane.

Density alone can mislead, because a big planet squeezes its own insides. The Earth is denser than Mercury partly
because it is bigger and more squashed by its own weight. So we need more clues: quakes and spin.

??? note "Sources"
    - The densities: NASA, [Planetary Fact Sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/).
    - The densities of water, rock and iron, and squeezing raising the density of a big planet: standard.

## 3.2 Layers { #layers }

The rocky planets are not the same all the way through. They are in **layers**, like an onion:

- a thin **crust** of light rock on top,
- a thick **mantle** of heavier rock under it,
- a **core** of iron and nickel at the centre.

The layers formed while the planets were young. Collisions with planetesimals (chapter 2) and the heat of short-lived
radioactive atoms, mostly aluminium-26, melted much of each young planet. In the melt, heavy iron sank to the middle
and lighter rock floated up. This sorting is called **differentiation**.

The Earth's crust is 5 to 70 km thick: thin under the oceans, thick under the continents. The mantle goes down to
2,890 km. Below it is a liquid **outer core** about 2,260 km thick, whose flowing metal makes the Earth's magnetic
field, and at the centre a solid **inner core** about 1,220 km in radius (Figure A).

## 3.3 Listening to quakes { #quakes }

An earthquake sends waves through the whole planet. There are two main kinds:

- **P waves** push and pull, like sound. They go through solids and liquids.
- **S waves** shake from side to side. They go through solids but **not through liquids**.

The waves bend and bounce where the rock changes, so stations around the world pick them up at different times. From
those times, seismologists worked out the inside of the Earth, layer by layer.

- **The core, 1906.** Richard Dixon Oldham, a British geologist who worked for the Geological Survey of India, gave
  the first clear evidence that the Earth has a central core.
- **The crust, 1909.** After an earthquake on 8 October 1909, 39 km south-east of Zagreb, the Croatian geophysicist
  **Andrija Mohorovičić** found a sudden change in wave speed at about 54 km depth: the bottom of the crust. It is
  called the **Mohorovičić discontinuity**, or the Moho.
- **How deep the core starts.** Beno Gutenberg, a German-American seismologist, worked on the depth of the boundary
  between the mantle and the core.
- **The inner core, 1936.** The Danish seismologist **Inge Lehmann** found that there is a solid inner core inside
  the liquid outer core.

The liquid outer core shows itself because S waves cannot cross it: on the far side of the Earth from a big quake,
there is a wide zone where no S waves arrive.

## 3.4 The spin test { #spin }

There is a clever way to look inside a planet without quakes: watch how it spins and how it pulls on spacecraft and
moons. From these, astronomers find the planet's **moment of inertia factor**, a number that says how much of the
mass is near the middle.

- A ball of the same density all the way through has a factor of exactly **0.4**.
- The more the mass is packed into the centre, the smaller the number.

| Planet | Moment of inertia factor |
| --- | --- |
| Mars | 0.366 |
| Earth | 0.331 |
| Venus | 0.337 |
| Mercury | 0.346 |
| Jupiter | 0.254 |
| Uranus | 0.225 |
| Saturn | 0.210 |

All are below 0.4, so all have denser middles. Mars is closest to 0.4, so its core is a smaller share of the planet
than the Earth's. The giants have much smaller numbers: their heavy elements are deep down, under a vast, light
envelope of hydrogen and helium. For Mercury, models that fit its factor give a core 2,020 km in radius, out of
2,440 km.

## 3.5 Inside Jupiter and Saturn { #gasgiants }

Jupiter and Saturn are more than 90% hydrogen and helium by mass. They have no solid surface: the gas just gets
thicker and hotter the deeper you go.

- **Metallic hydrogen.** Deep inside, the pressure is so great that hydrogen becomes a liquid metal, its electrons
  free to move as in copper. Eugene Wigner and Hillard Bell Huntington predicted this in 1935. Jupiter's huge
  magnetic field is made by currents swirling in this metallic hydrogen.
- **A fuzzy core.** Astronomers expected a small, dense core of rock and ice at the centre. Instead, NASA's **Juno**
  spacecraft, by measuring Jupiter's gravity closely, found a **diffuse core** that blends into the layers above it.
  It reaches 30 to 50% of the way out and holds 7 to 25 Earth masses of heavy elements mixed with hydrogen and
  helium. Measurements of waves in Saturn's rings suggest that Saturn has one too: about 17 Earth masses, reaching
  about 60% of the way out.
- **Helium rain.** Deep inside, helium may separate from the hydrogen and fall as drops.
- **Heat from inside.** Jupiter gives out more heat than it gets from the Sun, because it is still slowly shrinking
  and the squeezing heats it. Saturn gives out about 2.5 times as much as it gets.

## 3.6 The ice giants { #icegiants }

Uranus and Neptune are less than half as wide as Jupiter and Saturn, and only about **20%** of their mass is hydrogen
and helium. Most of the rest is what planet scientists call **ices**: water, ammonia and methane. They came into the
planets as ice in the young disc (chapter 2), but inside the planets, at pressures of up to several hundred billion
pascals and temperatures of thousands of kelvin, they are not ice at all. The water is a hot, dense fluid.

So Uranus and Neptune are now called **ice giants**, a separate kind from the **gas giants** Jupiter and Saturn. The
difference became clear in the 1990s, mainly from the visit of **Voyager 2**, still the only spacecraft to have
flown past them. Their magnetic fields are thought to come from the electrically charged fluid in their deep mantles.

They also differ from each other. Neptune gives out about 2.6 times as much heat as it absorbs from the Sun, more
than any other giant. Uranus gives out the least, a tenth as much as Neptune, a puzzle still being worked on.

??? note "Sources"

## 3.7 Inside Mars { #mars }

For Mars we now have quakes too. NASA's **InSight** lander came down on 26 November 2018 at Elysium Planitia, near
the equator. Its seismometer, SEIS, was supplied by the French space agency CNES. By May 2022 it had recorded 1,313
**marsquakes**, including one of magnitude 5 on 4 May 2022. The mission ended on 21 December 2022.

- **2021: the first look.** Three papers in *Science* in July 2021 gave the first measured sizes of the crust and
  core. The crust under InSight is either about 20 km or just under 40 km thick, and across Mars it averages 24 to
  72 km. The core is about 3,700 km across, half the width of the Earth's core, and **liquid**: S waves bounce off it
  instead of going through. Its density, about 6,000 kg/m³, is far below that of the Earth's core, so the iron must
  be mixed with lighter elements such as sulphur, carbon, oxygen or hydrogen.
- **2023: a layer of molten rock.** A team led from ETH Zurich, and a second team in France, found a layer of
  **molten rock about 150 km thick** wrapped around the core. The real metal core is smaller, 1,650 to 1,700 km in
  radius, about half the radius of Mars, and denser than thought.
- **2025: a solid heart?** A team at the University of Science and Technology of China, led by Sun Daoyuan and Mao
  Zhu, reported in *Nature* waves that pass through the very centre faster than a liquid core allows. They found a
  **solid inner core about 600 km in radius**, likely an iron-nickel alloy rich in sulphur and oxygen. If it is
  confirmed, it is the first solid inner core found in a planet other than the Earth.

So Mars has the same layers as the Earth, but with a thicker crust for its size, a core that is a smaller share of
the planet, and no global magnetic field today, which is still a puzzle if part of its core is solid (chapter 19).

## 3.8 Working it out { #working }

**Core volume.** The share of a planet's volume taken by its core is $(r_\text{core}/R)^3$:

- Mars: $(1{,}675/3{,}390)^3 = 0.494^3 = 0.12$, so 12%.
- Earth: $(3{,}485/6{,}371)^3 = 0.547^3 = 0.16$, so 16%.
- Mercury: $(2{,}020/2{,}440)^3 = 0.828^3 = 0.57$, so 57%.

| | Mars | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Radius | 3,390 km | 6,371 km |
| Mean density | 3,934 kg/m³ | 5,513 kg/m³ |
| Moment of inertia factor | 0.366 | 0.331 |
| Crust | 24 to 72 km | 5 to 70 km |
| Core radius | 1,650 to 1,700 km | 3,485 km |
| Core as a share of the radius | about 50% | 55% |
| Core as a share of the volume | 12% | 16% |
| State of the core | liquid, with perhaps a 600 km solid inner core | liquid outer core, 1,220 km solid inner core |
| Core density | about 6,000 (2021) or higher (2023) | 9,000 to 13,000 kg/m³ |

**Would Saturn float?** Its mean density is 687 kg/m³, 0.69 of water's. A ball of that density would float with
69% of it under water, if you could find an ocean big enough.

**How much heavier the inner planets are.** Mercury's density, 5,429 kg/m³, is $5{,}429/3{,}934 = 1.38$ times
Mars's, even though Mercury is smaller and less squeezed. It must have a much bigger share of metal, as Figure A shows.

## 3.9 Two layers from two numbers { #derive }

Model a planet as a core of density $\rho_c$ and radius $xR$, inside a mantle of density $\rho_m$. Two measured
numbers pin it down: the mean density $\bar\rho$ and the moment of inertia factor $C$.

**Mass.** The planet is a ball of density $\rho_m$ plus an extra $\rho_c - \rho_m$ in the core:

$$\bar\rho = \rho_m + (\rho_c - \rho_m)\,x^3.$$

**Spin.** A uniform ball has $C = 0.4$, and a ball of radius $xR$ adds in proportion to $x^5$ (its mass goes as $x^3$
and the square of its size as $x^2$):

$$C\,\bar\rho = 0.4\left[\rho_m + (\rho_c - \rho_m)\,x^5\right].$$

Subtract the second from the first, divided by 0.4, to remove $\rho_m$:

$$\left(1 - \frac{C}{0.4}\right)\bar\rho = (\rho_c - \rho_m)\left(x^3 - x^5\right).$$

**Mars.** Take the core size from the quakes. With $x = 1{,}830/3{,}390 = 0.540$, the 2021 value: $x^3 = 0.157$,
$x^5 = 0.046$, and $(1 - 0.366/0.4) \times 3{,}934 = 334$. So $\rho_c - \rho_m = 334/0.111 = 3{,}000$, the mantle is
$3{,}934 - 3{,}000 \times 0.157 = 3{,}460$ kg/m³ and the core $6{,}460$ kg/m³, close to the 6,000 measured in 2021.

With the 2023 size, $x = 1{,}675/3{,}390 = 0.494$: $\rho_c - \rho_m = 334/0.091 = 3{,}660$, mantle 3,490 and core
**7,160 kg/m³**. A smaller core must be denser to hold the same mass, just as the 2023 teams said.

**Earth.** With $x = 3{,}485/6{,}371 = 0.547$ and $C = 0.3308$: $(1 - 0.827) \times 5{,}513 = 954$, $x^3 - x^5 =
0.115$, so $\rho_c - \rho_m = 8{,}320$, the mantle 4,150 and the core 12,500 kg/m³, inside the 9,000 to 13,000
range of the real core.

**What it leaves out.** Real planets have a crust, mantles that get denser with depth, and cores in two parts. So two
numbers give only a rough picture, and they cannot find the core size by themselves: that took InSight's quakes.

??? note "Sources"
    - The two-layer model and the $x^5$ term: standard. The values: NASA fact sheets and sections 3.2 and 3.7.
    - The Earth's core density range: DLR (above).
    - Everything else: our arithmetic.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Name the three main layers of a rocky planet, from the outside in.

??? note "Answer"
    Crust, mantle and core. The Earth's core is in two parts, a liquid outer core and a solid inner core.

**2.** (beginner) Why do we think Saturn is made mostly of light stuff?

??? note "Answer"
    Its mean density is only 687 kg/m³, less than water's 1,000. Rock or metal would make it far denser.

**3.** (beginner) Which kind of quake wave cannot pass through a liquid, and what did that show about the Earth?

??? note "Answer"
    S waves. They do not cross the Earth's outer core, so the outer core must be liquid.

**4.** (intermediate) Mars has a moment of inertia factor of 0.366 and the Earth 0.331. Which has more of its mass
packed into the middle?

??? note "Answer"
    The Earth. Its number is further below 0.4, the value for a ball of the same density all through.

**5.** (intermediate) What is metallic hydrogen, and where is it found?

??? note "Answer"
    Hydrogen squeezed so hard that it becomes a liquid metal, with its electrons free to move. It is deep inside
    Jupiter and Saturn, and its currents make Jupiter's magnetic field.

**6.** (intermediate) Give two ways in which Uranus and Neptune differ from Jupiter and Saturn.

??? note "Answer"
    Any two: they are much smaller; only about 20% of their mass is hydrogen and helium, against more than 90%; most
    of their mass is water, ammonia and methane as a hot, dense fluid; they have only been visited by one spacecraft.

**7.** (advanced) What share of Mars's volume was its core thought to take in 2021, with a radius of 1,830 km, and
what share after 2023, with 1,675 km?

??? note "Answer"
    $(1{,}830/3{,}390)^3 = 0.157$, about 16%; $(1{,}675/3{,}390)^3 = 0.121$, about 12%.

**8.** (expert) Mercury's mean density is 5,429 kg/m³ and its core radius 2,020 km out of 2,440. Take its mantle as
3,300 kg/m³. What core density does the mass equation give?

??? note "Answer"
    $x = 2{,}020/2{,}440 = 0.828$, $x^3 = 0.567$. Then $\rho_c - \rho_m = (5{,}429 - 3{,}300)/0.567 = 3{,}750$, so
    $\rho_c = 7{,}050$ kg/m³, a core of iron with some lighter elements.

## Summary

- Density shows what a planet is made of: the rocky planets hold rock and metal, the giants mostly light elements.
- The rocky planets have a crust, a mantle and an iron core, sorted out when the young planets melted.
- Quake waves found the Earth's layers: Oldham the core in 1906, Mohorovičić the base of the crust in 1909, Lehmann
  the solid inner core in 1936.
- The moment of inertia factor, from a planet's spin and pull, shows how much mass is near the centre.
- Jupiter and Saturn are mostly hydrogen and helium, metallic deep down, around fuzzy cores.
- Uranus and Neptune are ice giants, mostly water, ammonia and methane as a hot fluid.
- InSight showed that Mars has a crust 24 to 72 km thick, a molten rock layer and a liquid core about half its
  radius, and in 2025 a team reported a solid inner core.
