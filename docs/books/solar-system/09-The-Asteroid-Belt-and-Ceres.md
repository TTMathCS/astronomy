# 9. The asteroid belt and Ceres

*Past Mars there is a wide ring of rocky leftovers that never became a planet. Over a million of them are known, yet
put together they weigh only a few percent of the Moon, and more than a third of that is a single body, Ceres. This
chapter looks at where the belt is, how it was found and sorted, what spacecraft saw at Ceres and Vesta, and why the
biggest asteroids are round while the small ones are lumps.*

## 9.1 The belt at a glance { #belt }

The asteroid belt is the region between Mars, at 1.52 AU from the Sun, and Jupiter, at 5.20 AU, where most of the
solar system's asteroids go round. Its main part runs from **2.06 AU to 3.27 AU**. Jupiter sets both edges: an
asteroid at 2.06 AU would go round four times for each of Jupiter's orbits, and one at 3.27 AU twice, and Jupiter
clears both places out (section 9.3).

**How many.** On 9 November 2026 JPL's Small-Body Database listed **1,480,348** asteroids in its three main-belt
classes: 33,178 in the inner belt, closer than 2.0 AU; 1,396,133 in the main belt proper, from 2.0 to 3.2 AU; and
51,037 in the outer belt, from 3.2 to 4.6 AU. Of them, 96.6% lie between 2.06 and 3.27 AU.

**How much.** Together they have only about **2.39 × 10²¹ kg**, about 3% of the Moon's mass. The four largest,
Ceres, Vesta, Pallas and Hygiea, hold about 62% of it, and Ceres alone 39% (Figure A). NASA's Ceres page gives a
smaller share, 25%.

**How empty.** Films show asteroid fields packed with tumbling rocks. The real belt is mostly empty: on average the
asteroids are about **1 million km apart**, two and a half times the distance from the Earth to the Moon. Pioneer 10
was the first spacecraft to cross it, entering on 16 July 1972. Some feared the debris would be a danger, but many
spacecraft have crossed since without trouble.

**Leftovers, not wreckage.** The belt is not the remains of a planet that broke up. It formed from planetesimals
(chapter 2, [section 2.3](02-How-the-Solar-System-Formed.md#growth)) that were stopped from growing into a planet:
Mars and Jupiter stirred them up so much that they hit too hard to stick, and broke instead. About **99.9%** of the
belt's first mass was lost in the first 100 million years. What is left today is the scraps.

## 9.2 Finding them { #found }

The first asteroid, **Ceres**, was found by Giuseppe Piazzi at Palermo on 1 January 1801, lost in the Sun's glare,
and found again at the end of the year thanks to a new method of Carl Friedrich Gauss. Book 11 tells that story
(history book, [section 17.4](../history/17-Herschel-and-the-Canals-of-Mars.md#rule)) and book 3 explains Gauss's
method ([section 12.2](../orbits/12-Measuring-Orbits.md#gauss)). Piazzi named it after Ceres, the Roman goddess of
farming, whose oldest temple was in Sicily. He wanted to add *Ferdinandea*, after his king, but other countries would
not have it.

**More of them.** About 15 months after Ceres, Heinrich Olbers found a second body in the same region, **Pallas**.
**Juno** followed, and Olbers found **Vesta** on 29 March 1807. Even in the best telescopes they stayed points of
light and never showed a disc, so in 1802 William Herschel suggested a new name for them, **asteroids**, from the
Greek for "star-like". Olbers suggested that Ceres and Pallas were pieces of a larger planet that had blown up or been
hit. The idea did not last: all the asteroids together have far too little mass for a planet, and they differ too
much in make-up to come from one body.

**The count grows.** Over 100 asteroids were known by mid-1868. In 1891 Max Wolf began finding them on photographs,
which sped things up: there were 1,000 by 1921, 10,000 by 1981 and 100,000 by 2000. Today automatic surveys do most
of the finding, from all over the world. The Purple Mountain Observatory near Nanjing in China, for example, is
credited with 149 discoveries between 1955 and 1983, and its survey of near-Earth objects with more than 600 between
2006 and 2013. The Vera C. Rubin Observatory on Cerro Pachón in Chile showed over 2,000 new asteroids in its very first
images, released in 2025. The world list of minor planets passed 1.5 million in 2026 (chapter 1,
[section 1.5](01-A-Tour-of-the-Solar-System.md#small)).

**Names and numbers.** Once an asteroid's orbit is known well, it gets a number in order of that, and its finder may
suggest a name: 1 Ceres, 4 Vesta, 433 Eros.

## 9.3 Gaps and families { #gaps }

**Gaps.** In 1866 Daniel Kirkwood noticed that hardly any asteroids go round at certain distances, and explained why.
At those distances an asteroid's year is a simple fraction of Jupiter's 11.86 years, so it meets Jupiter in the same
places over and over, and the repeated pulls stretch its orbit until, within a few million years, it crosses the
paths of the planets. Book 3 shows the gaps and how resonances work (chapter 11,
[section 11.2](../orbits/11-Resonances.md#gaps)). Kepler's third law gives where they should be (section 9.8):

| Resonance | Year | Kepler's law gives | Gap seen at |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 4:1 | 2.97 years | 2.06 AU | 2.065 AU, the inner edge |
| 3:1 | 3.95 years | 2.50 AU | 2.502 AU |
| 5:2 | 4.75 years | 2.82 AU | 2.825 AU |
| 7:3 | 5.08 years | 2.96 AU | 2.958 AU |
| 2:1 | 5.93 years | 3.28 AU | 3.279 AU, the outer edge |
| 3:2 | 7.91 years | 3.97 AU | 3.972 AU, a crowd, the Hildas |

The gaps are deep. In the JPL list, the strip from 2.48 to 2.50 AU holds 3,799 asteroids, while the strip of the same
width from 2.56 to 2.58 AU holds 43,343, more than eleven times as many.

Not every resonance empties its strip. At the 3:2 resonance there is a crowd instead, the **Hilda** asteroids: 5,124
of them between 3.95 and 4.00 AU against only 55 between 3.70 and 3.75. Their orbits are arranged so that they never
come close to Jupiter. Jupiter's own orbit has two more crowds, the **Trojans**, 60° ahead and behind
(book 3, [section 9.4](../orbits/09-Lagrange-Points-and-Trojans.md#trojans)).

The asteroids thrown out of the gaps do not vanish. Many end up crossing the orbits of Mars and the Earth, and that is
where most of the near-Earth asteroids, and most meteorites, come from (chapter 10).

**Families.** In 1918 the Japanese astronomer **Kiyotsugu Hirayama** noticed that some asteroids have nearly the same
orbits: the same distance, the same stretch and the same tilt. He named the Koronis, Eos and Themis families, and later
the Flora and Maria families. Each family is the debris of one collision that broke a bigger body, its pieces still
going round on similar paths. About a third of the asteroids in the belt belong to a family. The **Vesta family**,
for example, is probably debris blasted out of Vesta when its great southern crater formed (section 9.6).

## 9.4 What asteroids are made of { #types }

We cannot pick up most asteroids, but we can measure how much light they reflect and in what colours. Asteroids with
similar spectra are put in the same **type**, named with a letter:

- **C-types** are dark and carbon-rich, like the carbonaceous chondrite meteorites. They make up over 75% of the
  asteroids we can see, rule the outer belt and are rare in the inner belt.
- **S-types** are stony, made of silicate rock, and brighter. They are common in the inner belt.
- **X-types** have plain spectra and are split by how much light they reflect into **M-types**, which look like iron
  and nickel, the dark **P-types**, and the bright **E-types**, found mostly among the Hungarias at the inner edge.
- **V-types** are made of basalt, like Vesta and its family.

So the belt is sorted: dry stony asteroids near the Sun, dark carbon-rich ones farther out. That fits the **snow
line** (chapter 2, [section 2.4](02-How-the-Solar-System-Formed.md#snowline)): bodies that formed beyond about 2.7 AU
could take in ice, those closer in could not.

How much light they reflect varies a lot. JPL gives Ceres an albedo of 0.09, so its surface reflects 9% of the light
falling on it, about like asphalt; Eros reflects 25% and Vesta 42%.

**Samples.** Three missions have brought asteroid material back to the Earth (section 9.6). The grains from **Itokawa**,
an S-type, match a common kind of stony meteorite, the LL chondrites, so at least some of the meteorites that fall on
the Earth come from S-type asteroids like it. **Ryugu** and **Bennu** are both dark carbon-rich asteroids, and both
have a density of only about **1.19 g/cm³**, much less than solid rock. Ryugu turned out to be a **rubble pile**,
about half empty space, a loose heap of boulders held together by its own very weak gravity.

**Metal worlds?** M-types such as **16 Psyche** may be the metal cores of bodies that were broken up by collisions,
though some rocks can give a similar spectrum. Psyche's density of 4.17 g/cm³ is high for an asteroid but well
below that of iron, 7.9, so it is probably a mixture of metal and rock.

## 9.5 Ceres { #ceres }

**Ceres** is the largest body in the belt, **939.4 km** across, about a quarter of the Moon's width. Its mass is
**9.38 × 10²⁰ kg**, three and a half times Vesta's but only 1/78 of the Moon's, and its surface gravity is 1/35 of
ours. Its density, **2.16 g/cm³**, suggests that about a quarter of its mass is water ice. It turns in 9 hours 4
minutes and its axis is tilted by only 4°, so some craters near its poles never see the Sun and can trap ice. It goes
round at 2.77 AU in **4.6 years**, close to the old snow line, on an orbit tilted by 10.6°.

In 2006 Ceres was moved from asteroid to **dwarf planet** (chapter 1,
[section 1.6](01-A-Tour-of-the-Solar-System.md#planet)). It is the only dwarf planet in the inner solar system. At
its brightest it reaches magnitude 6.7, just too faint to see without binoculars except under very dark skies.

**Dawn.** NASA's Dawn spacecraft went into orbit round Ceres on **6 March 2015**, the first visit to a dwarf planet.
It found a surface made of water ice mixed with minerals that formed in water, such as carbonates and clays. Its
measurements of Ceres's gravity suggest a muddy mantle of ice and rock under a lighter but stronger crust that is at
most 30% ice. Some scientists think Ceres formed farther out, between Jupiter and Saturn, and was moved in later.

**Craters.** Ceres is covered in craters, but it has fewer big ones than expected. Models had predicted 10 to 15
craters wider than 400 km; none of the craters Dawn found is wider than about 280 km. Something, perhaps the ice in
the crust slowly flowing, seems to have smoothed the biggest ones away.

**Bright spots.** Inside the crater **Occator** are the brightest places on Ceres: **Cerealia Facula** in the middle,
and the **Vinalia Faculae** to the east. In 2017 they were found to be mostly **sodium carbonate**, with some ammonium
salts, left behind when salty water, **brine**, came up and dried out. In August 2020 NASA confirmed that a deep
reservoir of brine lies under Occator and has reached the surface in hundreds of places. A dome in the middle of the
crater seems to have formed as water underneath froze and pushed up the ground, much like the ice hills called pingos
in the Arctic.

**An ice volcano.** **Ahuna Mons** is a lonely mountain about 4 km high that seems to have been pushed up by brine
from below: an ice volcano, or **cryovolcano**. Such volcanoes seem to form on Ceres about once every fifty million
years, which makes Ceres the closest body to the Sun known to have ice volcanoes. Ceres also has a very thin, patchy
haze of water vapour that comes and goes.

Water, salts and carbon compounds together make Ceres interesting to people who look for places where the chemistry
of life could start. Dawn ran out of fuel on **1 November 2018**. It still goes round Ceres, silent, on a stable orbit.

## 9.6 Vesta and the visits { #visits }

**Vesta** is the second-largest asteroid, about **525 km** across, and the brightest in our sky: at its best it
reaches magnitude 5.1 and can just be seen without a telescope. It holds about 9% of the belt's mass. Its density,
3.46 g/cm³, is higher than that of most asteroids, and it has separated into crust, mantle and core, like a small
planet. It is thought to be the only one left of the rocky building blocks, the protoplanets, that went into the
rocky planets.

Dawn went into orbit round Vesta on **16 July 2011** and left on **5 September 2012** for Ceres. It showed that the
south pole of Vesta is a huge crater, **Rheasilvia**, about 500 km wide, some 95% of Vesta's own width, made by a
collision less than a billion years ago. Its central peak, about **22 km** high, is one of the tallest mountains in the
solar system. Rheasilvia lies on top of an older crater, **Veneneia**, about 400 km wide. Pieces of Vesta thrown out
by such blows have fallen on the Earth as the **HED meteorites** (chapter 10), so we have pieces of Vesta in our
museums.

Several of these targets, such as Eros, Itokawa, Ryugu, Bennu and Kamoʻoalewa, are near-Earth asteroids rather than
members of the belt. They are easier to reach, and most of them came from the belt in the first place (section 9.3).

**Hitting back.** DART's crash on **26 September 2022** was the first test of moving an asteroid. The target,
Dimorphos, is a moonlet about 160 m wide going round a larger asteroid, Didymos; neither is any danger to the Earth.
The impact cut Dimorphos's orbit by 32 minutes, far more than the 73 seconds counted as success, mostly because of the
push from the debris thrown out. Hera, from the European Space Agency, is due to reach the pair in November 2026, a
month earlier than first planned, to measure the result close up, with two small CubeSats, Milani and Juventas.

**Tianwen-2.** China's Tianwen-2 was launched on 28 May 2025 and reached **Kamoʻoalewa** in early July 2026. This small
asteroid is a quasi-satellite of the Earth: it goes round the Sun with us, appearing from the Earth to loop round us.
The first close pictures, from 20 km on 2 July, showed a small, long, rocky body. The plan is to collect a sample, drop
it off at the Earth in 2027, and then go on to 311P/PanSTARRS, a comet in the main belt. As of early November 2026 we
had found no report that the sample had been collected.

Mars has nearly 700 times the mass of Ceres, and over 2,000 times that of Vesta. Both asteroids spin much faster than
Mars, and their gravity is so weak that a person who could jump half a metre on the Earth could jump more than 15 m on
either.

**Mars at the edge.** Mars is the belt's nearest planet. Most bodies that formed inside the belt's inner edge were
swept up by Mars, whose orbit takes it out to 1.67 AU. The few that survive there, the Hungarias, keep away from Mars by
going round on steeply tilted orbits (book 3, [section 11.7](../orbits/11-Resonances.md#mars)). Mars also has
Trojans of its own (book 3, [section 9.8](../orbits/09-Lagrange-Points-and-Trojans.md#mars)), and its two little moons
may be captured asteroids, though that is still argued over (chapter 7, [section 7.7](07-The-Moon.md#mars)).

**Why Mars is small.** The same stirring by the giants that stopped the belt from becoming a planet probably also
left Mars short of material (chapter 2, [section 2.7](02-How-the-Solar-System-Formed.md#mars)). In that sense Mars and
the belt are two results of the same history.

**Vesta, a small Mars?** Vesta and Mars both separated into crust, mantle and core, and both made basalt lava. Pieces
of both have landed on the Earth as meteorites, which is how we can study them in laboratories (chapter 10). Ceres is
different: a world of ice, mud and salts. NASA's Ceres page says that if Ceres is up to 25% water, it has more water
than the Earth.

**A stepping stone.** Spacecraft for the belt often borrow speed from Mars on the way (book 3, chapter 16,
[section 16.1](../orbits/16-Gravity-Assists.md#idea)). Dawn passed 549 km above Mars on 17 February 2009, Hera flew
past in March 2025, and Psyche in 2026.

## 9.8 Working it out { #working }

**Where the gaps are.** Kepler's third law (book 3, [chapter 3](../orbits/03-Keplers-Three-Laws.md)) says that for
anything going round the Sun, the year $T$ in Earth years and the distance $a$ in AU obey $T^2 = a^3$. An asteroid in
the 3:1 resonance goes round three times for each of Jupiter's 11.862-year orbits, so

$$T = \frac{11.862}{3} = 3.954 \text{ years}, \qquad a = T^{2/3} = 3.954^{2/3} = 2.50 \text{ AU},$$

right on the gap at 2.502 AU. The other rows of the table in section 9.3 are worked out the same way.

**Ceres's year.** The other way round, Ceres at $a = 2.77$ AU should take $T = 2.77^{3/2} = 4.61$ years. JPL gives
1,680 days, which is $1{,}680/365.25 = 4.60$ years.

**The whole belt against the Moon.** $2.394 \times 10^{21} / 7.346 \times 10^{22} = 0.033$, about 3%. (Another part
of Wikipedia's page gives about 4%, from an older estimate.) The Moon would have to be broken into about 30 pieces the
size of the whole belt.

**Gravity on Ceres.** JPL gives Ceres $GM = 62.63$ km³/s², which is $6.263 \times 10^{10}$ m³/s², and its radius is
469,700 m. So

$$g = \frac{GM}{R^2} = \frac{6.263 \times 10^{10}}{469{,}700^2} = 0.284 \text{ m/s}^2,$$

which is $9.81/0.284 = 35$ times less than the Earth's, as NASA and Wikipedia say. The escape speed is

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{2GM}{R}} = \sqrt{\frac{2 \times 6.263 \times 10^{10}}{469{,}700}} = 516 \text{ m/s},$$

about one and a half times the speed of sound in air. For Vesta, with $GM = 17.29$ km³/s² and $R = 261{,}400$ m, the
same sums give 0.253 m/s² and 364 m/s.

**Jumping.** A jump uses the same energy anywhere, so the height goes as $1/g$: half a metre on the Earth becomes
$0.5 \times 35 = 17$ m on Ceres and $0.5 \times 39 = 19$ m on Vesta.

      2.502 AU: Wikipedia, Kirkwood gap (above).
    - Ceres's $a$, period, GM and diameter, and Vesta's GM and diameter: JPL, Small-Body Database Lookup (above).

## 9.9 Why big asteroids are round { #derive }

Figure A shows that the biggest asteroids are round or nearly so, and the small ones are lumps. The reason is the
pressure inside.

Take a ball of radius $R$ and even density $\rho$. At a distance $r$ from the centre, only the mass inside $r$ pulls,
so the gravity there is

$$g(r) = \frac{G}{r^2} \cdot \frac43 \pi r^3 \rho = \frac{4\pi}{3} G \rho\, r.$$

Going down a small step $dr$, the pressure grows by the weight of that thin layer, $\rho\, g(r)\, dr$, the same rule as
in water. Adding up all the layers from the surface, where the pressure is zero, to the centre:

$$P_c = \int_0^R \rho \cdot \frac{4\pi}{3} G \rho\, r \, dr = \frac{2\pi}{3} G \rho^2 R^2.$$

For Ceres, with $\rho = 2{,}162$ kg/m³ and $R = 469{,}700$ m:

$$P_c = \frac{2\pi}{3} \times 6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times 2{,}162^2 \times 469{,}700^2 = 1.4 \times 10^8 \text{ Pa}.$$

In chapter 8 ([section 8.9](08-Mars-among-the-Planets.md#derive)) we found that rock can bear about $\sigma \approx
3 \times 10^8$ Pa, and weaker rock perhaps $10^8$ Pa. When the pressure at the centre is above the strength of the
rock, the inside slowly gives way, the high parts sink and the low parts are pushed up, and the body is pulled into a
ball. When it is well below, the body keeps whatever shape the collisions gave it.

**Where round begins.** Set $P_c = \sigma$ and solve for the width $D = 2R$:

$$D = 2\sqrt{\frac{3\sigma}{2\pi G \rho^2}}.$$

With $\sigma = 10^8$ Pa this gives about 560 km for rock of 3,000 kg/m³, 490 km for Vesta's density and 590 km for
Pallas's. Vesta, at 523 km, is just past the line and nearly round, though Rheasilvia took a big bite out of its south
pole; Pallas, at 513 km, is just short of it. Ceres, at 939 km, is well past its own line of 780 km.

**The same answer from mountains.** In chapter 8 we found the tallest mountain a world can hold, $h_{\max} \approx
\sigma/(\rho g)$. On a small body $h_{\max}$ can be as big as the body itself; chapter 8's last question found 350 km
for Ceres. A body stays lumpy when its lumps, mountains as tall as its own radius, can stand. With $g = \frac{4\pi}{3}
G\rho R$, setting $h_{\max} = R$ gives

$$R^2 = \frac{3\sigma}{4\pi G\rho^2},$$

the same form as before, with the radius smaller by only a factor of $\sqrt2 = 1.4$. Two different ways of looking at
it agree.

These are rough numbers: rock is not one fixed strength, it slowly creeps under steady loads, and ice is much weaker
than rock. That is why icy moons are round at smaller sizes than rocky asteroids, as we will see with the moons of the
giants. The rule the International Astronomical Union uses for a dwarf planet, that it is massive enough to pull
itself round, is this same balance.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Between which two planets is the asteroid belt, and where are the edges of its main part?

??? note "Answer"
    Between Mars and Jupiter. The main part runs from 2.06 to 3.27 AU, edges set by the 4:1 and 2:1 resonances with
    Jupiter.

**2.** (beginner) Why is a spacecraft crossing the belt very unlikely to hit anything?

??? note "Answer"
    The belt is mostly empty: the asteroids are on average about a million kilometres apart. Many spacecraft have
    crossed it safely since Pioneer 10 in 1972.

**3.** (beginner) Who found Ceres, and when? What is it called now?

??? note "Answer"
    Giuseppe Piazzi, at Palermo on 1 January 1801. It was first called a planet, then an asteroid, and since 2006 it
    has been a dwarf planet.

**4.** (intermediate) What did Kiyotsugu Hirayama notice in 1918, and what does an asteroid family tell us?

??? note "Answer"
    That some asteroids have nearly the same orbits. A family is the debris of one collision that broke up a bigger
    body.

**5.** (intermediate) What are Ceres's bright spots made of, and how did they get there?

??? note "Answer"
    Mostly sodium carbonate, with some ammonium salts, left behind when brine from a deep reservoir came up to the
    surface and dried.

**6.** (intermediate) Vesta is smaller than Ceres, so why is it the brightest asteroid in our sky?

??? note "Answer"
    Its surface reflects about 42% of the light falling on it, against Ceres's 9%, and its orbit is closer to the Sun
    and the Earth.

**7.** (advanced) Use Kepler's third law to find where the 5:2 gap should be. Jupiter's year is 11.862 years.

??? note "Answer"
    The asteroid's year is $11.862 \times 2/5 = 4.745$ years, so $a = 4.745^{2/3} = 2.82$ AU, close to the gap at
    2.825 AU.

**8.** (expert) Using $P_c = \tfrac{2\pi}{3} G \rho^2 R^2$, how wide must a rocky asteroid of density 3,000 kg/m³ be
for the pressure at its centre to reach $10^8$ Pa?

??? note "Answer"
    $R = \sqrt{3\sigma/(2\pi G\rho^2)} = \sqrt{3 \times 10^8/(2\pi \times 6.674 \times 10^{-11} \times 3{,}000^2)} =
    \sqrt{7.9 \times 10^{10}} = 282{,}000$ m, so about 560 km wide, a little wider than Vesta.

## Summary

- The main belt runs from 2.06 to 3.27 AU, with about 1.48 million known asteroids, though all together they have only
  about 3% of the Moon's mass.
- The four largest hold about 62% of that, Ceres alone 39%; the belt is mostly empty space.
- Ceres was found in 1801; the name "asteroid" came in 1802; the count passed 100,000 in 2000.
- Jupiter's resonances cut the Kirkwood gaps; collisions made the families Hirayama found.
- Dark carbon-rich asteroids rule the outer belt and stony ones the inner, as the snow line suggests.
- Dawn found brines, salts and an ice volcano on Ceres, and a crater almost as wide as Vesta at Vesta's south pole.
- Bodies are pulled round when the pressure at the centre is more than rock can bear, which happens at about 500 km.
