# 11. Resonances

*When two orbits keep in step, so that one body goes round a whole number of times while the other goes round another
whole number, they meet again and again in the same places. Each meeting gives the same small tug, and the tugs add
up. This chapter shows how that clears gaps in the asteroid belt and in Saturn's rings, how it keeps Pluto safe from
Neptune, how it heats Io until its volcanoes erupt, why Mercury's day lasts two of its years, and how Mars has shaped
the asteroids that come nearest to it.*

    - why some resonances protect instead: the Hildas, the Trojans and Pluto

    - how Mars has cleared out the asteroids just inside the belt

## 11.1 Pushing a swing { #swing }

Push a child on a swing once and not much happens. Push at the same point of every swing and the swing goes higher and
higher. Orbits work the same way. If one body goes round, say, three times while another goes round once, they meet in
the same places again and again, and each meeting gives the same small tug. This is an **orbital resonance**, and it
is written as a ratio of the two counts, such as 3:1.

Many pairs come close to a resonance. In 8 years the Earth goes round the Sun 8 times and Venus 13 times, and the two
are back within 1.5° of where they started. Five orbits of Jupiter take almost as long as two of Saturn, which gave
Laplace his 900-year swing (chapter 10, [section 10.6](10-Wobbling-Orbits-and-the-Finding-of-Neptune.md#stable)). Mars
goes round 42 times in 79 years (chapter 2, [section 2.9](02-Wandering-Stars.md#working)).

A near match on its own does little if the pulls are tiny. Resonance matters when one body is big, like Jupiter, or
close, like a moon next to its neighbour, and when the meetings fall in the same places for a long time. Then it can
do one of two opposite things. It can clear bodies out, or it can hold them safe. Which one depends on where in the
orbit the meetings happen (section 11.9).

## 11.2 Gaps in the belt and in the rings { #gaps }

**Kirkwood gaps.** In 1866 Daniel Kirkwood, a mathematics professor at Indiana University, noticed that the asteroid
belt has gaps, and explained them. Hardly any asteroids orbit at 2.50 AU, where an asteroid would go round in 3.95
years, three times for each of Jupiter's 11.86-year orbits. The same happens at the 4:1, 5:2, 7:3 and 2:1 points. An
asteroid in one of these gaps meets Jupiter in the same places over and over, its orbit changes chaotically, and within
a few million years it is thrown onto a path that crosses the orbits of the planets.

The gaps do not show in a picture of where the asteroids are at one moment, because their orbits are ellipses and many
asteroids pass through the gap distances on the way round. They show when the asteroids are sorted by the size of
their orbits, as in Figure A.

**The Cassini Division.** In 1675 Giovanni Domenico Cassini saw that Saturn's ring is really several rings with gaps
between them. The widest gap, the Cassini Division, is 4,800 km across, between the A ring and the B ring. Its inner
edge is where a ring particle would go round Saturn twice for each orbit of the moon Mimas, and this 2:1 resonance keeps
it clear.

## 11.3 Kept apart { #apart }

**The Hildas.** Just outside the belt, at 3.97 AU, more than 7,000 asteroids go round three times for every two of
Jupiter's orbits. They are the Hildas, named after 153 Hilda. Their orbits are timed so that they reach their farthest
point from the Sun, the part of the orbit nearest Jupiter's, just when Jupiter is at least 60° away along its orbit.
Seen from a frame that turns with Jupiter, the group forms a triangle with its corners at Jupiter's L3, L4 and L5
points.

**The Trojans.** Jupiter's Trojans share its orbit in a 1:1 resonance, 60° ahead and behind (chapter 9,
[section 9.4](09-Lagrange-Points-and-Trojans.md#trojans)).

**Pluto.** Pluto's orbit is so stretched that it comes closer to the Sun than Neptune: the last time was from 1979 to
1999. Yet the two can never collide. Pluto goes round twice while Neptune goes round three times, every 495 years, and
the timing keeps them apart. When Pluto reached its nearest point to the Sun in 1989, Neptune was 57° ahead of it, and
at the next one Neptune will be 123° behind. Pluto comes closest to Neptune near its own farthest point, and even then
stays more than 17 AU away. It comes closer to Uranus, 11 AU.

The resonance even corrects itself. When Pluto drifts a little too close behind Neptune, Neptune's pull gives it a
little more speed, which moves it to a slightly bigger, slower orbit (chapter 7,
[section 7.2](07-Speed-Energy-and-Escape.md#paradox)). Pluto falls back, and the pattern swings to and fro over about
20,000 years.

Pluto is not alone. About a quarter of the known bodies of the Kuiper belt share the same 3:2 resonance. They are the
**plutinos**, named after creatures of the underworld, such as Orcus and Ixion; the first after Pluto was found on
16 September 1993. They were probably caught when Neptune moved outwards early in the solar system's history, and
swept them along.

## 11.4 Three moons in step and Io's volcanoes { #io }

Io goes round Jupiter in 1.769 days, Europa in 3.551 and Ganymede in 7.155. Io makes two orbits for each of Europa's,
and four for each of Ganymede's. This 1:2:4 pattern is called a **Laplace resonance**, after Laplace, who studied it.
It is locked so tightly that the three moons can never all line up on the same side of Jupiter.

The repeated tugs from Europa and Ganymede keep Io's orbit slightly stretched, with an eccentricity of 0.004. So Io's
distance from Jupiter changes a little in every orbit, and Jupiter's tide squeezes and relaxes it once every orbit
(chapter 8, [section 8.6](08-Tides.md#locking)). Without the resonance, the tides would soon make Io's orbit round and
the squeezing would stop.

Stan Peale, Patrick Cassen and R. Reynolds worked this out and published it shortly before Voyager 1 flew past Io on
5 March 1979. They predicted that Io's inside must be heated strongly. Soon after the flyby Linda Morabito, a Voyager
navigation engineer, noticed a plume rising from the edge of Io in one of the pictures. Other pictures showed nine
plumes. Io has more than 400 active volcanoes, more than any other world in the solar system.

## 11.5 Spins in step { #spin }

A resonance can also tie a body's spin to its orbit. The Moon, Phobos and Deimos each turn once for each orbit, a 1:1
spin–orbit resonance, so they always show the same face (chapter 8, [section 8.6](08-Tides.md#locking)).

For a long time Mercury was thought to do the same. In the 1880s Giovanni Schiaparelli mapped it and suggested that it
turned once in 88 days, the length of its year, always facing the Sun with the same side. In June 1962 Soviet
scientists led by Vladimir Kotelnikov became the first to bounce radar off Mercury. In 1965 Gordon Pettengill and Rolf
Dyce, using the 300 m Arecibo radio telescope in Puerto Rico, found that it turns once in about 59 days. The same year
the Italian astronomer Giuseppe Colombo noticed that 59 is two-thirds of 88: Mercury turns three times for every two
orbits, a **3:2 spin–orbit resonance**.

The result is a strange day. From one sunrise to the next on Mercury takes 176 Earth days, exactly two of its years.
The European and Japanese mission to Mercury, BepiColombo, is named after Colombo.

## 11.6 Chains round other stars { #chains }

Planets round other stars fall into step too, sometimes in long chains. **Kepler-223** has four planets with periods
of 7.38, 9.85, 14.79 and 19.73 days, in the ratio 3:4:6:8.

**TRAPPIST-1** is a small, cool red dwarf star with seven planets about the size of the Earth. A team led by the
Belgian astronomer Michaël Gillon found the first two in 2016, with the small TRAPPIST-South telescope at La Silla in
Chile and others. In 2017 the team found five more, using the Spitzer Space Telescope (book 2,
[section 18.2](../light-and-telescopes/18-Infrared-Ultraviolet-X-ray-and-Gamma-ray-Astronomy.md#infrared)),
TRAPPIST-North at Oukaïmeden in Morocco, the South African Astronomical Observatory and telescopes in Spain. Each
planet's period is close to a simple fraction of the next one's: about 8/5, 5/3, 3/2, 3/2, 4/3 and 3/2 going outwards.
It is the longest such chain known.

Chains like these are hard to make by chance. They are thought to form when planets drift slowly through the disc they
grew in and catch each other in step one after another, much as Neptune swept up the plutinos.

## 11.7 Resonances at Mars { #mars }

**The Hungarias.** Inside the 4:1 gap at 2.06 AU, the inner edge of the main belt, the asteroids are ruled by Mars
rather than Jupiter. Over the life of the solar system Mars has thrown out almost every asteroid there whose orbit lies
close to the plane of its own. What is left is the **Hungaria** group, between 1.78 and 2.00 AU, named after 434
Hungaria, the largest at about 11 km. Their orbits are fairly round but tilted by 16° to 34°, which keeps them well
above or below Mars most of the time. They are the innermost crowd of asteroids in the solar system. Even now some
Hungarias cross Mars's orbit and are slowly being thrown out, and the slow stretching of Mars's own orbit
(chapter 10, [section 10.7](10-Wobbling-Orbits-and-the-Finding-of-Neptune.md#mars)) helps to do it.

**A slow resonance that feeds Mars.** Not every resonance is a matter of whole orbits. An asteroid's orbit slowly turns
(chapter 10), and so does Saturn's. Where the two turn at the same rate, a **secular resonance** called $\nu_6$, the
asteroid's orbit is stretched little by little, until it crosses Mars's orbit. Then a close pass by Mars usually throws
it out of the belt. Some of the asteroids sent this way, and out of the Kirkwood gaps, end up crossing the Earth's
orbit too.

**Mars's own resonances.** Mars shares its orbit with its Trojans in a 1:1 resonance (chapter 9,
[section 9.8](09-Lagrange-Points-and-Trojans.md#mars)), and Phobos and Deimos are locked 1:1 in spin. Mars and the
Earth are near a 42:79 match, which is why its close approaches repeat (book 1,
[section 14.3](../night-sky/14-Mars-Backward-Loop-and-Close-Approaches.md#close)), but their pulls on each other are
too weak for that to matter to their orbits. Resonances between the slow turning of Mars's spin axis and the slow
changes in its orbit make its tilt swing widely over millions of years; chapter 20 tells that story.

## 11.8 Working it out { #working }

**Where a resonance lies.** By Kepler's third law (chapter 3), $a \propto P^{2/3}$. An asteroid that goes round $p$
times while Jupiter goes round $q$ times has a period $q/p$ of Jupiter's, so it lies at $a = a_J (q/p)^{2/3}$. With
$a_J = 5.2029$ AU:

| Resonance | $(q/p)^{2/3}$ | Distance | Period |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 4:1 | 0.3969 | 2.065 AU | 2.97 years |
| 3:1 | 0.4807 | 2.501 AU | 3.95 years |
| 5:2 | 0.5429 | 2.825 AU | 4.74 years |
| 7:3 | 0.5685 | 2.958 AU | 5.08 years |
| 2:1 | 0.6300 | 3.278 AU | 5.93 years |
| 3:2 | 0.7631 | 3.971 AU | 7.91 years |

These match the gaps and the Hildas in Figure A.

**Pluto and Neptune.** $2 \times 247.94 = 495.9$ years and $3 \times 164.79 = 494.4$ years, the same to 0.3%.

**Jupiter's three moons.** Count the turns each moon makes per day: $1/1.769138 = 0.565247$, $1/3.551181 = 0.281596$
and $1/7.154553 = 0.139771$. Then $0.565247 - 3 \times 0.281596 + 2 \times 0.139771 = 0.000000$ to six places. The
three keep the same pattern for ever, so the angle they make cannot drift into a line-up.

**Mercury.** $87.969 / 58.65 = 1.4999$, so three turns take as long as two orbits.

## 11.9 Where the meetings fall { #derive }

**How many meeting places.** Let an inner body go round $p$ times while an outer one goes round $q$ times, with
$p > q$. The time between meetings, when the inner body laps the outer one, is

$$S = \frac{1}{1/P_{in} - 1/P_{out}}.$$

With $P_{out} = (p/q)\,P_{in}$, this gives $S = \dfrac{p}{p - q}\,P_{in}$. So between meetings the inner body makes
$p/(p - q)$ orbits. The whole-number part brings it back to the same place, and the rest moves the meeting point on
by $360° \times q/(p - q)$, give or take whole turns. After $p - q$ meetings the pattern repeats. So:

- a 2:1 or 3:2 resonance has **one** meeting place;
- a 3:1 has **two**, on opposite sides;
- a 5:2 has **three**, 120° apart;
- a 7:3 has **four**.

**Clearing or protecting.** If a meeting place falls where the small body comes nearest the big one, the tug is strong
and always the same, and it builds up, as in the Kirkwood gaps. If the meeting place falls where they are far apart,
as at Pluto's farthest point, the tugs are weak, and they push the meeting place back whenever it starts to drift, as
section 11.3 showed for Pluto. A resonance is a trap; whether it is a safe or a dangerous one depends on where the trap
closes.

**For Pluto**, $p = 3$ and $q = 2$ with Neptune as the inner body, so there is one meeting place. Neptune laps Pluto
every $1/(1/164.79 - 1/247.94) = 491$ years, almost the full 495-year cycle, and the meeting place stays near Pluto's
aphelion.

??? note "Sources"
    - The synodic period and the counting of meeting places are standard. Pluto's meetings near its aphelion and the
      self-correcting swing: section 11.3 and its sources. The numbers are our arithmetic.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) What is an orbital resonance?

??? note "Answer"
    Two bodies whose periods are in a ratio of small whole numbers, such as 3:1 or 3:2, so they meet in the same
    places again and again and give each other the same tug each time.

**2.** (beginner) Why are there gaps in the asteroid belt?

??? note "Answer"
    At certain distances an asteroid would go round a whole number of times for a whole number of Jupiter's orbits,
    such as 3 for 1 at 2.50 AU. Jupiter's repeated tugs make such orbits change chaotically until the asteroid is
    thrown onto a planet-crossing path, leaving a gap.

**3.** (beginner) Pluto crosses inside Neptune's orbit. Why can they never collide?

??? note "Answer"
    Pluto goes round twice while Neptune goes round three times, and the timing means Neptune only passes Pluto when
    Pluto is near its far point. They never come closer than about 17 AU.

**4.** (intermediate) Why is Io the most volcanic world in the solar system?

??? note "Answer"
    Its 1:2:4 resonance with Europa and Ganymede keeps its orbit slightly stretched, so Jupiter's tide keeps squeezing
    and relaxing it. The friction heats its inside.

**5.** (intermediate) Use Kepler's third law to find where an asteroid would go round twice for each of Jupiter's
orbits. Jupiter is at 5.203 AU.

??? note "Answer"
    $a = 5.203 \times (1/2)^{2/3} = 5.203 \times 0.630 = 3.28$ AU, the outer edge of the main belt.

**6.** (intermediate) Why do most asteroids inside 2.06 AU have orbits tilted by 16° to 34°?

??? note "Answer"
    Mars has thrown out almost all the asteroids there whose orbits lie close to its own plane. The tilted ones spend
    most of their time well above or below Mars, so they survive. They are the Hungarias.

**7.** (advanced) Mercury turns once in 58.65 days and goes round the Sun in 87.97 days. Show that its day, from one
sunrise to the next, lasts two of its years.

??? note "Answer"
    The Sun moves round Mercury's sky at $1/58.65 - 1/87.97 = 0.017050 - 0.011368 = 0.005683$ turns a day, so a solar
    day is $1/0.005683 = 176.0$ days, and $176.0 / 87.97 = 2.00$ years.

**8.** (expert) How many meeting places does a 7:2 resonance have, and how far apart are they?

??? note "Answer"
    $p - q = 7 - 2 = 5$ meeting places. Each meeting moves the place on by $360° \times 2/5 = 144°$, and five
    such steps visit five places spaced $360° / 5 = 72°$ apart.

## Summary

- An orbital resonance is a ratio of small whole numbers between two periods; the bodies meet in the same places again
  and again, and the same tugs add up.
- Resonances with Jupiter clear the Kirkwood gaps in the asteroid belt; a 2:1 resonance with Mimas clears the edge of
  Saturn's Cassini Division.
- Other resonances protect: the Hildas at 3:2 with Jupiter, the Trojans at 1:1, and Pluto and the plutinos at 3:2 with
  Neptune.
- Io, Europa and Ganymede are locked 1:2:4; the resonance keeps Io's orbit stretched and its volcanoes erupting.
- Mercury turns three times in two orbits, found by radar from Arecibo in 1965 and explained by Colombo.
- TRAPPIST-1's seven planets form the longest resonant chain known.
- Mars has cleared the region inside 2.06 AU of all but the tilted Hungaria asteroids, and the $\nu_6$ resonance sends
  asteroids across its orbit to be thrown out.
- Whether a resonance clears or protects depends on where in the orbit the meetings fall.
