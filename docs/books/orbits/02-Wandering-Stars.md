# 2. Wandering stars: the puzzle of the planets

*Five bright lights move among the stars, and for thousands of years nobody could say how. They drift one way, stop,
go backwards, stop again, and they hurry through some parts of the sky and dawdle through others. This chapter sets out
the puzzle that every theory of the planets had to solve. It follows the people who counted the planets' cycles, the
Greek circles on circles that copied the motions without knowing why, and the moment when putting the Sun in the
middle turned those circles into real distances. It ends with the one planet that still would not fit: Mars.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    **You will learn:**

    - the five facts about the planets that any theory has to explain
    - the two separate ways a planet's motion is uneven
    - how people in Babylon, China, India and the Americas predicted the planets by counting cycles
    - how Ptolemy's circles on circles worked, and why every one of them turned once a year
    - how putting the Sun in the middle turned Ptolemy's circles into distances
    - that the farther a planet is from the Sun, the slower it goes
    - why Mars was the planet that broke the circles
    - how the puzzle looks from Mars

    **You need:** chapter 1. Book 1, [chapter 14](../night-sky/14-Mars-Backward-Loop-and-Close-Approaches.md), on
    Mars's backward loop, helps.

    **Levels:** 2.1 to 2.3 are beginner; 2.4 to 2.8 are intermediate; 2.9 is advanced; 2.10 is expert.

## 2.1 Five facts to explain { #facts }

The word planet comes from the Greek for "wanderers": five lights, Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn, that move
among the fixed stars (book 1, [section 13.1](../night-sky/13-Finding-the-Planets.md#wanderers)). Watch them for a few
years and you find five things that need explaining.

1. **They keep to one road.** All five stay close to the ecliptic, the Sun's yearly path through the zodiac (book 1,
   [section 13.2](../night-sky/13-Finding-the-Planets.md#ecliptic)).
2. **Each has its own pace.** On average Mars gets once round the zodiac in about 2 years, Jupiter in 12 and Saturn in
   29½. Mercury and Venus go round in a year on average, keeping pace with the Sun.
3. **Mercury and Venus stay near the Sun.** Mercury is never more than 28° from it and Venus never more than 47°, so
   both are morning or evening stars (book 1, [section 13.3](../night-sky/13-Finding-the-Planets.md#inner)).
4. **Mars, Jupiter and Saturn loop backwards.** Each time one of them is opposite the Sun in our sky, it stops, goes
   backwards for weeks, stops again and carries on. It is brightest then (book 1,
   [chapter 14](../night-sky/14-Mars-Backward-Loop-and-Close-Approaches.md#loop)).
5. **They are not steady.** Even apart from the loops, a planet moves faster through some constellations than others
   (section 2.2).

The first fact has a simple modern answer: the planets' orbits lie almost in one flat plane with the Earth's. Mars's
orbit is tilted only 1.85° to the Earth's, Jupiter's 1.30°, Saturn's 2.49° and Venus's 3.39°; only Mercury's, at 7.0°,
is tilted more. The others take the rest of this chapter.

## 2.2 Two kinds of unevenness { #uneven }

On average every planet drifts eastward through the zodiac. Two quite different things spoil the steady pace.

**The first is tied to the Sun.** Mars loops backwards whenever it is opposite the Sun, about every 780 days, wherever
in the zodiac that happens. Venus and Mercury swing out to one side of the Sun, back past it, and out to the other.
Whatever is going on, the Sun is part of it.

**The second is tied to the stars.** Leave the loops aside and look only at where Mars is each time it is opposite the
Sun. If Mars moved at a steady pace, each opposition would come the same step farther round, 48.7°. It does not.

The steps run from 77° down to 34° and back up again. Mars hurries through one part of the sky, around Aquarius
and Pisces, and dawdles through the opposite part, around Leo and Virgo, every time round.

Today we know what each one is. The first is our own motion: we watch from a moving Earth (section 2.5). The second
is Mars's: its orbit is not a circle centred on the Sun, and Mars moves faster when it is nearer the Sun (chapter 3).
But for two thousand years nobody knew this, and every model had to copy both.

## 2.3 Counting the cycles { #cycles }

Long before anyone drew an orbit, people learned to predict the planets by counting.

- **Babylon.** Scribes recorded Venus's appearances for years on end. Its pattern repeats every eight years,
  because five of its cycles fit almost exactly into eight years (history book,
  [chapter 2, section 2.5](../history/02-Mesopotamia.md#venus)). In the last four centuries BC, scholars in Babylon
  and Uruk built tables of pure arithmetic, with no circles at all, that gave where the Moon and planets would be on
  any date (history book, [section 2.10](../history/02-Mesopotamia.md#tables)). One of their tricks, a speed that
  changes from one part of the zodiac to the next, copies the second kind of unevenness.
- **The Maya.** The Dresden Codex tracks Venus through its 584-day cycle and Mars through its 780-day cycle (history
  book, [chapter 11, section 11.3](../history/11-The-Americas.md#dresden)).
- **China.** Court astronomers watched where Mars stopped to start and end its loops, because a loop in the wrong
  place was a terrible omen (history book, [chapter 4, section 4.8](../history/04-China.md#mars)).
- **India.** Aryabhata, in AD 499, gave the number of times each planet goes round in a great cycle of 4.32 million
  years (history book, [chapter 5, section 5.5](../history/05-India.md#aryabhata)). Whole numbers of turns over such a
  long time give very exact periods.

Counting goes a long way. Mars, for example, comes back to almost the same place in the sky on almost the same date
after 79 years (section 2.9). But a cycle only says *when*; it says nothing about *why*. For that, people needed a
picture of how the planets actually move.

??? note "Sources"
    - The Venus cycle, the arithmetic tables with their speeds that change from one part of the zodiac to another,
      the Dresden Codex, Mars in China and Aryabhata: this site's history book, chapters 2, 11, 4 and 5, and their
      sources. That the changing speeds copy the second kind of unevenness is our reading.
    - Babylonian planetary models as mostly arithmetical, without geometry: Wikipedia,
      [Babylonian astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Babylonian_astronomy) (weaker).
    - Aryabhata's planetary revolutions in a mahayuga of 4.32 million years: Wikipedia,
      [Aryabhatiya](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aryabhatiya) (weaker).
    - The 79-year cycle of Mars: our arithmetic, section 2.9.

## 2.4 Circles on circles { #epicycles }

Greek astronomers wanted a picture, and they built it from circles. In the version Ptolemy set out in his *Almagest*
around AD 150, the Earth is in the middle. Each planet rides on a small circle, the **epicycle**, and the centre of the
epicycle travels round a large circle, the **deferent** (history book,
[chapter 7, section 7.8](../history/07-Greece-and-Alexandria.md#ptolemy)). When the planet is on the near side of its
epicycle, the two motions work against each other, and it seems to go backwards. It is then also at its closest, and
brightest (book 1, [section 14.5](../night-sky/14-Mars-Backward-Loop-and-Close-Approaches.md#models)).

Ptolemy worked out each planet on its own. For each one he found only the *ratio* of epicycle to deferent; he had no way
to measure how far away any planet was, apart from the Moon. But the planets were linked in one odd way. For Mars,
Jupiter and Saturn, the line from the epicycle's centre to the planet always pointed the same way as the line from the
Earth to the Sun. Every epicycle turned once a year, in step with the Sun.

*Figure B. Why the epicycle turns once a year.*

Figure B shows why. The arrow from the Earth to Mars can be made in two ways: from the Earth to the Sun and then from
the Sun to Mars, or from the Earth out along the deferent and then round the epicycle. The pieces are the same, taken
in the other order. Ptolemy's epicycle for Mars is the Earth's own orbit, moved onto Mars. (For Mercury and Venus it
is the other way round: their deferent is the Earth's orbit, and their epicycle is their own orbit.)

Indian astronomy found the same link. Aryabhata's model also put the Earth in the middle, with epicycles, and each
planet's motion included a basic period in relation to the Sun. Some historians see this as a sign of a Sun-centred
picture hidden underneath.

## 2.5 Turning it inside out { #inside }

In 1543 Nicolaus Copernicus took Figure B from right to left (history book,
[chapter 13](../history/13-Copernicus-and-Maragha.md#book)). Put the Sun in the middle, and the Sun's own yearly
circle round the Earth, together with the yearly circles of all five planets, become one circle: the Earth's orbit.
The loops of Mars, Jupiter and Saturn become the Earth overtaking them on the inside track (book 1,
[section 14.2](../night-sky/14-Mars-Backward-Loop-and-Close-Approaches.md#why)).

Best of all, the ratios now mean something. For an outer planet, the epicycle is the Earth's orbit and the deferent is
the planet's, so the ratio between them gives the planet's distance from the Sun in units of the Earth's. For the first
time the planets had a scale.

| Planet | Copernicus's distance | Modern value |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mercury | 0.38 | 0.387 |
| Venus | 0.72 | 0.723 |
| Earth | 1 | 1 |
| Mars | 1.52 | 1.524 |
| Jupiter | 5.22 | 5.20 |
| Saturn | 9.17 | 9.57 |

*Distances from the Sun, in units of the Earth's distance (astronomical units).*

Ptolemy had guessed the distances too, later, in a book called the *Planetary Hypotheses*, by assuming that each
planet's circles fit snugly inside the next. He put the Sun 1,210 Earth radii away, almost 20 times too close, and
Mars 5,040 Earth radii, about 7 times too close. Copernicus's distances are relative ones only, but they have the right
shape: once one distance in kilometres is known, all the rest follow (chapter 12).

Copernicus still used circles. To replace Ptolemy's equant he used small extra circles, tools invented earlier by
astronomers at Maragha in Persia and by Ibn al-Shatir of Damascus (history book,
[section 13.4](../history/13-Copernicus-and-Maragha.md#maragha)). How he found the
distances is in the history book, [section 13.10](../history/13-Copernicus-and-Maragha.md#working), and in section 2.10.

## 2.6 Farther is slower { #slower }

With the distances known, a pattern jumps out.

| Planet | Distance from the Sun (AU) | Year (Earth years) | Average speed (km/s) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mercury | 0.387 | 0.241 | 47.4 |
| Venus | 0.723 | 0.615 | 35.0 |
| Earth | 1 | 1 | 29.8 |
| Mars | 1.524 | 1.881 | 24.1 |
| Jupiter | 5.20 | 11.86 | 13.1 |
| Saturn | 9.57 | 29.45 | 9.7 |

The farther a planet is from the Sun, the longer its path round, but that is not the whole story: it also moves more
slowly. Saturn is 9.6 times farther out than the Earth, so its path is 9.6 times longer, yet its year is 29 times
longer, because it travels at only a third of the Earth's speed. Mars is in between: half as far again as the Earth,
with a year nearly twice as long. The physics lesson
[Slower farther out](https://physics.akadian.ca/topics/gravity/level-4/03-slower-farther-out.html) shows why gravity
makes it so.

There is an exact rule hidden in this table. Johannes Kepler found it in 1618, and it is the third of his three laws
(chapter 3).

## 2.7 The planet that broke the circles { #mars-breaks }

Circles, even with epicycles and offsets, could copy the planets well enough for most purposes. They could not copy
Mars. Its orbit is the most stretched of the five planets the old astronomers knew, apart from Mercury, and Mercury is
so close to the Sun that it is hard to watch at all.

| Planet | Mercury | Venus | Earth | Mars | Jupiter | Saturn |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Eccentricity | 0.206 | 0.007 | 0.017 | 0.093 | 0.049 | 0.052 |

*Eccentricity measures how stretched an orbit is: 0 is a circle (chapter 4).*

Copernicus's own observations of Mars were sometimes out by more than two degrees (history book,
[section 13.9](../history/13-Copernicus-and-Maragha.md#mars)). The Danish astronomer Tycho Brahe changed that. Working
without a telescope, with large instruments on his island observatory, he measured the planets to about one minute of
arc, roughly five times better than anyone before him (history book,
[chapter 14](../history/14-Tycho-and-Kepler.md#tycho)). After Tycho died in 1601, his assistant Johannes Kepler tried
to fit a circle to Tycho's Mars. The best circle he could find still missed by 8 minutes of arc in places, eight times
Tycho's error. Kepler would not ignore it, and gave up circles altogether (history book,
[section 14.7](../history/14-Tycho-and-Kepler.md#mars)). Chapter 3 follows what he found.

    - Eccentricities: NASA fact sheets (above).

## 2.8 The puzzle from Mars { #mars }

A Martian astronomer would face the same puzzle, with the parts in different sizes.

**The first kind of unevenness** is there too. For a Martian the Earth is an inner planet: it never gets more than 41°
from the Sun, and it loops backwards each time it passes between Mars and the Sun, every 780 days (book 1,
[section 14.7](../night-sky/14-Mars-Backward-Loop-and-Close-Approaches.md#mars)). Jupiter and Saturn loop too.

**The second kind is much bigger.** Mars's orbit is far more stretched than the Earth's, so from Mars even the Sun
moves unevenly through the stars. Seen from the Earth, the Sun runs at most 1.9° ahead of or behind a steady pace.
Seen from Mars it runs up to 10.7° ahead or behind (section 2.9). A Martian would notice it at once in the seasons:
northern spring on Mars lasts 194 sols and autumn only 142 (history book,
[section 14.9](../history/14-Tycho-and-Kepler.md#onmars)). On Mars, the clue that orbits are not circles is written
on the calendar.

**The 780-day rhythm matters for travel.** The time between one meeting of the Earth and Mars and the next is also how
often the best chances to fly from one to the other come round: about once every 26 months (physics lesson
[Launch windows](https://physics.akadian.ca/topics/gravity/level-7/06-launch-windows.html); chapter 15).

## 2.9 Working it out { #working }

**The mean step between oppositions.** In 779.94 days Mars goes round the Sun $779.94 / 686.98 = 1.1353$ times. So each
opposition happens, on average, $0.1353 \times 360^\circ = 48.7^\circ$ farther round than the last. Figure A shows the
real steps running from 34° to 77°.

**How far ahead or behind.** A planet on a slightly stretched orbit runs ahead of a steady pace on one side of its orbit
and falls behind on the other. The largest gap, for an orbit of eccentricity $e$, is $e + \arcsin e$ radians, which is
very nearly $2e$. For Mars, $e = 0.0935$:

$$0.0935 + \arcsin 0.0935 = 0.0935 + 0.0936 = 0.1871 \text{ radians} = 10.7^\circ.$$

For the Earth, $e = 0.0167$ gives 0.0334 radians, or 1.9°.

**Mars's 79-year cycle.** In 79 years the Earth goes round the Sun 79 times. If Mars goes round 42 times in the same
time, the Earth has gained $79 - 42 = 37$ laps on it, so there have been 37 oppositions. Check it in days:

- $79 \times 365.256 = 28{,}855$ days,
- $42 \times 686.98 = 28{,}853$ days,
- $37 \times 779.94 = 28{,}858$ days.

All three agree to within a few days, so after 79 years Mars is back in almost the same place, on almost the same
date.

**Ptolemy's scale.** His Sun at 1,210 Earth radii against the true 23,480 is $23{,}480 / 1{,}210 = 19.4$ times too
close; his Mars at 5,040 against 35,780 is 7.1 times too close.

| | Mars | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Year against the stars | 686.98 days | 365.256 days |
| Tilt of the orbit to the Earth's | 1.85° | 0° |
| Eccentricity of the orbit | 0.0935 | 0.0167 |
| Most the Sun runs ahead of or behind a steady pace, seen from here | 10.7° | 1.9° |
| Longest and shortest season | 194 and 142 sols | 93.65 and 88.99 days |

## 2.10 Copernicus's yardstick for the outer planets { #quadrature }

The history book shows how Copernicus found the distance of an inner planet from its greatest elongation. For an outer
planet he timed it instead.

Start at opposition, with the Sun, the Earth and Mars in a line. The Earth, on the inside, gains on Mars by

$$360^\circ \times \left(\frac{1}{365.256} - \frac{1}{686.98}\right) = 0.4616^\circ \text{ a day}.$$

Now wait until Mars stands at right angles to the Sun in our sky, 90° east of it, at **quadrature**. At that moment the
triangle Sun, Earth, Mars has a right angle at the Earth. If the wait was $t$ days, the angle at the Sun is
$\theta = 0.4616^\circ \times t$, and

$$\cos\theta = \frac{\text{Earth's distance}}{\text{Mars's distance}}, \qquad r = \frac{1}{\cos\theta}.$$

For Mars the wait is about 106 days: $\theta = 49.0^\circ$, $\cos\theta = 0.656$ and $r = 1.524$. The same method works
for Jupiter and Saturn:

| Planet | Earth gains per day | Wait from opposition to quadrature | Angle at the Sun | Distance |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Mars | 0.4616° | 106.1 days | 48.98° | 1.524 |
| Jupiter | 0.9025° | 87.4 days | 78.92° | 5.20 |
| Saturn | 0.9521° | 88.2 days | 84.00° | 9.57 |

**Why Saturn is hard.** Suppose the moment of quadrature is timed one day wrong. For Mars, the angle changes by 0.46°
and the distance by only 0.014, under 1%. For Saturn the angle is close to 90°, where the cosine is tiny and changes
fast, and the same one-day error moves the distance by 1.5, about 16%. The farthest planet is the hardest to measure
this way, and Copernicus's Saturn is indeed the farthest from the modern value (9.17 against 9.57).

The method also assumes circular orbits. Because Mars's real orbit is stretched, the answer changes depending on where
in its orbit the opposition happens. That was exactly the problem Kepler had to solve.

      source, University of Oregon, Copernican estimates of orbital sizes (above).
    - Periods and distances: NASA fact sheets (above). The table and the error estimates are our arithmetic, for
      circular orbits. The sensitivity is $\Delta r = (\sin\theta / \cos^2\theta)\,\Delta\theta$.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Why do the planets always appear close to the ecliptic?

??? note "Answer"
    Their orbits lie almost in the same plane as the Earth's, and the ecliptic is that plane seen from the Earth.
    Mars's orbit is tilted only 1.85° to ours.

**2.** (beginner) Name two peoples who predicted planets by counting cycles, and a planet each of them followed.

??? note "Answer"
    Any two of: the Babylonians (Venus, with its 8-year pattern), the Maya (Venus, 584 days, and Mars, 780 days), the
    Chinese (Mars and its loops), Indian astronomers such as Aryabhata (all the planets, by their turns in 4.32 million
    years).

**3.** (beginner) Mars loops backwards every 780 days. Is that the first or the second kind of unevenness, and why?

??? note "Answer"
    The first kind. It is tied to the Sun: Mars loops when it is opposite the Sun, wherever in the zodiac that is. It
    comes from the Earth overtaking Mars.

**4.** (intermediate) Mars's oppositions come up to 77° apart around Aquarius and Pisces, but only 34° apart around
Leo and Virgo. What does that tell
you about Mars's orbit?

??? note "Answer"
    Mars moves faster along one part of its orbit than the opposite part. Its orbit is not a circle centred on the
    Sun; Mars is nearer the Sun, and faster, on the Aquarius and Pisces side.

**5.** (intermediate) Why did every one of Ptolemy's epicycles for Mars, Jupiter and Saturn turn once a year?

??? note "Answer"
    Because each epicycle is really the Earth's own orbit, moved onto the planet. Going round it once is the Earth
    going round the Sun once.

**6.** (intermediate) Saturn is 9.57 times farther from the Sun than the Earth. If it moved at the Earth's speed, how
long would its year be? It is really 29.45 years. What does that tell you?

??? note "Answer"
    Its path is 9.57 times longer, so at the Earth's speed it would take 9.57 years. It takes $29.45 / 9.57 = 3.08$
    times longer still, so it moves 3.08 times more slowly: $29.78 / 3.08 = 9.7$ km/s, as in the table in section 2.6.

**7.** (advanced) Venus passes between us and the Sun 5 times in 8 years. How many times has it gone round the Sun?
Check it with Venus's year of 224.70 days.

??? note "Answer"
    Venus is the faster planet, so each meeting means Venus has gained one lap on the Earth. In 8 years the Earth
    goes round 8 times, so Venus goes round $8 + 5 = 13$ times. Check: $13 \times 224.70 = 2{,}921$ days, and
    $8 \times 365.256 = 2{,}922$ days.

**8.** (expert) An observer times Jupiter's quadrature at 88 days after opposition, instead of the true 87.4. What
distance do they get, and how far off is it?

??? note "Answer"
    $\theta = 88 \times 0.9025^\circ = 79.42^\circ$, $\cos\theta = 0.1836$, so $r = 1 / 0.1836 = 5.45$. The true value
    is 5.20, so a timing error of just over half a day gives a distance about 5% too large.

## Summary

- The planets keep to the zodiac because their orbits lie almost in one plane; each has its own pace round the sky.
- Their motion is uneven in two separate ways: loops tied to the Sun, and speeding up and slowing down tied to the
  stars.
- The first comes from our moving Earth; the second from orbits that are not circles centred on the Sun.
- People in Babylon, the Americas, China and India predicted the planets by counting cycles.
- Ptolemy copied the motions with epicycles, and every outer planet's epicycle turned once a year: it was the Earth's
  orbit in disguise.
- With the Sun in the middle, Copernicus turned the ratios of the circles into real distances: Mars 1.52, Jupiter 5.22
  times the Earth's distance.
- The farther a planet is from the Sun, the slower it moves.
- Mars, with the most stretched orbit that could be watched well, broke the circles once Tycho measured it to a minute
  of arc.
- From Mars the second kind of unevenness is much bigger: the Martian seasons are 194 and 142 sols long.

into ellipses and give three rules that every orbit obeys.
