# 18. Einstein and orbits

*Newton's law of gravity steered the Voyagers to Neptune and Perseverance to Jezero. But it is not quite the whole
story. Mercury's orbit turns a little faster than Newton allows, starlight bends round the Sun twice as much as he
would predict, radio signals that skim the Sun arrive late, and clocks run at different speeds in different places.
Einstein's general relativity, published in 1915, explains all of these. This chapter shows what it changes for
orbits, how each change was measured, and why a clock on Mars gains about half a millisecond a day.*

## 18.1 Mercury's leftover 43 arcseconds { #mercury }

Chapter 10 ([section 10.4](10-Wobbling-Orbits-and-the-Finding-of-Neptune.md#turning)) left a puzzle. Mercury's
perihelion, its closest point to the Sun, creeps round a little each orbit. The pulls of the other planets explain most
of it, 532 arcseconds a century. But in 1859 Urbain Le Verrier, going through timed transits of Mercury across the Sun
from 1697 to 1848, found that some was left over. Simon Newcomb measured it again in 1882: **43 arcseconds a
century**. Le Verrier blamed an unseen planet, Vulcan, which was never found
([section 10.5](10-Wobbling-Orbits-and-the-Finding-of-Neptune.md#failures)).

In November 1915 Albert Einstein worked out what his new theory of gravity did to Mercury. Without any new planet and
without adjusting anything, it gave 43 arcseconds a century. That was the first success of **general relativity**.

The effect is tiny. By itself, it would take about **3 million years** to turn Mercury's orbit once round. But it had
been measured, and Newton's law could not explain it.

      Wikipedia, [Tests of general relativity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tests_of_general_relativity) (weaker).
      532″ from the planets: chapter 10, section 10.4.
    - Einstein's 1915 theory explaining the 43″: chapter 10, section 10.5, and the same Wikipedia article, which lists

    - 3 million years: $1{,}296{,}000'' / 43''$ centuries, our arithmetic.

## 18.2 Gravity as bent space and time { #idea }

Newton pictured gravity as a pull reaching across empty space. Einstein pictured it differently. A heavy body like the
Sun **bends space and time** around it, and planets, spacecraft and light all travel along the straightest paths they
can through that bent space and time. Far from the Sun, where the bending is slight, the result is almost exactly
Newton's law. Close in, there are small differences.

- **Orbits do not quite close.** Each time round, the ellipse turns a little, most of all close to the Sun. Mercury,
  the closest planet, shows it best.
- **Light bends.** Starlight passing the Sun is bent by twice the amount you would get by treating light as a stream
  of tiny bullets falling under Newton's gravity.
- **Light is delayed.** A signal passing close to the Sun takes slightly longer than it would through empty, flat space.
- **Clocks run at different speeds.** A clock deeper in a gravity well runs slower than one higher up. Earlier, in
  1905, Einstein's special relativity had shown that a moving clock runs slower too.

None of this can be felt in daily life. All of it has now been measured, and spacecraft and satellites have to allow
for it.

    - Light bent by twice the Newtonian value: Wikipedia,

## 18.3 Starlight bent by the Sun { #light }

As early as 1801, Johann Georg von Soldner worked out that Newton's gravity should bend starlight passing the Sun.
In 1911 Einstein got the same small amount. By 1915 his finished theory gave twice as much: **1.75 arcseconds** for a
star seen just at the Sun's edge. The only time stars can be seen right next to the Sun is during a total eclipse.

**1914, the Crimea.** Three expeditions set out to test it at the eclipse of 21 August 1914: one led by Erwin
Finlay-Freundlich of the Berlin Observatory, one by William Wallace Campbell of the Lick Observatory in the United
States, and one by Charles Perrine of the Argentine National Observatory at Córdoba. But the First World War broke
out. The German astronomers were sent home or taken prisoner by the Russians, and clouds spoiled the view for the
others. Had they succeeded, they would have found Einstein's 1911 number wrong.

**1919, Príncipe and Sobral.** After the war, the British astronomers Frank Dyson and Arthur Eddington sent two teams
to the total eclipse of 29 May 1919: one to the island of Príncipe off West Africa, the other to the town of Sobral in
Brazil. Eddington, on Príncipe, wrote that he hardly saw the eclipse at all, he was so busy changing photographic
plates. The results, announced in London on 6 November 1919, agreed with Einstein's larger number, and newspapers in
Britain, the United States and Germany made Einstein famous almost overnight.

Today radio telescopes measure the bending far more precisely, and it agrees with Einstein.

    - Soldner in 1801, Einstein's 1911 value the same as Soldner's from the equivalence principle alone, and the 1915

    - The three 1914 expeditions from Germany, the United States and Argentina, Finlay-Freundlich, Campbell and

## 18.4 Radio signals running late { #shapiro }

In 1964 the physicist Irwin Shapiro noticed a fourth test. In his paper *Fourth Test of General
Relativity* he worked out that a radar signal passing close to the Sun should take slightly longer than expected, by up
to about 200 millionths of a second for a round trip to a planet behind the Sun. He proposed bouncing radar off Venus
and Mercury to look for it, and the delay was found.

Spacecraft do better than radar echoes, because they send back a clean, timed signal. When Mars went behind the Sun in
1976, the ranges to the **Viking landers** (chapter 12,
[section 12.8](12-Measuring-Orbits.md#mars)) showed signals arriving up to about **250 millionths of a second** late.

## 18.5 Clocks in orbit { #clocks }

A clock higher up in a gravity well runs faster than one lower down. A clock that moves fast runs slower. Both effects
are tiny, but modern clocks can see them.

**Gravity Probe A.** On 18 June 1976 a Scout rocket from Wallops Island in Virginia carried a hydrogen maser, a very
steady clock, 10,000 km up and back in 1 hour 55 minutes. Higher up, it ran faster, by just the amount Einstein
predicts, to about 70 parts in a million.

**GPS.** Each GPS satellite carries atomic clocks, and a receiver works out where it is from how long their signals
take to arrive (chapter 14, [section 14.6](14-Orbits-Around-the-Earth.md#gnss)). Seen from the ground:

- the satellite is higher up, in weaker gravity, so its clocks gain **45.8 millionths of a second a day**;
- it moves at about 3.9 km/s, so its clocks lose **7.2**;
- together, they gain **38.6 millionths of a second a day**.

Light travels about 11 km in that time, so if nobody allowed for it, GPS positions would drift by kilometres every day.
So before launch each satellite's clock is set to tick slightly slow: 10.22999999543 MHz instead of 10.23 MHz. Once in
orbit, relativity speeds it up to exactly the right rate. Figure B, in section 18.8, shows the sums.

??? note "Sources"
    - Gravity Probe A: launched on 18 June 1976 from Wallops Island on a Scout rocket, a hydrogen maser, 10,000 km,
      1 hour 55 minutes, agreement to about 70 parts per million: Wikipedia,
      [Gravity Probe A](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Gravity_Probe_A) (weaker).
    - GPS: +45.8 μs a day from height, −7.2 μs from speed, +38.6 μs in all; the clock set to 10.22999999543 MHz

## 18.6 The Moon, pulsars and black holes { #beyond }

**The Moon.** On 21 July 1969 the crew of Apollo 11 left a set of mirrors on the Moon. More followed: from Apollo 14
and 15, from the Soviet robot rovers Lunokhod 1 and 2, and from India's Chandrayaan-3. Observatories fire laser pulses
at them and time the echoes, and they now measure the distance to the Moon to within millimetres. That shows the Moon
moving away by 3.8 cm a year (chapter 8, [section 8.5](08-Tides.md#slowing)). It also checks that the Earth and the
Moon fall towards the Sun in exactly the same way, as Einstein's theory says they must, even though they are made
differently.

**A pair of pulsars.** In 1974 Russell Hulse and Joseph Taylor, using the 305 m Arecibo radio dish, found a pulsar going
round another star every 7.75 hours. Over the years since, the orbit has been shrinking, at exactly the rate general
relativity predicts for two stars losing energy as **gravitational waves**, ripples in space and time. They won the
1993 Nobel Prize in Physics.

**Two black holes.** On 14 September 2015 the two LIGO detectors in the United States caught gravitational waves
directly for the first time. They came from two black holes, of 36 and 29 times the Sun's mass, spiralling together
and merging more than a billion years ago. The announcement, with Europe's Virgo team, came on 11 February 2016.

**A star round a black hole.** At the centre of our galaxy, the star **S2** orbits a black hole of 4 million Suns. In
2018 it passed only 120 AU from it at 7,650 km/s, 2.5% of the speed of light. Its orbit turns, just as Mercury's does,
by the amount general relativity predicts.

## 18.7 Time, tables and the AU { #tables }

If clocks tick at different rates in different places, which clock tells the time for the whole solar system? A clock
on the Earth sits deep in the Sun's gravity well and moves at 30 km/s, so it runs slow compared with one far out and
still. Astronomers therefore keep separate time scales: one for clocks on the Earth, and one for the solar system as a
whole, and they convert between them.

The great tables of planet positions (chapter 12, [section 12.7](12-Measuring-Orbits.md#ephemeris)) are worked out
with Einstein's corrections built in. Without them, the radio ranges to Mars landers and orbiters would not fit.

This is also why the AU was fixed in metres in 2012 (chapter 12, [section 12.6](12-Measuring-Orbits.md#au)). The old
definition tied the AU to how fast a small body would go round the Sun, which meant tying it to a time scale and to
the Sun's mass. With relativity built in, that became too tangled, so the IAU simply made the AU exactly
149,597,870,700 m.

??? note "Sources"
    - Separate time scales for the Earth and for the solar system's centre of mass, and converting between them:
      standard (Terrestrial Time and Barycentric Dynamical Time).
    - Relativistic corrections in the planetary tables: chapter 12, section 12.7, citing Folkner and others, DE430.
    - The 2012 redefinition because building in relativity would have made the old definition too complex: chapter
      12, section 12.6, citing Wikipedia, [Astronomical unit](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomical_unit)
      (weaker).

## 18.8 Einstein at Mars { #mars }

**Mars's orbit turns too.** General relativity turns Mars's perihelion by about **1.35 arcseconds a century**, a
thirtieth of Mercury's 43, because Mars is four times farther from the Sun and moves more slowly. The pulls of the
other planets turn it far more (chapter 10,
[section 10.7](10-Wobbling-Orbits-and-the-Finding-of-Neptune.md#mars)), but the tables of chapter 12 include the
small extra part, and the Mars ranges need it.

**Clocks on Mars run fast.** In December 2025 Neil Ashby and Bijunath Patla of NIST, the United States' measurement
laboratory, published a careful study of time on Mars. A clock on Mars gains on a clock on the Earth by an average of
**477 millionths of a second a day**, almost half a millisecond. The gain changes through the Mars year, by up to
226 millionths of a second a day.

- Mars moves more slowly round the Sun, 24 km/s against 30: about +147.

Our simple total is 488, close to NIST's 477. The biggest part depends on Mars's distance from the Sun, which changes
along its oval orbit, so it is no surprise that the gain changes through the Mars year. Future explorers who want to
navigate on Mars with satellites, as we do with GPS, or keep Mars and Earth networks in step, will have to allow for
it.

## 18.9 Working it out { #working }

Use $GM_\odot = 1.327 \times 10^{20}$ m³/s² for the Sun, $c = 2.998 \times 10^8$ m/s and 1 AU $= 1.496 \times
10^{11}$ m.

**An orbit's turning.** Each time round, general relativity turns an orbit by

$$\Delta\varphi = \frac{6\pi\,GM}{c^2\,a\,(1 - e^2)} \text{ radians}.$$

- **Mercury:** $a = 0.3871$ AU, $e = 0.2056$: $\Delta\varphi = 5.02 \times 10^{-7}$ rad an orbit. In a century it goes
  round $36{,}525 / 87.97 = 415.2$ times: $2.08 \times 10^{-4}$ rad, which is **43.0″**.
- **Mars:** $a = 1.5237$ AU, $e = 0.0934$: $1.23 \times 10^{-7}$ rad an orbit, 53.2 orbits a century: **1.35″**.

**Bending at the Sun's edge.** $\theta = 4GM/(c^2 R)$ with $R = 6.957 \times 10^8$ m:
$\theta = 8.49 \times 10^{-6}$ rad $=$ **1.75″**.

**Clocks.** A clock where the gravitational potential is $-GM/r$ and the speed is $v$ runs at the rate
$1 - GM/(rc^2) - v^2/(2c^2)$, compared with a distant clock at rest. For GPS, $r = 26{,}562$ km and
$v = \sqrt{GM_\oplus/r} = 3.87$ km/s, with $GM_\oplus = 3.986 \times 10^{14}$ m³/s² and the ground at 6,378 km:

- height: $\dfrac{GM_\oplus}{c^2}\left(\dfrac{1}{6{,}378{,}000} - \dfrac{1}{26{,}562{,}000}\right) = 5.29 \times
  10^{-10}$, or $+45.7$ μs a day;
- speed: $v^2/(2c^2) = 8.35 \times 10^{-11}$, or $-7.2$ μs a day;
- together $+38.5$ μs a day, which takes light $38.5 \times 10^{-6} \times c = 11.5$ km.

**Mars against the Earth**, circular orbits, in μs a day ($\times 86{,}400 \times 10^6$):

- own pull: $(GM_\oplus/R_\oplus - GM_♂/R_♂)/c^2 = (6.25 - 1.26) \times 10^7 / c^2$, giving $+47.9$;
- the Sun's pull: $GM_\odot (1/1\,\text{AU} - 1/1.5237\,\text{AU})/c^2 = 3.39 \times 10^{-9}$, giving $+293.1$;
- speed: half of that for circular orbits, since $v^2 = GM/r$: $+146.6$.

## 18.10 The delay, worked out { #derive }

**Where the delay comes from.** In general relativity, light seen from far away moves a little slower than $c$ where
gravity is strong: at a distance $r$ from a mass $M$, at about $c\,(1 - 2GM/(rc^2))$. Add up the extra time along the
whole path and the delay for one way, for a path passing at closest distance $b$ between points at distances $r_1$
and $r_2$ from the Sun, is close to

$$\Delta t_{\text{one way}} \approx \frac{2GM}{c^3}\ln\frac{4 r_1 r_2}{b^2}.$$

For radar or ranging the signal goes there and back, so the delay doubles:

$$\Delta t \approx \frac{4GM}{c^3}\ln\frac{4 r_1 r_2}{b^2}.$$

**The numbers.** $4GM_\odot/c^3 = 19.7$ μs. For the Earth at $r_1 = 1$ AU and Mars at $r_2 = 1.5237$ AU:

- grazing the Sun, $b = 6.957 \times 10^8$ m: $4 r_1 r_2 / b^2 = 2.82 \times 10^5$, $\ln = 12.6$, so
  $\Delta t = 247$ μs;
- at $b = 10$ solar radii: $\ln = 8.0$, so $\Delta t = 157$ μs.

These are figure A's numbers, and the first is Viking's "up to about 250 μs". The delay changes slowly with $b$,
because of the logarithm: moving the path ten times farther from the Sun only cuts the delay from 247 to 157 μs.

**How the clock formula is used.** Two clocks with potentials $\Phi_1, \Phi_2$ and speeds $v_1, v_2$ differ in rate by

$$\frac{\Delta f}{f} = \frac{\Phi_2 - \Phi_1}{c^2} - \frac{v_2^2 - v_1^2}{2c^2},$$

with $\Phi = -GM/r$ summed over every body that pulls. Section 18.9 used it for GPS and for Mars.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) What did Le Verrier find wrong with Mercury's orbit, and what did he think caused it?

??? note "Answer"
    Its perihelion turned about 43 arcseconds a century more than the other planets could explain. He blamed an
    unseen planet, Vulcan, which does not exist.

**2.** (beginner) Why did the 1919 test of Einstein's theory need a total eclipse?

??? note "Answer"
    The bending is largest for stars seen right next to the Sun, and those stars can only be photographed when the
    Moon blocks the Sun's glare.

**3.** (beginner) Does a clock on a GPS satellite run faster or slower than one on the ground, and by how much?

??? note "Answer"
    Faster, by about 38 millionths of a second a day: it gains 46 from being higher up and loses 7 from moving fast.

**4.** (intermediate) Why were the Viking landers good for testing the delay of light by the Sun?

??? note "Answer"
    They sat still on Mars and answered radio signals, so the distance between the Earth and Mars could be measured
    very precisely. When Mars went behind the Sun in 1976, the signals passed close to the Sun and arrived up to about
    250 millionths of a second late.

**5.** (intermediate) What did the binary pulsar found by Hulse and Taylor show?

??? note "Answer"
    Its orbit shrinks at exactly the rate general relativity predicts for two stars losing energy as gravitational
    waves.

**6.** (intermediate) Give two reasons why a clock on Mars runs faster than one on the Earth.

??? note "Answer"
    Any two of: Mars is farther from the Sun, higher in the Sun's gravity well; Mars moves more slowly round the Sun;
    Mars's own pull at its ground is weaker than the Earth's.

**7.** (advanced) Find relativity's turn of the Earth's perihelion per century ($a = 1$ AU, $e = 0.0167$).

??? note "Answer"
    With the values of section 18.9, $\Delta\varphi = 6\pi GM/(c^2 a (1 - e^2)) = 1.86 \times 10^{-7}$ rad an orbit;
    100 orbits a century give $1.86 \times 10^{-5}$ rad, about **3.8″**.

**8.** (expert) A spacecraft beyond the Sun at 1 AU on the far side ($r_1 = r_2 = 1$ AU) sends signals that pass
4 solar radii from the Sun. Estimate the round-trip delay.

??? note "Answer"
    $4 r_1 r_2 / b^2 = 4 \times (1.496 \times 10^{11})^2 / (2.783 \times 10^9)^2 = 1.156 \times 10^4$, $\ln = 9.36$,
    so $\Delta t = 19.7 \times 9.36 = 184$ μs.

## Summary

- General relativity explained Mercury's leftover 43″ a century in 1915, with no new planet.
- Einstein pictured gravity as bent space and time: orbits do not quite close, light bends and is delayed, and clocks
  run at different rates.
- The eclipse expeditions of 1919 to Príncipe and Sobral saw starlight bent by about Einstein's 1.75″.
- Radio signals passing the Sun run late: Viking measured up to about 250 μs, and Cassini matched Einstein to 0.002%.
- GPS clocks gain 38.6 μs a day in orbit and are set slow before launch.
- Laser ranging to the Moon, the Hulse–Taylor pulsar, LIGO's black holes and the star S2 all agree with Einstein.
- Relativity turns Mars's orbit by 1.35″ a century, and a clock on Mars gains about 477 μs a day on the Earth.
