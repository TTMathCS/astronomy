# 8. Tides

*Gravity does not only pull; it stretches. Because the Moon pulls the near side of the Earth harder than the far side,
the oceans rise twice a day on coasts all over the world. This chapter explains why there are two tides and not one,
why some are huge and others small, and how people from Babylon to Brazil worked out the link with the Moon. Then it
follows what tides do over millions of years: they slow the Earth's spin, push the Moon away, lock moons face-on to
their planets, and will one day tear Phobos apart.*

## 8.1 Two tides a day { #two }

The Moon pulls every part of the Earth, but not equally. The side facing the Moon is about 6,400 km nearer to it than
the centre, so it is pulled a little harder; the far side is pulled a little less. The whole Earth is falling towards
the Moon all the time, as chapter 5 showed: the two go round their balance point. Take that shared fall away, and what
is left is a **stretch**.

So there are two bulges of water: one under the Moon and one on the opposite side. The Earth turns under both, and a
coast passes through two high tides and two low tides each day. Because the Moon moves on along its orbit while the
Earth turns, it takes about 24 hours 50 minutes to come back to the same place in the sky. High tides are about
12 hours 25 minutes apart, and come about 50 minutes later each day.

The stretching force is tiny. At the Earth's surface it is about $1.1 \times 10^{-6}$ m/s², roughly a ten-millionth of
the Earth's own gravity. It works because it acts on whole oceans, again and again, twice a day.

## 8.2 Springs and neaps { #springs }

The Sun raises tides too. It is far heavier than the Moon, but also far more distant, and the stretch weakens with the
**cube** of the distance (section 8.9). The Sun's tide on the Earth comes out at 46% of the Moon's.

When the Sun, Moon and Earth are in a line, at new Moon and full Moon, the two tides add up and the range from high to
low water is largest: a **spring tide**, from an old word meaning to leap up, not from the season. At first and last
quarter, when the Sun and Moon are at right angles in the sky, they partly cancel and the range is smallest: a
**neap tide**. The cycle repeats every two weeks. In the simplest picture, spring tides have a range
$(1 + 0.46) / (1 - 0.46) = 2.7$ times that of neap tides.

??? note "Sources"
    - The Sun's tidal force 46% of the Moon's, spring tides at new and full Moon, the name from "jump, burst forth",
      neap tides and the two-week cycle: Wikipedia, Tide (above).
    - The ratio 2.7 is our arithmetic, in the equilibrium picture of section 8.3.

## 8.3 Real tides { #real }

If the whole Earth were covered by deep ocean, the bulges would be well under a metre high. Real tides are shaped by
coasts and sea floors. The water sloshes in each ocean basin like water in a bath, and where a bay is just the right
size and shape, the slosh keeps time with the tide and grows.

- **The Bay of Fundy**, between New Brunswick and Nova Scotia in Canada, has the largest tidal range in the world,
  averaging about 16 m. In each half-day, around 100 billion tonnes of water flows in and out, twice the flow of all
  the world's rivers together.
- **The Qiantang River** in China, flowing into Hangzhou Bay, has the world's largest **tidal bore**, which local
  people call the "Silver Dragon": the front of the incoming tide rises as a wave up to 9 m high and runs up the river.

To predict real tides, the simple picture of two bulges is not enough. Pierre-Simon Laplace treated the oceans as
moving water rather than still bulges, and from 1867 William Thomson, Lord Kelvin, split tide records into a sum of
regular waves, each tied to one motion of the Moon or Sun. He built a **tide-predicting machine** of pulleys that added
the waves together and drew the coming tides.

## 8.4 Who linked the tides to the Moon? { #history }

People who lived by the sea noticed the Moon's hand in the tides long before anyone could explain it.

- Indian and East Asian writings described the sea as breathing or pulsing like a living being.
- The Greek explorer **Pytheas**, sailing to the British Isles around 325 BC, seems to have seen that the size of the
  tides followed the phases of the Moon.
- **Seleucus of Seleucia**, an astronomer of the second century BC on the Tigris, held that the tides were caused by
  the Moon (history book, [section 7.10](../history/07-Greece-and-Alexandria.md#wider)).
- **Abu Ma'shar al-Balkhi**, who died around 886, taught that ebb and flood are caused by the Moon and discussed the
  effect of its phases.
- **Bede**, an English monk, linked the twice-daily tides to the Moon's daily motion and spring and neap tides to its
  phases in 725 (history book, [section 9.2](../history/09-Byzantium-and-Medieval-Europe.md#bede)).
- The oldest known **tide table** was made in China in 1056, for visitors who came to see the Qiantang bore.
- The **Yolŋu** people of Arnhem Land, in northern Australia, link the Moon with the tides in their traditions
  (history book, [section 12.6](../history/12-Oceania-and-Australia.md#aboriginal)).
- The **Tupinambá** of Brazil attributed the tides to the Moon and knew that the highest come at new and full Moon, as
  a French friar recorded in 1614 (history book, [section 11.8](../history/11-The-Americas.md#plains)).

In Europe, Simon Stevin in 1608 and Johannes Kepler in 1609 argued that the Moon's attraction caused the tides, while
Galileo in 1632 wrongly put them down to the Earth's own motions. **Isaac Newton** was the first to explain them as
the result of gravity, with the stretch of section 8.1.

## 8.5 A longer day and a farther Moon { #slowing }

The Earth turns faster than the Moon goes round it, so it drags its tidal bulges a little ahead of the line to the
Moon. The Moon pulls back on the nearer bulge, which slows the Earth's spin, and the bulge pulls the Moon forwards,
which lifts it into a higher orbit. The Earth loses spin, and the Moon gains it.

**The day grows longer.** Timed eclipses recorded in Babylon, China, Greece and the Arab world over 2,700 years show
that the day has been growing longer by about 1.8 thousandths of a second each century (history book,
[section 2.12](../history/02-Mesopotamia.md#rotation)). Tides alone would give 2.3; other changes inside the Earth make
up the difference. Over hundreds of millions of years it adds up. Layers in rocks laid down 620 million years ago show
a day of about 21.9 hours, and about 400 days in a year.

**The Moon moves away.** Astronauts and robots have left mirrors on the Moon: Apollo 11 on 21 July 1969, then
Apollo 14 and 15, the Soviet rovers Lunokhod 1 and 2, and India's Chandrayaan-3. Observatories fire laser pulses at
them and time the echo, measuring the distance to the Moon to within millimetres. The Moon is moving away by
**3.8 cm a year**, about as fast as fingernails grow.

## 8.6 Locked faces, hot moons and broken comets { #locking }

**Locked faces.** Tides act on moons too, and much more strongly, because a planet is so much heavier than a moon. The
Earth's stretch on the Moon is about 22 times the Moon's on the Earth (section 8.8). Long ago it slowed the Moon's spin
until the Moon turned once for each trip round the Earth, so it always shows us the same face. This is **tidal
locking**. Phobos and Deimos are locked to Mars in the same way. Pluto and Charon have gone further: each is locked to
the other, so each always shows the other the same face.

**Hot moons.** A moon on a slightly stretched orbit is squeezed and relaxed every time round, and the flexing makes
heat. Jupiter's moon Io is heated this way into the most volcanically active body in the solar system, and Saturn's
moon Enceladus is thought to keep a liquid ocean under its ice.

**Broken comets.** Close enough to a planet, the stretch is stronger than a small body's own gravity, and it is pulled
apart. The distance is called the **Roche limit**. Comet Shoemaker–Levy 9 passed inside Jupiter's Roche limit in
July 1992 and broke into a string of pieces, which later crashed into Jupiter. Inside the limit, loose material spreads
into a ring instead of gathering into a moon.

## 8.7 Tides at Mars { #mars }

**Tides on Mars.** Mars has no oceans, but its rock and core still flex. The two main tides acting on it come from the
Sun and from Phobos; the Sun's stretch is about 13 times Phobos's. Orbiters measure how much Mars bulges in response,
a number called $k_2$. Tracking of Mars Global Surveyor, Mars Odyssey and Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter gives
$k_2 = 0.1697$. A planet with a solid core would give less than 0.10, so at least the outer part of Mars's core must be
liquid, with a radius worked out at between 1,520 and 1,840 km.

**Phobos falls, Deimos drifts.** Whether a moon is pushed out or pulled in depends on which goes round faster. The Moon
takes a month to orbit while the Earth turns in a day, so the Earth drags the bulge ahead and the Moon is pushed out.
Phobos is the other way round. It orbits in 7 hours 39 minutes, faster than Mars turns, because it is inside the
stationary orbit, 20,428 km from Mars's centre (chapter 3, [section 3.8](03-Keplers-Three-Laws.md#mars)). The bulge it
raises lags behind it and pulls it back, and Phobos sinks by about 2 m every century. Deimos is just outside the
stationary orbit, and its orbit is very slowly growing.

## 8.8 Working it out { #working }

The stretch on a body of radius $r$, from a mass $M$ at distance $d$, is about $2GMr / d^3$ (section 8.9).

**The Moon on the Earth.** $2 \times 4.903 \times 10^{12} \times 6.371 \times 10^{6} / (3.844 \times 10^{8})^3 =
1.10 \times 10^{-6}$ m/s².

**The Sun on the Earth.** $2 \times 1.327 \times 10^{20} \times 6.371 \times 10^{6} / (1.496 \times 10^{11})^3 =
5.05 \times 10^{-7}$ m/s², which is 0.46 of the Moon's.

**The Earth on the Moon.** $2 \times 3.986 \times 10^{14} \times 1.737 \times 10^{6} / (3.844 \times 10^{8})^3 =
2.44 \times 10^{-5}$ m/s², 22 times the Moon's stretch on the Earth.

**Mars on Phobos.** At the end of Phobos's long axis, 13 km from its centre, the stretch is
$2 \times 4.283 \times 10^{13} \times 1.3 \times 10^4 / (9.378 \times 10^6)^3 = 0.00135$ m/s². Phobos's spin adds
$(2\pi / 27{,}554\ \text{s})^2 \times 13{,}000 = 0.00068$ m/s², so the outward pull is 0.0020 m/s², about a third of
Phobos's average surface gravity of 0.0057.

## 8.9 The stretch, and how close is too close { #roche }

**The stretch.** A point on the near side of a planet is at distance $d - r$ from the moon, the centre at $d$. The
difference in pull is

$$\frac{GM}{(d - r)^2} - \frac{GM}{d^2} = \frac{GM}{d^2}\left[\left(1 - \frac{r}{d}\right)^{-2} - 1\right]
\approx \frac{GM}{d^2}\cdot\frac{2r}{d} = \frac{2GMr}{d^3},$$

using $(1 - x)^{-2} \approx 1 + 2x$ for small $x$. The cube is why the Sun, 390 times farther than the Moon but
27 million times heavier, raises the smaller tide.

**Too close.** Picture a small moon of radius $r$ and mass $m$, held together only by its own gravity, at distance $d$
from a planet of radius $R$ and mass $M$. It comes apart when the stretch beats its own pull at the surface:

$$\frac{2GMr}{d^3} = \frac{Gm}{r^2} \qquad\Rightarrow\qquad d = r\left(\frac{2M}{m}\right)^{1/3}.$$

Writing the masses as densities times volumes, $M = \tfrac43\pi R^3\rho_M$ and $m = \tfrac43\pi r^3\rho_m$, the moon's
size cancels:

$$d = R\left(\frac{2\rho_M}{\rho_m}\right)^{1/3} = 1.26\,R\left(\frac{\rho_M}{\rho_m}\right)^{1/3}.$$

This is the limit for a firm, round moon. A fluid moon stretches into an egg shape, which makes the stretch worse, and
Édouard Roche found the limit for it to be about $2.44\,R(\rho_M/\rho_m)^{1/3}$.

**For Phobos**, with Mars's density 3,934 and Phobos's 1,853 kg/m³, $(\rho_M/\rho_m)^{1/3} = 1.285$. The firm limit is
1.62 Mars radii and the fluid limit 3.14. Phobos, at 2.77 Mars radii, is already inside the fluid limit; it survives
only because it has some strength. A rubble pile of its shape is expected to fail at about 2.1 Mars radii, between
the two.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Why are there two high tides a day, not one?

??? note "Answer"
    The Moon pulls the near side of the Earth more than the centre, and the centre more than the far side. That
    stretches the Earth into two bulges, one facing the Moon and one facing away, and the Earth turns under both.

**2.** (beginner) When do spring tides happen, and why are they larger?

??? note "Answer"
    At new Moon and full Moon, when the Sun, Moon and Earth are in a line. The Sun's tide then adds to the Moon's.

**3.** (beginner) Is the Moon getting nearer or farther, and how do we know?

??? note "Answer"
    Farther, by 3.8 cm a year. Lasers fired at mirrors left on the Moon by Apollo, Lunokhod and Chandrayaan-3 time the
    round trip to within millimetres.

**4.** (intermediate) Why does Phobos sink while the Moon and Deimos move outwards?

??? note "Answer"
    Phobos is inside Mars's stationary orbit, so it goes round faster than Mars turns. The bulge it raises lags behind
    it and pulls it back. The Moon and Deimos are outside their planets' stationary orbits, so the bulges run ahead and
    pull them forwards.

**5.** (intermediate) If the day keeps growing longer by 1.8 thousandths of a second each century, how much longer
will it be in a million years?

??? note "Answer"
    A million years is 10,000 centuries, so $10{,}000 \times 0.0018 = 18$ seconds longer.

**6.** (intermediate) Why is Mars's main tide raised by the Sun, while the Earth's is raised by the Moon?

??? note "Answer"
    The stretch goes as $M/d^3$. Phobos is tiny, about $10^{16}$ kg, so even close to Mars its stretch is small. The
    Moon is far heavier, $7.3 \times 10^{22}$ kg, and beats the Sun at the Earth. At Mars the Sun's stretch is about
    13 times Phobos's.

**7.** (advanced) Show that the Sun's stretch on the Earth is about 46% of the Moon's, using $GM = 1.327 \times
10^{20}$ and $4.903 \times 10^{12}$ m³/s² and distances of $1.496 \times 10^{11}$ and $3.844 \times 10^{8}$ m.

??? note "Answer"
    The ratio is $\dfrac{1.327 \times 10^{20}}{4.903 \times 10^{12}} \times \left(\dfrac{3.844 \times 10^{8}}{1.496
    \times 10^{11}}\right)^3 = 2.707 \times 10^{7} \times 1.696 \times 10^{-8} = 0.459$.

**8.** (expert) Find the firm-body limit $d = R(2\rho_M/\rho_m)^{1/3}$ for a moon made of water ice, density
1,000 kg/m³, round the Earth, density 5,513 kg/m³. Is the Moon in any danger?

??? note "Answer"
    $(2 \times 5{,}513 / 1{,}000)^{1/3} = 11.03^{1/3} = 2.23$, so $d = 2.23 \times 6{,}371 = 14{,}200$ km. The Moon is
    denser than ice and 384,400 km away, about 27 times farther. It is in no danger, and it is moving away.

## Summary

- The Moon stretches the Earth into two bulges, so most coasts have two high tides a day, about 12 hours 25 minutes
  apart.
- The Sun's tide is 46% of the Moon's; together they give spring tides at new and full Moon and neap tides at the
  quarters.
- Coasts and basins shape real tides: about 16 m in the Bay of Fundy, a 9 m bore on the Qiantang.
- People from Greece, the Arab world, England, China, Australia and Brazil linked the tides to the Moon; Newton
  explained them by gravity.
- Tides slow the Earth's spin and push the Moon away by 3.8 cm a year.
- Tides lock moons face-on, heat Io and Enceladus, and break apart bodies that come inside the Roche limit.
- Mars's tides show that its core is at least partly liquid. Phobos, inside the stationary orbit, is sinking and should
  break into a ring in 30 to 50 million years.
