# 20. Mars's changing orbit and tilt

*Today Mars is tilted by 25°, much like the Earth, and its orbit is a fairly gentle oval. Neither will last. Over
hundreds of thousands of years the tilt swings between about 15° and 35°, and over millions of years it can go much
further. The orbit stretches and relaxes, and the seasons slide round it. Each change moves sunlight between the
poles and the equator, and with it Mars's ice. This last chapter follows those slow swings, the people who first
linked ice ages to the sky, and the layers of ice on Mars that keep the record.*

    - how far Mars's tilt has swung over the last ten million years

    - how Mars's orbit stretches and how its seasons slide round it

    - what an ice age on Mars looks like, and when the last one ended

## 20.1 A tilt that wanders { #tilt }

Mars's axis is tilted by **25.19°** to its orbit, close to the Earth's 23.4°, so Mars has seasons like ours
(book 1, [section 8.7](../night-sky/08-The-Suns-Path-and-the-Seasons.md#mars)). That likeness is an accident of the
present.

The tilt of the Earth changes too, but only between about **22.1° and 24.5°**, once every 41,000 years. Mars's tilt
swings much further. Calculations by Jacques Laskar's team at the Paris Observatory, called **La2004**, show it going
between about **15° and 35°** in the last million years, with a swing roughly every 120,000 years. Over the last ten
million years it has reached nearly 48°, and more than five million years ago it often passed 45°.

## 20.2 Why the Earth's tilt stays put { #moon }

A spinning planet bulges at its equator. The Sun pulls on the bulge and tries to stand the planet up, and because it
is spinning, the axis does not tip but slowly swings round in a cone, like a spinning top. This is **precession**.
The Earth's axis goes round once in about 26,000 years (book 1,
[section 6.10](../night-sky/06-Rising-Setting-and-the-Pole-Star.md#precession)). Mars's goes round once in about
**171,000 years**, about 7.6 arcseconds a year.

Meanwhile the planet's orbit is not fixed either. The pulls of the other planets slowly rock the plane of the orbit
(chapter 10, [section 10.4](10-Wobbling-Orbits-and-the-Finding-of-Neptune.md#turning)). If the axis swings round at
the same rate as some of that rocking, the two keep in step, like pushes on a swing at the right moment
([chapter 11](11-Resonances.md#swing)), and the tilt can change a lot. Mars's slow precession sits right among those
rates. Worse, the rates themselves drift, so Mars wanders from one resonance to the next. The result is **chaos**.

In 1993 two teams showed this. Laskar and Robutel found that Mars's tilt is in a large chaotic zone,
from 0° to 60°, and Touma and Wisdom published "The chaotic obliquity of Mars" the same year.

The Earth is saved by the **Moon**. The Moon pulls on the Earth's bulge too, so the Earth's axis swings much faster
than the planets rock its orbit, and it stays clear of the resonances. Laskar, Joutel and Robutel found the
Earth's tilt is stable, varying by only about ±1.3° around 23.3°. Without the Moon, the chaotic zone would stretch
from nearly 0° to about 85°. Phobos and Deimos are far too small to steady Mars.

## 20.3 A stretching orbit and sliding seasons { #orbit }

**The stretch.** Mars's orbit has an eccentricity of 0.093 today, and it is growing (chapter 10,
[section 10.7](10-Wobbling-Orbits-and-the-Finding-of-Neptune.md#mars)). In the La2004 data it was 0.075 about 26,000
years ago, reaches about 0.11 in 100,000 years and about **0.12** in some 200,000 years. About 1.35 million years ago
the orbit was almost a circle, with an eccentricity of only 0.002. The orbit stretches and relaxes roughly every
**100,000 years**, with slower swings over about two million years.

**The sliding seasons.** Today Mars is closest to the Sun just before southern summer starts, and then it gets about
**45% more sunlight** than at its farthest. So southern summers are short and hot, southern winters long and cold, and
the north has milder seasons (book 1, [section 8.9](../night-sky/08-The-Suns-Path-and-the-Seasons.md#kepler)). But the
perihelion turns one way and the axis swings the other, so the seasons slide round the orbit. In the La2004 data they
go once round in about **51,000 years**. In about 28,000 years it will be the north that has short, hot summers.

**Together.** The tilt sets how much sun the poles get in summer, the stretch sets how much closer to the Sun one
summer is than the other, and the sliding sets which pole gets the closer summer. Over the last million years these
have made midsummer sunshine at Mars's north pole vary from about two-thirds of today's to almost twice today's.

## 20.4 Ice ages and the sky: a worldwide story { #history }

The idea that the sky drives the climate needed two things: knowing that the Earth's tilt and orbit change, and
finding records of past climate to check against.

**Measuring the tilt.** Pytheas of Marseilles measured the tilt from a shadow at the summer solstice around 350 BCE.
About 830 CE the caliph al-Ma'mun had his astronomers in Baghdad measure it, and their value was used in the Arab world
for many years. Ulugh Beg measured it in 1437 as 23°30′17″. In the Middle Ages many believed the tilt rocked to and
fro every 672 years; Ibn al-Shatir, in the fourteenth century, was perhaps the first to see that this was wrong, and
Fracastoro in 1538 the first to see that the tilt was slowly shrinking.

**Linking the sky to ice.** In 1842 the French mathematician Joseph Adhémar was the first to suggest that ice ages
were controlled by the sky, in his book *Revolutions of the Sea*. The Scottish scientist James Croll, largely
self-taught, built a fuller theory of the climate changing with the Earth's orbit. Then the Serbian geophysicist and
astronomer **Milutin Milanković** worked out how the sunshine at each latitude changed over hundreds of thousands of
years, by hand. He kept at it even as a prisoner of war in the First World War, writing in his cell on his first
night. His great *Canon of Insolation* went to the printer on 2 April 1941, four days before Germany and its allies
attacked Yugoslavia. The bombing of Belgrade destroyed the printing house, but the printed sheets in its warehouse
survived.

Milanković also looked at Mars. In 1916 he published "Investigation of the climate of the planet Mars", working out an
average surface temperature of −17 °C from Mars's size, distance and tilt.

**The proof.** In 1976 James Hays, John Imbrie and Nicholas Shackleton matched the cycles to layers in deep-sea mud.
On land, the Chinese geologist **Liu Tungsheng** spent half a century reading the thick layers of windblown dust and
old soils of China's Loess Plateau. He made them a first-rank record of climate over the past 2.5 million years,
matching the deep-sea and polar ice records. Today, the cycles in the Earth's orbit and tilt are called **Milankovitch
cycles**.

## 20.5 Ice ages on Mars { #iceages }

On the Earth, an ice age means cold poles and ice sheets spreading towards the equator. On Mars it is the other way
round. When the tilt is high, the poles get strong summer sun and **warm up**. Their ice turns to vapour, the air
carries it towards the equator, and it falls as snow or frost on dust lower down.

Computer models of Mars's air, run by François Forget's team in Paris, show what happens at each tilt:

- **Above about 35°**, the north polar cap cannot survive the summers. Its ice moves to the high volcanoes of Tharsis,
  Arsia, Pavonis, Ascraeus and Olympus Mons, whose sides show what may be traces of recent glaciers.
- **At lower tilts**, the ice near the equator becomes unstable in turn and goes back to the poles and to high
  latitudes in both hemispheres.

In 2003 James Head and his colleagues found **dusty, ice-rich layers** metres thick spread over both hemispheres from
the poles down to about **30°** latitude, as far from the equator as Saudi Arabia or the southern United States on the
Earth. They dated them to an **ice age from about 2.1 to 0.4 million years ago**, when the tilt reached 30° to 35°.
Mars is now in a warm spell between ice ages, and those deposits are slowly breaking down.

## 20.6 Reading the layers { #layers }

Both of Mars's polar caps are stacks of thin layers of ice and dust, the **polar layered deposits**, kilometres thick.
They are thought to have built up over a series of ice ages, a little like tree rings or the ice cores of Greenland
and Antarctica.

People have suspected since 1972 that the bright and dark bands are set by the swings in Mars's orbit and tilt. In
2002 Laskar, Levrard and Mustard compared the brightness of the layers, going down into the north cap,
with the calculated summer sunshine at the north pole, and found they matched, much as climate records on the Earth
match the Milankovitch cycles.

The layers are hard to date, because nobody has drilled into them yet. Radar from orbit can see down through them,
and the 2016 measurement in section 20.5 is the clearest link so far between a layer and a date.

## 20.7 How far back can we know? { #chaos }

For the last few million years we can trust the numbers. Laskar's team give a precise answer only for the last **10
to 20 million years**. Beyond that, chaos takes over: tiny uncertainties in Mars's spin and in the planets' orbits
grow until two calculations that start almost the same end up completely different
([chapter 10](10-Wobbling-Orbits-and-the-Finding-of-Neptune.md#stable)).

So for the deep past they ran **over 200,000** calculations and studied them as a crowd. Over 5 billion years the
tilt averaged **37.6°**, and reached as much as **82°**. There is a 63% chance that it went above 60° at some time in
the last billion years, and 89% in the last three billion. The most likely tilt over Mars's history is about 42°, and
the most likely eccentricity about 0.07.

So Mars's present 25° is lower than usual. In times of high tilt, Mars's climate would have been very different, and
some scientists have suggested that shifts in the tilt help explain why rivers and lakes came and went on early Mars.
Not everyone agrees that the tilt is chaotic now: some argue that tides and friction between core and mantle may have
calmed it down, as happened to Mercury and Venus.

**Perihelion against aphelion.** Sunlight goes as $1/r^2$, and $r$ runs from $a(1 - e)$ to $a(1 + e)$, so the ratio
is $\left(\dfrac{1 + e}{1 - e}\right)^2$. For Mars, $(1.0933/0.9067)^2 = 1.45$, 45% more. For the Earth, $e = 0.0167$
gives 1.07.

**How fast the seasons slide.** The axis swings round once in 171,000 years, so the equinox slides back by
$360/171 = 2.1°$ every thousand years. The perihelion moves forward by about $4.4°$ every thousand years (chapter
10). Measured from the equinox, the perihelion gains $2.1 + 4.4 = 6.5°$ every thousand years and goes round once in
$360/6.5 \approx 55{,}000$ years. La2004, which averages the varying rates properly, gives 51,000 years. The
perihelion is now $19°$ of Mars's orbit short of the southern summer solstice; at about $7°$ every thousand years
that is some 3,000 years away. The north's turn comes half a cycle later, about 28,000 years from now.

**What the tilt does.** In section 20.9 the polar summer sunshine goes as $\sin\varepsilon$.
$\sin 35° / \sin 15° = 0.574/0.259 = 2.2$, so the swing in the last million years more than doubles it.

## 20.9 Sunshine at the pole, worked out { #derive }

At a pole on the day of its summer solstice, the Sun circles the sky all day at a height equal to the tilt
$\varepsilon$. Sunlight of strength $F$ arriving at height $\varepsilon$ spreads over a flat patch of ground, so
the ground gets $F\sin\varepsilon$, steadily for the whole day. With no air to worry about,

$$Q = F\sin\varepsilon, \qquad F = \frac{S_0}{(r/a)^2},$$

where $S_0$ is the sunlight at the planet's mean distance $a$. For Mars, $S_0 = 1{,}361/1.5237^2 = 586$ W/m².

**The distance at the solstice.** With $\varpi$ the longitude of perihelion measured round the Sun from the planet's
spring equinox direction, the northern summer solstice comes when the planet is at longitude 270°, and
$r = a(1 - e^2)/(1 - e\sin\varpi)$. So

$$Q_{\text{north}} = S_0 \sin\varepsilon \, \frac{(1 - e\sin\varpi)^2}{(1 - e^2)^2},
\qquad Q_{\text{south}} = S_0 \sin\varepsilon \, \frac{(1 + e\sin\varpi)^2}{(1 - e^2)^2}.$$

- **Mars today:** $\varepsilon = 25.19°$, $e = 0.0933$, $\varpi = 71.0°$. The north pole gets
  $586 \times 0.426 \times 0.846 = $ **211 W/m²**; the south pole at its solstice gets $\times 1.205$: **301 W/m²**.
- **A high-tilt summer:** $\varepsilon = 35°$, $e = 0.11$ and the perihelion at the northern solstice
  ($\sin\varpi = -1$): $586 \times 0.574 \times 1.262 = $ **424 W/m²**, twice today's.
- **The Earth's north pole** in June: $1{,}361 \times 0.398 \times 0.968 = $ **524 W/m²**.

The La2004 data put the north pole's midsummer sunshine over the last million years between 0.64 and 1.89 times
today's, and over the last ten million years up to 2.45 times.

      book 2, chapter 4.
    - $\varpi = 71.04°$ for today, measured from the moving equinox: the La2004 data file (above).
    - The numbers: our arithmetic. They ignore the air, which on Mars is thin.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Mars and the Earth have almost the same tilt today. Why is that a coincidence?

??? note "Answer"
    Mars's tilt swings between about 15° and 35° every 120,000 years or so, and much further over millions of years.
    It just happens to be near the Earth's value now.

**2.** (beginner) What keeps the Earth's tilt steady?

??? note "Answer"
    The Moon. Its pull on the Earth's bulge makes the axis swing round fast, every 26,000 years, which keeps it away
    from resonances with the slow rocking of the Earth's orbit.

**3.** (beginner) Why does Mars's south have shorter, hotter summers than its north today?

??? note "Answer"
    Mars is closest to the Sun just before southern summer, when it gets about 45% more sunlight than at its
    farthest. It also moves fastest then, so southern summer is short.

**4.** (intermediate) How is an ice age on Mars different from one on the Earth?

??? note "Answer"
    On Mars it comes with a high tilt and warmer poles. Polar ice turns to vapour and settles as dusty ice at lower
    latitudes, down to about 30°. On the Earth, ice ages bring colder poles and ice sheets spreading out from them.

**5.** (intermediate) What evidence ties the end of Mars's last ice age to about 370,000 to 400,000 years ago?

??? note "Answer"
    Head and his colleagues dated the dusty ice layers to 2.1 to 0.4 million years ago. Radar from MRO found
    about 87,000 km³ of polar ice laid down since about 370,000 years ago. The tilt was last above 30° about 380,000
    years ago.

**6.** (intermediate) Name three people from different parts of the world who helped link the sky to ice ages or
measure the Earth's tilt.

??? note "Answer"
    Any three of: al-Ma'mun's astronomers in Baghdad, Ulugh Beg, Ibn al-Shatir, Joseph Adhémar in France, James Croll
    in Scotland, Milutin Milanković in Serbia, Liu Tungsheng in China, or Hays, Imbrie and Shackleton.

**7.** (advanced) Mars's orbit will reach an eccentricity of about 0.12. How much more sunlight will Mars then get at
perihelion than at aphelion?

??? note "Answer"
    $(1.12/0.88)^2 = 1.62$, about 62% more, against 45% today.

**8.** (expert) Find the summer solstice sunshine at Mars's north pole for a tilt of 15°, a circular orbit.

??? note "Answer"
    With $e = 0$, $Q = 586 \times \sin 15° = 586 \times 0.259 = 152$ W/m², less than three-quarters of today's 211.

## Summary

- Mars's tilt is 25° now, but in the last million years it has swung between about 15° and 35°, and over 10 million
  years up to nearly 48°. The Earth's moves only between 22.1° and 24.5°.
- Mars's axis swings round once in about 171,000 years, near the rates at which the planets rock its orbit. The
  resonances make its tilt chaotic. The Moon keeps the Earth's tilt steady.
- Mars's orbit stretches and relaxes about every 100,000 years, up to 0.12 in some 200,000 years, and its seasons slide
  round the orbit once in about 51,000 years.
- Measuring the Earth's tilt and linking it to ice ages took Pytheas, al-Ma'mun's astronomers in Baghdad, Ulugh Beg,
  Ibn al-Shatir, Adhémar in France, Croll in Scotland, Milanković in Serbia and Liu Tungsheng in China.
- Martian ice ages come with high tilt and warm poles. The last one ended about 400,000 years ago, and ice has been
  piling up at the poles since.
- The polar layers keep a record of the swings, but chaos hides Mars's tilt beyond 10 to 20 million years ago; over 5
  billion years it averaged about 38°.
