# 1. Falling round the Earth

*Throw a ball sideways and it curves down to the ground. Throw it harder and it lands farther away. This chapter asks
what happens if you could throw it hard enough that the ground curved away beneath it as fast as it fell. The answer
is an orbit, and it explains why the Moon does not fall down, why astronauts float, why getting to space is easy but
staying there is hard, and why satellites fall back in the end. It ends with the same fall round Mars, where it is far
easier.*

    - what Newton's cannon shows about orbits
    - how fast something must go to orbit the Earth, and where 7.9 km/s comes from

    you can see, is a good companion.

    **Levels:** 1.1 to 1.4 are beginner; 1.5 to 1.9 are intermediate; 1.10 is advanced; 1.11 is expert.

## 1.1 The Moon is falling { #moon }

The Earth pulls on the Moon, just as it pulls on a dropped spoon. So why doesn't the Moon come crashing down?

It is falling, all the time. The Moon moves along its orbit at about 1 km every second. If the Earth's pull suddenly
stopped, it would go straight on, off into space. Instead the pull bends its path, and in each second the Moon falls
about 1.4 mm below the straight line it would otherwise have followed (section 1.10). But because it is also moving
sideways, it never gets any closer: the Earth it is falling towards keeps curving away beneath it. It falls round the
Earth instead of onto it, once every 27.3 days.

In 1687 Isaac Newton showed that the pull that makes an apple fall is the same pull that bends the Moon's path
(history book, [chapter 16, section 16.2](../history/16-Newton-Halley-and-Romer.md#principia)). This whole book is
about that one idea: **an orbit is a fall that never reaches the ground.** The physics lesson
[The Moon goes round](https://physics.akadian.ca/topics/gravity/level-1/06-the-moon-goes-round.html) shows it with a
ball on a string.

*Figure A. Newton's cannon. The paths are worked out for a pull that weakens with distance, as gravity does.*

- Fired slowly, the ball curves down and lands near the mountain (path A).
- Fired faster, it lands farther away, because the ground curves away beneath it (paths B and C).
- At just the right speed, the ground curves away exactly as fast as the ball falls. The ball never lands, and comes
  all the way round to the mountain. This is a **circular orbit** (path D).
- A little faster still, and it swings out farther on the far side, on an oval path called an **ellipse**, but still
  comes back round to the mountain (path E). Chapter 4 is about ellipses.
- Faster again, and it leaves the Earth for good (chapter 7).

was published only after his death, in 1728, in English as *A Treatise of the System of the World*. The physics lesson
[Newton's cannon](https://physics.akadian.ca/topics/gravity/level-2/05-newtons-cannon.html) goes through it step by

## 1.3 How fast is fast enough? { #speed }

*Figure B. Why 7.9 km/s is orbital speed near the ground.*

So a ball moving sideways at 7.9 km each second falls 4.9 m in that second, and the ground beneath it drops away by
the same 4.9 m. It ends the second just as high above the ground as it began, and the next second is the same. That is
**orbital speed** near the Earth's surface: 7.9 km/s, about 28,000 km/h.

Near the ground, of course, the air would stop it at once. Real satellites orbit higher, where gravity is a little
weaker, so they go a little slower. The International Space Station, about 400 km up, moves at 7.67 km/s and goes
round in about 93 minutes (book 1, [section 16.3](../night-sky/16-Satellites-and-the-Space-Station.md#iss)). Sputnik
1, the first satellite, reached about 8 km/s at its fastest and went round once every 96.2 minutes.

## 1.4 Why astronauts float { #weightless }

At the space station, gravity is still about 89% as strong as on the ground. So why do astronauts float?

Because the station is falling, and they are falling with it. Picture standing on bathroom scales in a lift whose
cable snaps: you, the scales and the lift all fall together, your feet no longer press down, and the scales read zero.
Inside an orbiting station the same thing happens, except that the station never hits the ground. "Zero gravity" is
the wrong name for it; astronauts are in **free fall**, and the tiny pushes that remain are called **microgravity**.
The physics lesson [Weightless in orbit](https://physics.akadian.ca/topics/gravity/level-4/04-weightless-in-orbit.html)
works through it.

You can feel the same thing without going to space. Aircraft fly up and over in an arc, the path a thrown ball would
follow, with their engines only making up for the air's drag. Everyone inside floats for about 25 seconds out of each
65-second arc. Space agencies use these flights to train astronauts; Europe's runs from Bordeaux, in an Airbus called
*Zero-G*.

??? note "Sources"
    - Gravity at 400 km, $(6{,}371/6{,}771)^2 = 0.89$ of its value at the ground: our arithmetic, as in the physics
      lesson above.
    - Weightlessness in orbit as free fall, not zero gravity, and the name microgravity: Wikipedia,
      [Weightlessness](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Weightlessness) (weaker).
    - Reduced-gravity flights following a free-fall parabola with thrust making up for drag, about 25 seconds of

## 1.5 Height is easy, speed is hard { #height }

Where does space begin? There is no sharp edge; the air just thins out. By a convention of the FAI, the world body that
keeps records for flying, space starts at 100 km, a height called the **Kármán line**.

The first thing people made that reached space was a German V-2 rocket, launched from Peenemünde on 20 June 1944. It
climbed to 176 km. Then it fell straight back down. Getting high is not enough: to stay up, a satellite needs speed
sideways.

- To fly straight up to 100 km and fall back, a rocket needs about **1.4 km/s**.
- To orbit a few hundred kilometres up, it needs about **7.8 km/s**.

Because energy grows with the square of speed, reaching orbit takes about **30 times** the energy of a hop to 100 km
(section 1.10). That is why rockets that carry tourists briefly into space are small, and rockets that put satellites
into orbit are enormous (physics lesson
[Orbit is speed](https://physics.akadian.ca/topics/rockets/level-4/02-orbit-is-speed.html)).

??? note "Sources"
    - The Kármán line as a conventional edge of space, set by the FAI at 100 km: Wikipedia,
      [Kármán line](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K%C3%A1rm%C3%A1n_line) (weaker).
    - MW 18014, a V-2 launched at Peenemünde on 20 June 1944, the first human-made object to reach space, at 176 km:
      Wikipedia, [MW 18014](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/MW_18014) (weaker).

## 1.6 Why orbits run down { #decay }

Even 400 km up there is a trace of air. The station brushes through it at 7.67 km/s, and the drag slowly takes away its
speed, so it sinks. The space station loses about 2 km of height a month, and is pushed back up from time to time by
the engines of cargo ships docked to it, a **reboost**. At night it turns its solar panels edge-on to cut the drag.

Lower down, the air is thicker and orbits die faster. Below about 150 km, a satellite cannot even get once round the
Earth before the air brings it down. How fast an orbit decays also depends on the Sun: when the Sun is active, it
heats the upper air, which swells upwards and drags harder.

- **Sputnik 1**, launched on 4 October 1957, fell back into the air and burned up on 4 January 1958, three months
  later.
- **Skylab**, the first American space station, fell on 11 July 1979, scattering pieces across the Indian Ocean and
  Western Australia.
- **Tiangong-1**, China's first space station, came down over the South Pacific, northwest of Tahiti, on 2 April 2018.

Far above the air, orbits can last almost for ever. The Moon's orbit is not shrinking at all; it is in fact slowly
growing (chapter 8).

## 1.8 Falling round the Sun { #sun }

The Earth is falling too. It moves round the Sun at 29.8 km every second, and in each second the Sun's pull bends its
path about 3 mm towards the Sun. Mars, farther out, moves more slowly, at 24.1 km/s, and falls only about 1.3 mm
towards the Sun each second. The farther a planet is from the Sun, the weaker the pull and the slower it must go, and
the longer its path round: both make its year longer (physics lesson
[Slower farther out](https://physics.akadian.ca/topics/gravity/level-4/03-slower-farther-out.html)).

So the Moon falls round the Earth while the Earth falls round the Sun, and the Sun itself falls round the middle of
our galaxy. Everything in the sky is falling round something.

      Mars's 24.08 km/s and 227.96 million km: NASA,

      1.10.
    - That the Sun orbits the centre of the Milky Way is standard.

## 1.9 Falling round Mars { #mars }

On Mars a dropped ball falls only 1.87 m in the first second, because Mars's gravity is 38% of the Earth's. And Mars
is smaller, so its ground curves away more sharply: it drops 1.87 m below a level line over just 3.56 km. A ball
skimming the ground of an airless, smooth Mars would therefore orbit at **3.56 km/s**, less than half the Earth's
7.9 km/s.

That makes Mars a much easier place to leave. Per kilogram, getting into an orbit 200 km up takes about a fifth of the
energy it takes at the Earth (section 1.10). Mars's thin air still drags on low orbiters, and spacecraft arriving at
Mars have used that drag on purpose, dipping into the top of the air to slow down (chapter 17).

Mars has had artificial satellites since Mariner 9 reached it on 14 November 1971 (book 1,
[section 16.8](../night-sky/16-Satellites-and-the-Space-Station.md#mars)). Its own moon Phobos falls round it only
about 6,000 km up, once every 7 hours 39 minutes.

## 1.10 Working it out { #working }

**How far the Moon falls.** Anything moving at speed $v$ round a circle of radius $r$ is pulled towards the centre with
acceleration $a = v^2/r$ (section 1.11). For the Moon,

$$a = \frac{(1.022\text{ km/s})^2}{384{,}400\text{ km}} = 2.72\times10^{-6}\text{ km/s}^2 = 2.72\text{ mm/s}^2.$$

Starting from rest, a steady acceleration $a$ moves a body $\tfrac12 a t^2$ in time $t$, so in one second the Moon
falls $\tfrac12 \times 2.72 = 1.36$ mm below its straight path. The same sum for the Earth round the Sun gives
$29.78^2 / 149.6\times10^6$ km/s², or 5.93 mm/s², and a fall of 2.96 mm in a second.

**Orbital speed at the ground.** Set $a = g$: $v = \sqrt{gR} = \sqrt{9.82 \times 6{,}371{,}000} = 7{,}910$ m/s.

**Height against speed.** Lifting 1 kg from the ground to 200 km takes
$GM\left(\frac1R - \frac1{R+h}\right) = 398{,}600 \times \left(\frac1{6{,}371} - \frac1{6{,}571}\right) = 1.90$ km²/s²,
which is 1.90 MJ. To stay there it needs $v = \sqrt{GM/(R+h)} = 7.79$ km/s, and a kinetic energy of
$\tfrac12 v^2 = 30.3$ MJ. Speed costs 16 times as much as height. A hop straight up to 100 km and back takes only
0.97 MJ, about a thirtieth of the 32 MJ needed for orbit.

| | Mars | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pull of gravity at the ground | 3.73 m/s² | 9.82 m/s² |
| Fall in the first second | 1.87 m | 4.91 m |
| Distance over which the ground drops by that much | 3.56 km | 7.91 km |
| Orbital speed just above the ground | 3.56 km/s | 7.91 km/s |
| Time for one such orbit | 100 minutes | 84 minutes |
| Energy to reach an orbit 200 km up, per kilogram | 6.7 MJ | 32 MJ |
| Speed round the Sun | 24.1 km/s | 29.8 km/s |
| Fall towards the Sun each second | 1.3 mm | 3.0 mm |

Reaching orbit from Mars takes about a fifth of the energy needed at the Earth. Real rockets need far more than these
amounts, because they must also lift their own fuel and push through the air (chapter 13), but the advantage stays.

    - The arithmetic is ours. The energies leave out the spin of each planet, which gives a rocket launched eastwards a

## 1.11 Why a circle needs $v^2/r$ { #circle }

Take a body moving at speed $v$ round a circle of radius $r$. In a short time $t$ it would go a distance $vt$ along a
straight line, the tangent. The circle falls away below the tangent by a small distance $s$. By Pythagoras,

$$(r + s)^2 = r^2 + (vt)^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad 2rs + s^2 = v^2t^2 \quad\Rightarrow\quad s \approx \frac{v^2t^2}{2r},$$

since $s^2$ is tiny next to $2rs$. A steady acceleration $a$ towards the centre would make the body fall
$s = \tfrac12 a t^2$ below the tangent in the same time. For the body to stay on the circle these must match:

$$\tfrac12 a t^2 = \frac{v^2 t^2}{2r} \quad\Rightarrow\quad a = \frac{v^2}{r}.$$

**Why the two numbers in Figure B are the same.** Near the ground the fall in one second is $s = \tfrac12 g$. The
distance $d$ over which the ground drops by $s$ comes from the same Pythagoras: $d = \sqrt{2rs} = \sqrt{gr}$. And the
orbital speed is $v = \sqrt{gr}$ too. So, on any planet, the distance over which the ground drops by one second's fall
is exactly the distance an orbiting ball covers in that second: 7.91 km on the Earth, 3.56 km on Mars.

**The time round.** An orbit skimming the ground takes $T = 2\pi r / v = 2\pi\sqrt{r/g}$. Nothing in it depends on
the mass of the thing going round: a feather and a space station, without air, would orbit side by side.

??? note "Sources"
    - The derivations are ours; the result $a = v^2/r$ is standard.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) The Earth pulls on the Moon. Why doesn't the Moon fall onto the Earth?

??? note "Answer"
    It does fall, all the time, but it is also moving sideways at about 1 km/s. As it falls, the Earth curves away
    beneath it, so it falls round the Earth instead of onto it.

**2.** (beginner) In Newton's cannon, what happens if the ball is fired a little faster than orbital speed?

??? note "Answer"
    It swings out farther on the far side of the Earth, on an oval path called an ellipse, and comes back round to the
    mountain. Only if it is much faster still does it leave the Earth for good.

**3.** (beginner) Gravity at the space station is 89% as strong as on the ground. Why do the astronauts float?

??? note "Answer"
    They and the station are falling together, so nothing presses on them, just as in a falling lift. They are in free
    fall, not in zero gravity.

**4.** (intermediate) Why is putting a satellite in orbit so much harder than sending a rocket up to 100 km?

??? note "Answer"
    Orbit needs a sideways speed of about 7.8 km/s, against about 1.4 km/s for a hop to 100 km. Energy grows with the
    square of speed, so orbit takes about 30 times as much energy.

**5.** (intermediate) Why did Skylab and Tiangong-1 fall, and why does the space station need reboosts?

??? note "Answer"
    There is still a trace of air at their heights. Its drag slowly takes away their speed, so they sink lower, into
    thicker air, and in the end fall. The station would do the same if engines did not push it back up; it loses about
    2 km a month.

**6.** (intermediate) Name four countries that put a satellite into orbit on their own rocket, with the years.

??? note "Answer"
    Any four of: the Soviet Union (1957), the United States (1958), France (1965), Japan (1970), China (1970), the
    United Kingdom (1971), India (1980), Israel (1988), Iran (2009), North Korea (2012), South Korea (2013).

**7.** (advanced) The Moon's surface gravity is 1.62 m/s² and its radius 1,737.4 km. How fast would a ball have to go to
orbit just above the ground, and how long would one orbit take?

??? note "Answer"
    $v = \sqrt{1.62 \times 1{,}737{,}400} \approx 1{,}680$ m/s, about 1.7 km/s. One orbit takes
    $2\pi \times 1{,}737{,}400 / 1{,}680 \approx 6{,}500$ s, about 108 minutes.

**8.** (expert) Show that the time for an orbit skimming the ground depends only on the planet's average density
$\rho$, and check it for the Earth ($\rho = 5{,}513$ kg/m³) and Mars ($\rho = 3{,}934$ kg/m³).

??? note "Answer"
    $g = GM/r^2$ and $M = \tfrac43\pi r^3\rho$, so $g = \tfrac43\pi G\rho r$. Then
    $T = 2\pi\sqrt{r/g} = 2\pi\sqrt{3/(4\pi G\rho)} = \sqrt{3\pi/(G\rho)}$, with no $r$ left. With
    $G = 6.674\times10^{-11}$: for the Earth, $T = \sqrt{3\pi / (6.674\times10^{-11} \times 5{,}513)} \approx 5{,}060$
    s, 84 minutes; for Mars, about 5,990 s, 100 minutes. Both match the table in section 1.10.

## Summary

- An orbit is a fall that never reaches the ground: the body moves sideways fast enough that the ground curves away as
  fast as it falls.
- The Moon falls about 1.4 mm towards the Earth each second; the Earth falls about 3 mm towards the Sun.
- Newton's cannon shows the range of paths: falling back, circling, ellipses and escape.
- Orbital speed near the ground is 7.9 km/s on the Earth, because the ground drops 4.9 m over 7.9 km and a ball falls
  4.9 m in a second.
- Astronauts float because they fall with their station; gravity there is still 89% as strong.
- Reaching space is easy; staying there needs speed, and about 30 times the energy of a hop to 100 km.
- Air drag brings low orbits down: Sputnik 1 in 1958, Skylab in 1979, Tiangong-1 in 2018.
- The Soviet Union, the United States, France, Japan, China, the United Kingdom, India and others reached orbit with
  their own rockets.
- At Mars, orbital speed near the ground is 3.56 km/s, and orbit takes about a fifth of the energy.
