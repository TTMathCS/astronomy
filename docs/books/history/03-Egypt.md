# 3. Egypt

*Egypt is a strip of green along a river in the desert, and its year was set by the river: the Nile rose every
summer, spread over the fields, and went down again. The Egyptians gave the world a year of 365 days made of twelve
months of thirty days, the hours of the night counted by stars, and the habit of splitting day and night into twelve
hours each. They watched Sirius come back to the dawn sky each summer, painted star clocks on coffin lids and the
ceilings of tombs, and set their pyramids square to north with an error smaller than a tenth of a degree. Some of how
they did it we know; some is still argued over, and this chapter shows both.*

## 3.1 The gift of the river { #nile }

Almost no rain falls in Egypt. All its farming depended on the Nile, which rose every summer with the monsoon rains in
the highlands of Ethiopia, flooded the valley, and left fresh mud behind as it fell. The Egyptians split their year
into three seasons named after the river and the fields:

| Season | Meaning | Roughly |
|---|---|---|
| Akhet | the flood | September to January |
| Peret | the coming out (of the fields from the water) | January to May |
| Shemu | the harvest, or low water | May to September |

Records of the height of each year's flood were kept from the time of King Djer, around 3000 BC. With the flood so
important, it seems natural that the Egyptian year began with it. But a river is a poor clock. In the records the
first rise of the water came as early as 15 April and as late as 23 June: a spread of more than two months. Setting a
calendar by the flood, one Egyptologist said, would be like setting it by the first spring flowers. To fix the year
the Egyptians needed something steadier, and they found it in the sky.

## 3.2 A year of 365 days { #year }

The Egyptian **civil calendar**, used for government, taxes and dates of every kind, was the simplest calendar ever
used by a great state:

- 12 months of exactly 30 days, four months to each season
- each month split into three "decades" of 10 days
- 5 extra days at the end of the year, the **epagomenal** days ("added on"), treated as outside the year proper
- no leap day: every year had 365 days.

The scheme is so regular that for centuries afterwards astronomers liked it. Ptolemy dated observations in Egyptian
years in his *Almagest* in the second century AD, and Copernicus, 1,400 years later, built his planet tables on the
Egyptian year because it never changed length. Alongside it ran a **lunar calendar** for some festivals, with months
from one new Moon to the next.

**The wandering year.** The real year is about 365¼ days. A calendar of 365 days slips back a quarter of a day each
year, or a whole day every four years. After 40 years New Year's Day comes ten days earlier in the seasons; after 400
years, a hundred days. The Romans called it the *annus vagus*, the wandering year. The names of the seasons stayed on
the months, but slowly "flood" months came round in the harvest and "harvest" months in the flood.

The Egyptians knew this, and lived with it for about 3,000 years. The calendar was there to count days, not to tell
the farmers when to plant: for that they watched the river and the stars.

## 3.3 Sirius and the New Year { #sirius }

Sirius is the brightest star in the night sky. The Egyptians called it **Sopdet**, and the Greeks **Sothis**. For about
two months each year it is lost in the Sun's glare. Then one summer morning it appears again, low in the east just
before sunrise: its **heliacal rising**. In the old kingdom of Egypt this happened in July, around the time the Nile
began to rise, and the return of Sopdet became the herald of the flood and of the New Year.

**The Sothic cycle.** Because the civil year was 365 days and Sirius returns about every 365¼, the day of Sirius's
rising moved through the civil calendar by one day every four years. After $4 \times 365 = 1{,}460$ years it was back on
New Year's Day, the first of the month Thoth. This period is the **Sothic cycle**.

A Roman writer, Censorinus, recorded that the first of Thoth and the rising of Sirius had fallen together in a recent
year; the date works out as 20 July AD 139. Counting back whole cycles gives earlier coincidences around 1320 BC and
2780 BC (section 3.11).

**Dating the pharaohs.** A few Egyptian documents say that Sirius rose on a given day of the civil calendar in a given
year of a king. Each such date tells you how far through a Sothic cycle that year was, and so gives a date BC. In 1904
Eduard Meyer collected six such records, and they became one of the anchors of Egyptian chronology. The method has
weak points, and the chapter would mislead if it hid them:

- **Where was it seen?** Sirius rises later in the year the further north you are. A rising recorded in the reign of
  Amenhotep I gives 1525–1504 BC for his reign if it was seen at Thebes; if it was seen at Memphis or in the Delta, as
  some scholars argue, the whole 18th Dynasty moves by about 20 years.
- **Four years, one date.** Because the calendar slips one day in four years, Sirius rises on the same calendar day four
  years running, so each record is ambiguous by up to four years.
- **How bright must it be?** The day you first see a star depends on the clearness of the air and on the watcher.

So Sothic dates are useful, but they come with error bars of years to decades, and modern chronologies combine them
with radiocarbon dates and lists of kings.

## 3.4 Decans: star clocks on coffin lids { #decans }

How do you tell the time at night with no clock? The Egyptians used stars rising in the east. As the Earth turns, the
stars near the Sun's path come up over the horizon one after another, about one every 40 minutes. The Egyptians chose
36 stars or small groups of stars spread round the sky, now called the **decans** (from the Greek for "tenths"). Each
night a sequence of decans rose, and the rising of each marked an hour.

**Why 36?** The sky turns a little more than once a day, so each night a given star rises about 4 minutes earlier. After
ten days it rises 40 minutes earlier: a whole "hour" sooner. So every ten days the list of hour stars moves on by one. A
new decan comes up out of the Sun's glare at dawn every ten days, and 36 decans for 36 ten-day weeks make the 360 days
of the year's twelve months. The five extra days fall outside the scheme.

**Diagonal star tables.** From about the 21st century BC, in the 9th to 12th Dynasties, coffins were painted on the inside
of the lid with tables of decans: columns for the ten-day periods of the year, rows for the hours of the night, and in
each square the name of the decan that marked that hour. Because a decan rises an hour earlier each ten days, its name
climbs one row with each column, and the names make diagonal stripes across the table. The tables are called
**diagonal star tables** or star clocks.

Why put a clock on a coffin? Probably not for telling the time. The Egyptians believed the dead joined the stars, and
the tables belong with the other texts painted on coffins to help the dead on that journey.

**Which stars were they?** We know the decans' names; one, for example, means "in the middle of the boat". Sirius was
one of them, and the sequence was counted from it. But apart from Sirius and the stars of Orion, which stars made up
which decan is still not settled. As the astronomer and Egyptologist Sarah Symons has pointed out, we do not know
exactly how the tables were made or how the decans were chosen, so the stars cannot be matched with confidence.

**Working it out: why twelve hours of night?** At Thebes (latitude 25.7°) at midsummer, the time when the sky is really
dark, from the end of evening twilight to the start of morning twilight (Sun more than 12° below the horizon), is about
8.3 hours. In 8.3 hours, at 40 minutes a decan, $8.3 \times 60 / 40 \approx 12$ decans rise. So a count of decans made in
the short nights of summer, the season of Sirius and the flood, gives about twelve hours: a plausible origin for the
twelve hours of the night. At midwinter the dark lasts about 11.7 hours and some 17 decans rise, but the count of
twelve was kept.

## 3.5 Ceilings of the sky { #ceilings }

From the New Kingdom, the sky moved from coffin lids to the ceilings of tombs and temples.

**Senenmut's ceiling.** The oldest known astronomical ceiling in Egypt is in a tomb cut for Senenmut, a leading official
of the female pharaoh Hatshepsut, around 1479–1458 BC, at Thebes. It is split into two halves, the northern and the
southern sky, with the decans and the constellations drawn as gods and figures. The tomb was badly damaged in ancient times, perhaps when
Hatshepsut's memory was being erased; a full-size copy is in the Metropolitan Museum of Art in New York.

**The Book of Nut.** The sky goddess Nut is drawn arching over the Earth, her hands and feet touching the horizons,
with the Sun travelling along her body by day and through it at night. Around her runs the most important Egyptian
astronomical text, now called the Book of Nut. Its own title, found on a papyrus fragment and published in 2007, is
*The Fundamentals of the Course of the Stars*. It explains the cycles of the decans and the Sun's daily rebirth.
Copies survive on monuments (the cenotaph of Seti I at Abydos, the tomb of Ramesses IV) and on papyri of the second
century AD from Tebtunis, where it was still being studied and explained more than 1,400 years after Seti's copy.

**A clock with a man in it.** On the ceilings of the tombs of Ramesses VI and Ramesses IX, around 1140 BC, a later kind
of star clock appears. One man sat on the ground facing the observer, so that the line to the pole star passed over the
middle of his head. For each hour, on different days of the year, the table names a star crossing or nearly crossing
the meridian and says where it stood as seen past the seated man: "over the left
eye", "over the right shoulder", "in the middle". It is a way of timing stars as they cross the meridian, the method
astronomers used for thousands of years after.

## 3.6 Clocks for day and night { #clocks }

**The merkhet.** The Egyptian word *merkhet* means "instrument of knowing". It was a plumb line hanging from a short
bar, used with a sighting stick, the *bay*, made from a palm rib with a V-shaped slit cut in one end. Lined up on the
pole, the pair gave a north-south line; along that line an observer could time stars crossing the meridian, or lay out
the axis of a temple. Two inscribed examples survive in the Berlin museum.

**Water clocks.** A stone bowl with a small hole near the bottom lets water out at a steady rate; marks inside show the
hours. The oldest water clock anyone has found comes from the temple of Amun-Re at Karnak, from the reign of Amenhotep
III, about 1417–1379 BC. Such clocks worked on cloudy nights and needed no stars.

**Shadow clocks.** By day, the Egyptians read the time from shadows. The oldest known sundial was found in the Valley of
the Kings and dates from about 1500 BC.

**Hours that stretch.** Egypt gave us twelve hours of day and twelve of night. But an Egyptian hour was not a fixed
length. Day and night were each divided into twelve, so in summer a day hour was long and a night hour short, and in
winter the other way round.

## 3.7 Facing north: the pyramids { #pyramids }

The sides of the Great Pyramid of Khufu at Giza, built around 2500 BC, face the four directions with an average error
of 3 minutes and 38 seconds of arc: about a sixteenth of a degree. Flinders Petrie first measured this with a
theodolite in 1881. A survey in 2006 by Nell and Ruggles confirmed the precision and showed that the north-south
axes of Khufu's pyramid and his son Khafre's next to it differ by only about half a minute of arc. No compass was used (the
Egyptians had none, and magnetic north is far from true north anyway). The question is how they found north, and
there are several proposals.

**The Sun.** Plant a stick upright, mark the tip of its shadow through a day, draw a circle round the stick, and join the
two points where the shadow tip crosses the circle: that is an east-west line. Tested in modern experiments, this gave
lines about 2 minutes of arc from true, about as good as the pyramids.

**The pole star.** In the third millennium BC the star nearest the north pole of the sky was **Thuban**, in the
constellation Draco. It was closest to the pole in 2830 BC, less than a sixth of a degree away; by 2500 BC it was
about two degrees off, so it circled the pole every day. Half-way between its furthest points east and west is true north.

**Two stars in a line.** In 2000 the Egyptologist Kate Spence proposed a sharper method. Around 2500 BC two bright stars
on opposite sides of the pole, Kochab in the Little Dipper and Mizar in the Big Dipper, lined up one above the other
when seen against a plumb line, and that vertical line passed almost through the pole. Hang a plumb line, wait until
the two stars are on it, and you are facing north. But because of precession (chapter 2, section 2.3), the line between
the two stars swings slowly past the pole. A pyramid laid out this way would face slightly east or west of north
depending on its date. Spence found that the alignments of the Old Kingdom pyramids do drift in step with this swing,
and used it to date the start of the Great Pyramid to 2467 BC, give or take five years, some 80 years later than
conventional dates.

**The objections.** The idea was taken seriously and challenged at once:

- Dennis Rawlins and Keith Pickering argued in *Nature* in 2001 that Spence's calculation contained an error, and that
  when it is corrected another pair of stars fits better, which would push the dates earlier, not later.
- Juan Antonio Belmonte proposed instead that the builders timed two stars of the Big Dipper, Phecda and Megrez, which
  point towards Thuban, as they crossed the meridian.
- A shadow method gives about the same accuracy, so the stars may not have been used at all.

The argument now is about which stars, which method, and whether any of them can give dates to within five years.

**Working it out: years from minutes of arc.** Spence's star line swings past the pole by about 27 minutes of arc per
century, so 0.27′ per year. An error of 3.6′ then corresponds to $3.6 / 0.27 \approx 13$ years from the moment the line
passed exactly through the pole. That is why a careful survey of a few minutes of arc can, if the method is right,
pin down decades.

## 3.8 The Dendera zodiac { #dendera }

In the temple of the goddess Hathor at Dendera, a chapel to Osiris had a round carved ceiling: a disc of the sky held
up by four women and four pairs of falcon-headed figures. It is the only complete round map of the sky to survive from
the ancient world.

**What it shows.** In the middle, the stars round the north pole, with the Little Dipper drawn as a jackal. Around them,
the twelve signs of the zodiac, most in their Greek and Babylonian forms (the ram, the bull, the scorpion, the goat-fish),
but Aquarius drawn as Hapi, the Egyptian god of the flood, pouring water from two jars. Around the outside, 36 figures
for the 36 decans. Babylon's zodiac and Egypt's decans are on one ceiling.

**When it was made.** By the five planets shown in the signs, a configuration that happens about once in a thousand
years, and by two eclipses that seem to be drawn on it (a solar eclipse of 7 March 51 BC and a lunar eclipse of 25
September 52 BC), Sylvie Cauville and Éric Aubourg dated it to about 50 BC, near the end of the Ptolemaic kings. The
Louvre gives the same date.

It was not always so clear. In 1821 a French expedition cut the ceiling out with saws, jacks and gunpowder and took it to
Paris, where it caused a sensation. Some, including the mathematician Joseph Fourier, thought the sky on it showed a
date around 2500 BC or earlier; others put it in Roman times. The
young Jean-François Champollion, who was then reading hieroglyphs for the first time, argued that it was Greek or Roman,
and he was right. The ceiling is in the Louvre, and the Egyptologist Zahi Hawass has campaigned for its
return to Egypt.

## 3.9 A variable star in a calendar of lucky days? { #algol }

Some Egyptian papyri are calendars of lucky and unlucky days: for each day of the year, a forecast (good, bad, or
mixed) and often a story about the gods. The best preserved is the **Cairo Calendar**, written around 1244–1163 BC.

**The claim.** In 2013 a team led by the Finnish astronomer Lauri Jetsu analysed the pattern of good days with the
statistical tools astronomers use to find periods in starlight. They found two cycles: one of 29.6 days, close to the
Moon's month, and one of 2.850 days. They pointed out that the star **Algol**, in Perseus, dims for a few hours every
2.867 days, because a fainter companion passes in front of it. That period was discovered in 1783 by John Goodricke.
The team argued that Egyptian priests had noticed Algol's dimming, that the 2.850-day cycle is its period 3,200 years
ago (slightly shorter than today), and that Algol appears in the texts as the god Horus. If so, the Cairo Calendar is
the oldest record of a variable star.

**The case against.** The claim has been questioned. Rolf Krauss and Victor Reijs, in 2021, asked whether these
almanacs show any real sky cycle at all. There is a general reason for caution too: with 365 days of good-or-bad
labels, testing many possible periods can turn up patterns by chance (the same problem chapter 1, section 1.12 met
with alignments).

**Verdict.** A bold, testable idea, published in good journals and still disputed. It is not established.

      *Astrophysical Journal* 773 (2013), 1; L. Jetsu and S. Porceddu,
      [Shifting milestones of natural sciences: the ancient Egyptian discovery of Algol's period confirmed](https://arxiv.org/abs/1601.06990),
      *PLOS ONE* 10 (2015), e0144140, which gives the date 1244–1163 BC and the 29.6-day period.

## 3.10 A leap day at last { #leap }

In 238 BC, under the Greek king Ptolemy III, a great meeting of Egyptian priests at Canopus passed a decree in three
scripts, hieroglyphs, demotic and Greek. Among other things it ordered that every fourth year should have six added days
instead of five, so that the festivals would stop wandering. It was the first official year of 365¼ days. It did not
last: the priests and people kept to the old count, and the reform lapsed.

Two centuries later the idea won. In 46 BC Julius Caesar reformed the Roman calendar with the help, according to Pliny,
of the astronomer **Sosigenes of Alexandria**, giving the Julian year of 365¼ days, with a leap day every four years.
In the 20s BC, under Augustus, Egypt's own calendar was given the same leap day, creating the Alexandrian calendar.

**Still running.** That calendar lives on as the **Coptic calendar**: twelve months of 30 days with the old Egyptian
names (Thout, Paopi, Hathor and so on), and a short thirteenth month of five or six days. The Coptic Church uses it, and
farmers in Egypt, Muslim and Christian alike, still use its months for the farming year. The Egyptian state used it for
taxes until 1875. Ethiopia's calendar is built the same way (chapter 10).

## 3.11 Working it out: the wandering year { #working }

**How fast does the calendar wander against the seasons?** The year of the seasons, equinox to equinox, is 365.2422
days. The civil year falls short by 0.2422 days a year, so the calendar goes once round the seasons in

$$\frac{365.2422}{0.2422} \approx 1{,}508 \text{ years}.$$

**Against Sirius?** The interval between risings of Sirius was then very close to 365.25 days (a little different from
the year of the seasons because of precession and the star's position far from the Sun's path). Against Sirius the
calendar loses 0.25 days a year, so the cycle is $365.25 / 0.25 = 1{,}461$ civil years, which is $1{,}460$ years of
365¼ days. The two cycles differ by about 50 years: over a Sothic cycle the rising of Sirius itself drifts against the
seasons.

**Counting back from Censorinus.** If Sirius rose on 1 Thoth in AD 139, then whole cycles earlier: $139 - 1460 = -1321$
and $139 - 2920 = -2781$ in astronomers' years, that is 1322 BC and 2782 BC. (Remember from chapter 2 that the astronomers'
year 0 is 1 BC.) Because Sirius rises on the same calendar day for four years in a row, these are only good to a few
years, which is why books give "about 1320 BC" and "about 2780 BC".

**A calendar for Mars.** A Mars calendar with a whole number of sols would face the same choice Egypt faced:

| | Mars | Earth |
|---|---|---|
| Year of the seasons | 668.59 sols | 365.2422 days |
| Calendar year with no leap days | 668 sols | 365 days |
| Shortfall each year | 0.59 sols | 0.24 days |
| Time to wander once round the seasons | about 1,130 Mars years | about 1,508 years |

Without leap days a Mars calendar would wander faster than Egypt's, by more than half a sol a year. Any practical Mars
calendar has to add a leap sol in more than half of all years.

    - The Sothic year of about 365.25 days and Censorinus's AD 139: Wikipedia, [Sothic cycle](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sothic_cycle) (weaker).
    - The Mars year (tropical orbit period 686.972 days) and the sol (24.6597 hours): NASA,

## 3.12 How much did Egypt know? { #assessment }

Egypt is often compared with Babylon, and the comparison is useful.

**What Egypt did superbly.** Practical timekeeping: a simple, perfectly regular calendar that astronomers still found
useful 2,000 years later; star clocks and water clocks for the night; shadow clocks for the day; and the 24 hours.
Surveying: the pyramids' alignment to north is one of the most precise things built in the ancient world.

**What we do not find.** There are no long series of dated observations like the Babylonian Diaries, and no Egyptian
text from before the Greek period predicts eclipses or computes where the planets will be. Some of this may be bad luck: papyrus survives
much worse than clay, and the temple libraries are lost. But the evidence we have points to an astronomy of time and of
religion, not of prediction.

**What came after.** After Alexander's conquest in 332 BC, Egypt became the home of Greek science. In Alexandria,
Babylonian methods, Egyptian calendars and Greek geometry met, and the result was the astronomy of Hipparchus and
Ptolemy (chapter 7). The Dendera zodiac shows the meeting in stone; the Coptic calendar carries the Egyptian year into
the present.

A word of warning about the many popular claims that the pyramids encode the speed of light, the distance to the Sun or
the layout of Orion's belt. None of these has support from Egyptologists or historians of astronomy, and they rest on
picking numbers until something fits. Section 3.7 shows what a careful astronomical argument about the pyramids looks
like, and even that one is disputed.

??? note "Sources"
    - The Ptolemaic and Roman blending of Egyptian, Babylonian and Greek astronomy in Alexandria: Wikipedia,
      [Egyptian astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Egyptian_astronomy) (weaker).
    - For a careful survey of what is and is not known, M. Clagett, *Ancient Egyptian Science*, vol. 2: *Calendars,
      Clocks, and Astronomy* (American Philosophical Society, 1995), listed under Further reading.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) How many days were in the Egyptian civil year, and how were they arranged?

??? note "Answer"
    365: twelve months of 30 days (three seasons of four months each), plus five extra days at the end of the year.

**2.** (beginner) What is the heliacal rising of Sirius, and why did it matter to the Egyptians?

??? note "Answer"
    It is the first morning after its weeks of invisibility when Sirius can be seen low in the east just before
    sunrise. In Egypt it came in summer, about when the Nile began to flood, so it marked the start of the year.

**3.** (beginner) Why does a diagonal star table have diagonals?

??? note "Answer"
    Each decan rises about 40 minutes earlier every ten days, so in the next ten-day column it marks the hour before.
    Its name moves up one row per column, and the repeated names form diagonal lines.

**4.** (intermediate) In AD 139 the first of Thoth fell on 20 July in the Julian calendar. On what Julian date did it
fall in AD 239?

??? note "Answer"
    The Egyptian year is a quarter of a day shorter than the Julian year, so 1 Thoth comes one Julian day earlier every
    four years. In 100 years that is 25 days earlier: 25 June.

**5.** (intermediate) Give two reasons why a recorded rising of Sirius cannot date a king's reign exactly.

??? note "Answer"
    The day of the rising depends on where it was seen, and the place is often unknown; Sirius rises on the same civil
    date for four years running; and the first sighting depends on the weather and the watcher. Any two of these.

**6.** (advanced) At midsummer in Thebes a day hour lasted about 69 minutes. How long was a night hour, and why?

??? note "Answer"
    Day and night together are 24 hours of 60 minutes, 1,440 minutes, split into 12 day hours and 12 night hours. The
    day was $12 \times 69 = 828$ minutes, so the night was $1440 - 828 = 612$ minutes, and a night hour was
    $612 / 12 = 51$ minutes.

**7.** (advanced) On Spence's figure of 27′ per century, how many years correspond to an alignment error of 1′? Why is
it hard to turn this into an exact date?

??? note "Answer"
    $1 / 0.27 \approx 3.7$ years. But it assumes the builders used exactly that pair of stars, sighted perfectly; if they
    used another pair, or another method, or made a small error in the sighting, the date shifts by decades. That is the
    point the critics make.

**8.** (expert) Why does the Sothic cycle (1,460 years) differ from the time the civil calendar takes to wander round
the seasons (about 1,508 years)? What does this mean for the date of Sirius's rising in the year of the seasons?

??? note "Answer"
    The interval between risings of Sirius was about 365.25 days, while the year of the seasons is 365.2422 days. The
    calendar loses 0.25 days a year on Sirius but only 0.2422 days on the seasons, so it takes longer to wander round the
    seasons. The difference means that Sirius's rising itself moves later in the year of the seasons by about 0.008 days
    a year, roughly 11 days in 1,460 years. Over the millennia of Egyptian history the herald of the flood slowly drifted
    against the flood.

## Summary

- Egypt's farming depended on the Nile flood, but the flood is too irregular to be a calendar; the sky gave a steadier
  mark.
- The civil year had 365 days (12 × 30 + 5) and no leap day, so it wandered through the seasons, a day every four years.
- The return of Sirius to the dawn sky heralded the flood. The Sothic cycle of 1,460 years, fixed by Censorinus's AD 139,
  helps date the pharaohs, but with error bars of years to decades.
- The 36 decans rose one after another through the night; diagonal star tables painted on coffin lids from about 2100 BC
  record them. Their stars are mostly unidentified.
- Senenmut's ceiling (about 1470 BC) is the oldest astronomical ceiling; the Book of Nut explains the decans; Ramesside
  tombs show star clocks timed against a seated man.
- Merkhet and bay, water clocks (Karnak, about 1400 BC) and shadow clocks (about 1500 BC) told the time. Egypt's twelve
  hours of day and twelve of night were of changing length; equal hours of 60 minutes came later, joining Egypt and
  Babylon.
- The Great Pyramid faces north to within about 3.6′. Spence's star method dates it to 2467 BC; critics dispute both the
  star pair and the precision.
- The Dendera zodiac (about 50 BC) puts Babylon's zodiac and Egypt's decans on one ceiling.
- The claim that the Cairo Calendar recorded Algol's 2.85-day period is bold and disputed.
- A leap day was decreed in 238 BC but failed; it arrived with Caesar and Augustus. The Coptic calendar still runs on the
  Egyptian year.
