# 7. Greece and Alexandria

*Babylon gave the Greeks centuries of numbers. The Greeks added geometry: they asked what shape the world is, how big
it is and how far away the Sun and Moon are, and they built pictures of the heavens out of circles that could be
calculated. A librarian measured the Earth with a shadow. An astronomer on Rhodes found that the whole sky slowly
drifts. Someone built a bronze computer of the heavens, which sank in a shipwreck. And in Roman Alexandria Ptolemy
wrote the book that ruled astronomy in Europe and the Islamic world for fourteen centuries. This chapter also shows
how much of this story rests on lost books, late reports and arguments that are still going on.*

## 7.1 The first Greek thinkers { #ionians }

Greek science began in the 6th century BC in the cities of Ionia, on the coast of what is now Turkey.

**Thales' eclipse.** The historian Herodotus, writing about 150 years later, says that **Thales** of Miletus predicted
a solar eclipse that stopped a battle between the Lydians and the Medes; day turned to night and the two sides made
peace. The only eclipse that fits is the one of **28 May 585 BC**. If the story is true it is the earliest eclipse known
to have been predicted. But nobody knows how Thales could have done it, and modern scholars doubt the story. Even the
Babylonian eclipse cycles of later centuries could only flag a possible eclipse, not say whether it would be seen
(chapter 2).

**Shapes of the world.** Thales is said to have thought the Earth floats on water like a log. **Anaximander** pictured it
as a short cylinder with a flat top, which stayed where it was because it was the same distance from everything. The idea
of a round Earth appears with the followers of Pythagoras. One of them, **Philolaus** (5th century BC), even took the
Earth out of the centre: the Earth, Sun, Moon and planets all circled a central fire, with an unseen "counter-Earth" to
make the number of moving bodies ten. Nearly two thousand years later Copernicus mentioned Philolaus as someone who
had made the Earth move.

## 7.2 A round Earth { #round }

Plato wrote about a spherical Earth in the early 4th century BC. By about 330 BC his student **Aristotle** gave evidence
for it in his book *On the Heavens*:

- **Eclipses of the Moon.** The Moon shows every kind of shape in a month, "but in eclipses the outline is always
  curved". Since the eclipse is the Earth's shadow, the Earth must be round.
- **Travelling changes the stars.** Even a small trip north or south changes which stars are overhead. "There are some
  stars seen in Egypt and in the neighbourhood of Cyprus which are not seen in the northerly regions." So the Earth is
  not only round but "a sphere of no great size".

He added that mathematicians had put its circumference at 400,000 stades. After Aristotle the round Earth spread
across the Hellenistic world and beyond, and by the early Christian centuries it was widely held. The story that
medieval scholars thought the Earth was flat is a modern myth (chapter 9).

## 7.3 Cycles and spheres { #spheres }

**Meton.** In 432 BC **Meton** of Athens, working with **Euctemon**, brought into the Athenian calendar the cycle of
**19 years = 235 months**. He timed the solstices by watching where the Sun rose: from his observing place it rose
over Mount Lycabettus at the summer solstice and over Mount Hymettus at the winter one. He was famous enough to be
mocked as a character in Aristophanes' comedy *The Birds* (414 BC). Babylon was using the same 19-year cycle by then
(chapter 2); whether Meton learned it from Babylon or found it himself is not known. A century later **Callippus**
joined four Metonic cycles into one of 76 years, dropping one day, and started it at the summer solstice of 330 BC.

**Eudoxus.** The planets were the hard part: they move unevenly and sometimes go backwards (retrograde) for weeks.
**Eudoxus** of Cnidus, who had studied for 16 months at Heliopolis in Egypt, built the first geometric model for this.
Each body sat on a set of spheres nested inside one another, all centred on the Earth, each turning on its own axis.
For a planet, the outer sphere gave the daily turn, the next its slow path round the zodiac, and the two inner spheres,
turning the same speed in opposite directions, swung it along a figure-of-eight curve, the *hippopede* or "horse
fetter", that produced the backward loops. He used 27 spheres in all; Callippus added seven more.

The model could not explain why planets change brightness, since every planet stayed the same distance from the Earth.
But it set the Greek programme: explain every wandering in the sky with uniform circular motion.

## 7.4 Aristarchus: how far is the Sun? { #aristarchus }

**Aristarchus** of Samos (about 310 to 230 BC) wrote the only book of his that survives, *On the Sizes and Distances of
the Sun and Moon*. Its idea is beautiful. At half moon the Sun lights exactly half the Moon's face as we see it, so the
angle Sun–Moon–Earth is a right angle. Measure the angle between the Sun and the Moon in the sky at that moment, and you
have the shape of a right triangle, and so the ratio of the distances.

Aristarchus took the angle to be 87°. That gave the Sun **between 18 and 20 times** farther than the Moon, and, since
the two look the same size in the sky, between 18 and 20 times larger. The true angle is 89.85°, and the Sun is about
**390 times** farther (section 7.11). The method was right; the angle was simply too hard to measure, because a tiny
error near 90° makes an enormous difference.

**The Sun at the centre.** Aristarchus also proposed that the Earth turns daily on its axis and goes round the Sun
once a year. His book on this is lost. We know of it from **Archimedes**, who described it in *The Sand Reckoner*
when estimating how many grains of sand would fill the universe. Archimedes also says that Aristarchus found the Sun's
apparent diameter to be half a degree, which is about right; the surviving book's "2 degrees" seems to come from a
misread word.

Plutarch tells a story about Aristarchus and Cleanthes, the head of the Stoics. In printed editions since one made in
the 17th century, Cleanthes says Aristarchus should be charged with impiety for moving the Earth. In the manuscript, it is the
other way round: Aristarchus jokes that Cleanthes should be charged. So the popular tale of Aristarchus as a persecuted
heretic is weaker than it looks.

## 7.5 Eratosthenes measures the Earth { #eratosthenes }

**Eratosthenes** was born in 276 BC in Cyrene, in what is now Libya, and became chief librarian of the Library of
Alexandria. He worked on prime numbers (the "sieve of Eratosthenes"), on dating historical events, and on geography.
Critics called him "Beta", second best at everything. His most famous result is the size of the Earth, around 240 BC.

His own book is lost. The version that survives was simplified by the later writer **Cleomedes**:

- At Syene (now Aswan), at noon on midsummer day, the Sun was straight overhead: sticks cast no shadow, and sunlight
  reached the bottom of a well.
- At the same moment in Alexandria, to the north, a stick's shadow showed the Sun was 1/50 of a circle (7.2°) from
  overhead.
- The Sun is so far away that its rays arrive parallel. So the two vertical lines meet at the centre of the Earth at the
  same 7.2°, and the distance from Syene to Alexandria is 1/50 of the way round.
- That distance was taken as 5,000 stadia, so the Earth is $50 \times 5{,}000 = 250{,}000$ stadia round.

Roman writers give **252,000** stadia, a number that divides evenly by every whole number from 1 to 10. Whether that
was Eratosthenes' own adjustment is debated.

**How good was it?** It depends on how long his *stadion* was, and nobody knows. With a stadion of 155 to 160 m the
answer is within about 2% of the truth; with the Olympic stadion of 176.4 m or the Italian of 184.8 m it is 10% to 15%
too big. Two assumptions were also a little off: Syene is not exactly on the Tropic of Cancer, and it is about 3° east
of Alexandria, not due south. Whatever the exact number, the method is sound, and it was the first good measurement
of the planet.

Later **Posidonius** tried another way: the star Canopus just grazed the horizon at Rhodes but rose 7½° at Alexandria.
(The real difference in latitude is 5°14′.)

## 7.6 Hipparchus { #hipparchus }

**Hipparchus** (about 190 to 120 BC) was born in Nicaea (now İznik, Turkey) and worked mostly on the island of Rhodes;
his dated observations run from 147 to 127 BC, and perhaps from 162 BC. He wrote at least fourteen books, but only one
survives, a commentary on a popular poem about the constellations. Most of what we know of him comes through Ptolemy.

**He used Babylon.** Hipparchus drew on centuries of Babylonian eclipse records and on Babylonian period relations.
By comparing eclipses of his own time with Babylonian eclipses 345 years (4,267 months) earlier, he confirmed the
Babylonian value of the month, 29;31,50,8,20 days in base sixty, which is 29.530594 days (chapter 2).

**Precession.** He measured the positions of bright stars such as Spica and compared them with those recorded by
Timocharis and Aristyllus in the century before. Spica was about 2° further from the autumn equinox. He concluded
that the stars (or rather the equinoxes) were slowly shifting, the **precession of the equinoxes**, which is now known
to be caused by the slow wobble of the Earth's axis (chapter 2, section 2.9).

**A star catalogue.** Pliny says Hipparchus saw a **new star** and, wondering whether the stars change, set out to list
them all for posterity with their positions and brightness, about 850 stars. The catalogue was lost. Then in 2022
scholars using multispectral imaging found part of it hidden under a later Christian text in a reused parchment, the
**Codex Climaci Rescriptus**, from St Catherine's Monastery in Sinai. It describes the Northern Crown (Corona Borealis)
in an unusual notation thought to be Hipparchus' own, with coordinates matching the sky of about 129 BC to **within one
degree**. That is more accurate than Ptolemy's catalogue, and the positions differ from Ptolemy's, so Ptolemy's later
catalogue was not simply a copy of his.

**Trigonometry.** Hipparchus made the first known table of chords (the ancestor of the sine table), in steps of 7.5°,
which let him compute the shapes of the Sun's and Moon's orbits. He is often called the founder of trigonometry.

## 7.7 The Antikythera mechanism { #antikythera }

In 1900 sponge divers from the island of Symi found an ancient shipwreck off the small Greek island of Antikythera.
Among the finds brought up in 1900 and 1901 were lumps of corroded bronze. In 1902 a visitor to the National
Archaeological Museum in Athens noticed a **gear** in one of them.

It took a century to understand what it was. The historian Derek de Solla Price studied it from 1951, and in 1971 he and
the physicist Charalampos Karakalos X-rayed the 82 fragments. In 2005 a team used X-ray computed tomography to see
inside them. Only about a third of the machine survives, with **30 bronze gears**.

**What it did.** Turning a handle moved pointers on dials:

- on the front, the Sun and Moon round the zodiac, and a ring with the Egyptian calendar of twelve 30-day months and
  five extra days; a little half-white, half-black ball turned to show the Moon's phase;
- on the back, a spiral dial for the **19-year Metonic cycle**, with month names from a Corinthian calendar; a dial for
  the four-year cycle of the games, including the Olympic, Isthmian, Nemean and Pythian games; and a spiral for the **Saros eclipse cycle** of 223
  months, marking which months would have eclipses;
- the Moon's pointer sped up and slowed down, using two gears linked by a pin in a slot, to copy the Moon's uneven
  motion.

In 2016 researchers read the numbers 462 and 442 in the Venus and Saturn sections of an inscription on the front
cover. They give very accurate period relations for those planets, ones that can be built from gears of sensible size. In 2021 a team working with University College London published a model of the lost front:
rings for the Moon, Mercury, Venus, Sun, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn in their old cosmic order, needing 69 gears in all.
That front is a reconstruction, not a find.

**When and who?** The ship sank about 70 to 60 BC. The mechanism has been dated to about 205 BC (from the start of its
eclipse dial), to 150 to 100 BC, or to about 87 BC; the question is open. Cicero mentions geared spheres made by
Archimedes and by Posidonius, which shows that such machines were known, but the Antikythera device is not either of
them.

It is the oldest known geared calculator. Later geared devices in the Byzantine and Islamic worlds, such as a calendar
gear attached to a Byzantine sundial of the 5th or 6th century, were simpler.

## 7.8 Ptolemy and the *Almagest* { #ptolemy }

**Claudius Ptolemy** (about AD 100 to the 160s or 170s) lived in or near Alexandria, then part of the Roman Empire. His
great work, written around AD 150, was called the *Mathematical Syntaxis* ("treatise"). Later it was called the
"greatest", which in Arabic became *al-majisti*, and so in Latin *Almagest*.

It does what no earlier surviving book does: it takes every motion in the sky, the Sun, the Moon, the five planets and
the stars, and gives for each a geometric model with numbers, worked out from observations, from which positions can be
calculated for any date. It includes a catalogue of **1,022 stars** in **48 constellations**, the ancestors of many of
today's.

**The equant.** For the planets Ptolemy used a big circle (the **deferent**) carrying a small circle (the
**epicycle**), with the planet on the epicycle. Earlier models could not fit the observations, so he added a new
device: the Earth sits a little off the deferent's centre, and the epicycle's centre moves at a steady rate not as seen
from the Earth, nor from the centre, but from a third point, the **equant**, on the other side of the centre (figure in
section 7.9). It worked very well. But it broke the rule that heavenly motion should be uniform about a centre, and
astronomers in the Islamic world and later Copernicus spent centuries trying to remove it (chapters 8 and 13).

**Did he copy Hipparchus?** A long-standing charge is that Ptolemy took Hipparchus' star positions, added 2°40′ for
precession at his too-small rate of 1° per century, and passed them off as his own. Many historians think this charge is
exaggerated and judges an ancient author by modern rules of citation. The 2022 fragment (section 7.6) adds evidence that
Ptolemy's catalogue was not based only on Hipparchus' data.

He also wrote the *Handy Tables* for quick calculation, the *Planetary Hypotheses* on the physical arrangement of the
heavens, the *Tetrabiblos* on astrology, and the *Geography*, a guide to map-making.

**How it survived.** The oldest copies of the *Almagest* are from the 9th century, when it was translated into Arabic. In
1175 Gerard of Cremona in Spain translated it from Arabic into Latin. An earlier Latin translation made straight from
Greek had little influence, and Gerard's version stayed the standard until Greek copies came back into use in the
15th century.

## 7.9 Mars in the Greek sky { #mars }

In Plato's time the Greeks called Mars "the star of Ares", their god of war; Hellenistic Greeks also called it
**Pyroeis**, "the fiery one". The Romans turned "star of Ares" into "star of Mars".

Mars was a hard test for the models. Historians think that a model between Hipparchus' and Ptolemy's was built to fit
the observed motion of Mars.

In Ptolemy's model there is a strange coincidence. For Mars, Jupiter and Saturn, the line from the epicycle's centre to
the planet always stays **parallel to the line from the Earth to the Sun**. The model works, but it never says why the
Sun should control the planets in this way. Section 7.11 shows that the coincidence hides the Earth's own orbit: the
epicycle is the Earth's year, seen from the wrong place.

Even the best tables were not perfect for Mars. At a conjunction of planets in 1504, Copernicus wrote in his copy of
the medieval *Alfonsine Tables*, built on Ptolemy's models, that "Mars surpasses the numbers by more than two
degrees". Owen Gingerich checked with modern calculation and found Mars ahead of the tables by nearly two degrees. It was Mars that finally broke circles altogether, in Kepler's hands (chapter 14).

## 7.10 A wider Greek world { #wider }

"Greek astronomy" was never only Greek.

**Babylon.** Hipparchus and Ptolemy built on Babylonian eclipse records and period relations (section 7.6 and
chapter 2). **Seleucus of Seleucia** (born about 190 BC), probably from Seleucia on the Tigris in Mesopotamia, supported
Aristarchus' Sun-centred model. Plutarch says that where Aristarchus offered it as a hypothesis, Seleucus held it as a
definite opinion and gave a demonstration of it, perhaps from the tides. The geographer Strabo counted him among the
"Chaldaean", that is Babylonian, astronomers.

**Egypt.** Alexandria, where Eratosthenes, Ptolemy and Hypatia worked, was an Egyptian city with a Greek-speaking court.
Eudoxus studied at Heliopolis, and the Antikythera mechanism carried the Egyptian 365-day calendar on its front
(chapter 3).

**India.** Greek astronomy and astrology travelled east and were taken up and changed in India (chapter 5).

**Hypatia.** The last famous astronomer of ancient Alexandria was a woman. **Hypatia** (born about 350 to 370, died
415) was the daughter of the mathematician **Theon**. Her father's commentary on the *Almagest* suggests that she edited
the surviving text of Book III. She made astrolabes and hydrometers, though she invented neither; her pupil Synesius
wrote asking her to build him a hydrometer. In March 415 she was murdered by a Christian mob during a political feud in the city. Films and
television, including Carl Sagan's *Cosmos*, have linked her death to the burning of the Library of Alexandria, but the
library no longer existed in her lifetime.

## 7.11 Working it out { #working }

**Eratosthenes on Earth and on Mars.** If a Martian Eratosthenes found the Sun overhead at one place and 7.2° from
overhead at another, how far apart would they be? Using mean radii:

| | Mars | Earth |
|---|---|---|
| Mean radius | 3,389.5 km | 6,371.0 km |
| Circumference $2\pi r$ | 21,297 km | 40,030 km |
| 1/50 of the way round (7.2°) | 426 km | 801 km |
| One degree | 59.2 km | 111.2 km |

On Mars the same shadow angle needs only about half the walk. Alexandria and Aswan are really 7.1° apart in latitude,
about 790 km.

**Aristarchus' triangle.** At half moon the angle at the Moon is 90°, so if the Sun–Moon angle seen from Earth is
$\varphi$,

$$\frac{\text{Sun distance}}{\text{Moon distance}} = \frac{1}{\cos\varphi}.$$

| Angle $\varphi$ | Ratio |
|---|---|
| 87° (Aristarchus) | 19.1 |
| 89° | 57 |
| 89.5° | 115 |
| 89.85° (true) | 389 |

A change of less than 3° in the angle changes the answer twentyfold. That is why the method, though right, gave a poor
result.

**Meton's cycle.** Using today's year and month,

$$19 \times 365.24219 = 6{,}939.60 \text{ days}, \qquad 235 \times 29.530589 = 6{,}939.69 \text{ days}.$$

They differ by about 0.09 days, about 2 hours, so the cycle drifts by a day in roughly two centuries.

**The year hidden in Ptolemy's Mars.** In Ptolemy's model the epicycle's centre goes round the deferent in Mars's
period against the stars, 686.98 days, and Mars goes round the epicycle once each **synodic period**, the time between
one opposition and the next, 779.94 days. Add the two rates:

$$\frac{1}{686.98} + \frac{1}{779.94} = \frac{1}{365.26}.$$

So the arm on the epicycle turns once in 365.26 days, exactly the Earth's year against the stars. The epicycle is the
Earth's orbit around the Sun, copied onto Mars. Copernicus saw this, and it is one of the strongest reasons for putting
the Sun at the centre. For the same years from Mars's side, see
[The Mars year](https://physics.akadian.ca/topics/spacetime/level-4/04-the-mars-year.html).

## 7.12 How should we judge Greek astronomy? { #assessment }

**What was new.** The Greeks turned astronomy into geometry. Babylon could predict when things would happen with
arithmetic; the Greeks asked what shape and size things were, and drew models in space. Measuring the Earth with a
shadow, and estimating the distances of the Sun and Moon with triangles, were new kinds of question. Ptolemy's
*Almagest* was the first complete, quantitative model of everything in the sky, and it worked well enough to be used for
more than a thousand years.

**What was borrowed.** A great deal of the data was Babylonian, and the Greeks said so: Hipparchus and Ptolemy quote
Babylonian eclipses. The calendar Ptolemy used was Egyptian. Telling the story as a "Greek miracle" leaves these
partners out.

**What was lost.** Aristarchus' book on the Sun-centred world, Eratosthenes' book on the Earth, nearly all of
Hipparchus, and the Antikythera mechanism's makers are all gone. Much of what we know comes through later summaries,
Arabic translations and a palimpsest read with modern imaging. Some famous stories, such as Thales' eclipse, the
persecuted Aristarchus and Hypatia and the Library, do not survive a close look at the sources.

**What was wrong.** The Earth stayed at the centre, and circles stayed sacred. The equant was a brilliant patch on that
idea. The real answer came from Mars, in chapter 14.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Give one of Aristotle's two reasons for believing the Earth is a sphere.

??? note "Answer"
    The Earth's shadow on the Moon during a lunar eclipse is always curved; or, travelling a short way north or south
    changes which stars are seen, so the surface is curved (and the Earth not very large).

**2.** (beginner) What did Eratosthenes need to know to measure the Earth?

??? note "Answer"
    The angle of the Sun from overhead at Alexandria when it was overhead at Syene (7.2°, 1/50 of a circle), and the
    distance between the two places (5,000 stadia). Then the circumference is 50 times the distance.

**3.** (beginner) What was found in the Codex Climaci Rescriptus in 2022?

??? note "Answer"
    Part of Hipparchus' lost star catalogue, hidden under a later text in a reused parchment and read by multispectral
    imaging. It gives positions in the Northern Crown that match the sky of about 129 BC to within a degree.

**4.** (intermediate) What is precession, and how did Hipparchus notice it?

??? note "Answer"
    The slow drift of the equinoxes against the stars, caused by the wobble of the Earth's axis. Hipparchus found that
    Spica was about 2° further from the autumn equinox than in observations made by Timocharis and Aristyllus in the
    century before.

**5.** (intermediate) Name three cycles shown on the back of the Antikythera mechanism.

??? note "Answer"
    The 19-year Metonic cycle, the 223-month Saros eclipse cycle, and the four-year cycle of the games including the
    Olympics. (The 76-year Callippic cycle also appears.)

**6.** (intermediate) What is an equant, and why did later astronomers dislike it?

??? note "Answer"
    A point, off the centre of a planet's deferent and opposite the Earth, from which the epicycle's centre appears to
    move at a steady rate. It broke the principle of uniform motion about a circle's own centre, so astronomers from
    al-Tusi to Copernicus tried to replace it.

**7.** (advanced) Aristarchus measured 87° between the Sun and the half moon. What ratio of distances does this give,
and what would 89.85° give?

??? note "Answer"
    $1/\cos 87^\circ \approx 19.1$, and $1/\cos 89.85^\circ \approx 389$. The Sun is really about 390 times as far as
    the Moon.

**8.** (expert) Jupiter goes round the Sun in 4,332.59 days, and its synodic period is 398.88 days. In Ptolemy's model,
how long does Jupiter's arm on its epicycle take to turn once against the stars? What does this show?

??? note "Answer"
    $1/4{,}332.59 + 1/398.88 = 0.000231 + 0.002507 = 0.002738$ turns per day, so once every
    $1/0.002738 \approx 365.25$ days.
    As with Mars, every outer planet's epicycle is really the Earth's yearly orbit seen from the Earth, which points to a
    Sun-centred system.

## Summary

- Herodotus credits Thales with predicting the eclipse of 585 BC, but scholars doubt it. Anaximander's Earth was a
  cylinder; the Pythagoreans made it round, and Philolaus even moved it.
- Aristotle (about 330 BC) argued from eclipse shadows and travelling stars that the Earth is a fairly small sphere.
- Meton brought in the 19-year cycle in 432 BC; Eudoxus built nested spheres to explain planetary loops.
- Aristarchus' half-moon method was sound but gave the Sun 18 to 20 times the Moon's distance instead of about 390; he
  also proposed a Sun-centred world.
- Eratosthenes (around 240 BC) found the Earth to be 250,000 stadia round; how accurate that was depends on a stadion we
  cannot pin down.
- Hipparchus found precession, used Babylonian data, began trigonometry and made a star catalogue, part of which was
  found in 2022.
- The Antikythera mechanism (2nd century BC, sunk about 70 to 60 BC) modelled the Sun, Moon, calendars and eclipses
  with gears.
- Ptolemy's *Almagest* (about AD 150), with epicycles and the equant, ruled astronomy for 1,400 years; in its Mars model
  the Earth's own year is hidden.
- Babylon, Egypt and India were partners in this story, and Hypatia was the last great teacher of ancient Alexandria.
