# 14. Tycho and Kepler

*Copernicus had moved the Earth, but his system was no more accurate than Ptolemy's. What was missing was better data,
and a mind that would not let a small error go. Tycho Brahe, a Danish nobleman, built the best observatory in Europe
and measured the planets about five times better than anyone before him, without a telescope. Johannes Kepler, a
short-sighted mathematics teacher, took Tycho's observations of Mars and spent years trying to fit them, until he found
that the orbit was not a circle at all. This chapter follows both men, the arguments between them, and the three laws
that came out of Mars.*

## 14.1 A nobleman with a metal nose { #tycho }

Tycho Brahe was born on **14 December 1546** at Knutstorp, his family's castle in Scania, which was then part of
Denmark (it has been Swedish since 1658). He came from one of the great noble families of Denmark, and was expected
to serve the king. He chose the sky instead.

As a student at Rostock, on **29 December 1566**, he fought a sword duel in the dark with a cousin and lost part of
his nose. For the rest of his life he wore a false nose. It was said to be made of silver and gold; when his body was examined
in 2010, the traces showed it was probably brass.

His first and keenest helper was his younger sister **Sophie Brahe**, who worked with him at his observatories. Against
the custom of his class he lived with Kirsten Jørgensdatter, a pastor's daughter, in a marriage that was never
recognised as noble, so their children could not inherit his rank. They had eight, six of whom grew up.

## 14.2 A new star and a comet { #newstar }

**The star of 1572.** On **11 November 1572** Tycho saw a bright star in Cassiopeia where none had been before. It grew
as bright as Venus. Aristotle had taught that everything above the Moon was perfect and never changed, so a new star
had to be something close, in the air. Tycho measured it night after night and found that it showed **no daily
parallax**: it did not shift against the stars as the Earth turned, as the Moon does. So it was farther away than the
Moon, out among the stars. He published this in *De nova stella* (1573). Comparing his measurements with those of the Spanish
astronomer Jerónimo Muñoz confirmed it. Today we know it was a **supernova**, a star that blew itself apart.

**The comet of 1577.** A great comet was visible from November 1577 to January 1578. Again Tycho found no parallax, so
it too was beyond the Moon, and he worked out that it passed among the planets, perhaps between Venus and Mercury. If
the planets were carried on solid crystal spheres, the comet would have had to smash through them. In Istanbul,
**Taqi al-Din** watched the same comet from his new observatory (chapter 8, section 8.10).

## 14.3 Uraniborg: an island for the stars { #hven }

In 1576 King **Frederick II** gave Tycho the island of **Hven** (now Ven), in the strait between Denmark and today's
Sweden, and the money to build **Uraniborg**, "castle of Urania", the first large observatory in Christian Europe. It
had a library, an alchemy laboratory and a printing workshop, and the overflow from its fish ponds drove a paper mill. When he found that the
instruments in the castle were not steady enough, Tycho built **Stjerneborg**, "castle of the stars", partly
underground, so that the most important instruments would be firm and out of the wind. At one point in the 1580s his support from the Crown came to about **1%**
of the kingdom's yearly income.

**How good was it?** Tycho had no telescope. With giant quadrants and sextants, careful checks and repeated readings,
he measured positions to about **one minute of arc**, around five times better than Wilhelm of Hesse, the best observer
of the time. His catalogue of **1,000 stars**, finished just before he left Denmark in 1597, placed the bright stars to about a
minute, though the fainter ones are less accurate. Even more valuable was his long, regular record of the planets.

**The other side.** Tycho ran Hven as its lord. He made the island's farmers grow twice as much as before and work
without pay on his buildings, and they took him to court over his taxes.

**At the same time, in Istanbul.** In 1577, the year after Uraniborg was begun, Taqi al-Din completed an observatory for
the Ottoman sultan. It was pulled down in 1580 (chapter 8, section 8.10). Uraniborg lasted about twenty years.

## 14.4 Tycho's system, and Mars's circle { #system }

Tycho admired Copernicus's mathematics but could not accept a moving Earth. A heavy Earth flying through space seemed
against all physics, Scripture said the Earth stood still, and if the Earth moved, the stars should show a yearly
parallax, which his instruments could not find. To explain that, the stars would have to be so far away that even a
faint star would be bigger than the Earth's whole orbit.

So in 1588 he published his own system. The Earth stands still at the centre; the Moon and the Sun go round it; and
the five planets go round the Sun. Mathematically it is the same as Copernicus's, but with the Earth held still.

**Mars decided the shape.** In Tycho's system, Mars at opposition comes nearer to the Earth than the Sun does, so Mars's
circle crosses the Sun's circle. That is impossible if the planets ride on solid spheres, which cannot pass through
each other; the comet of 1577 had already suggested the spheres were not real. Tycho reached this view in stages. In
1584 he said that Mars had shown little or no daily parallax at the opposition of 1582, so it must be farther than the
Sun, and that this ruled out Copernicus, who put Mars much closer. Later he changed his mind and drew the circles
crossing, though he seems never to have really measured Mars's parallax (section 14.10 shows why he could not).

**Who was first?** Similar Earth-centred systems with planets round the Sun were drawn by several people at about the
same time, and Tycho accused Nicolaus Reimers, called Ursus, of stealing his. A century earlier, Nilakantha in Kerala
had proposed something very like it (chapter 5, section 5.7). Many Jesuit astronomers supported Tycho's system, and
the Jesuits taught it in Beijing (chapter 4, section 4.11).

## 14.5 Prague, and a death that was not murder { #prague }

When Frederick II died, the new king, **Christian IV**, fell out with Tycho, and in 1597 Tycho left Denmark with his
instruments and his records. In 1599 the Emperor **Rudolf II** took him into his service in Prague, and he began a new
observatory at the castle of Benátky nad Jizerou, outside the city.

There, on **4 February 1600**, he met a young mathematician who had written to him: Johannes Kepler. Tycho needed
someone to turn his records into a theory; Kepler needed Tycho's records. Tycho guarded his data closely. The two
quarrelled, then made up and agreed on Kepler's pay and lodging.

In October 1601 Tycho fell ill after a banquet in Prague and died eleven days later, on **24 October 1601**, aged 54.
Stories grew up that he had been poisoned, and some writers even accused Kepler. In 2010 Czech and Danish scientists
opened his tomb in Prague, and in November 2012 they reported that there was not enough mercury or other poison to
support murder. Doctors today think a bladder or kidney illness is more likely.

## 14.6 Kepler: a teacher with weak eyes { #kepler }

Johannes Kepler was born on **27 December 1571** in Weil der Stadt, in south-west Germany. Smallpox in childhood left
him with weak eyes and damaged hands, so he was never a good observer himself, which made Tycho's data all the more
precious to him. He studied at **Tübingen** to become a Lutheran pastor, but his teacher **Michael Maestlin** taught him
Copernicus's system, and in 1594 he went instead to teach mathematics at the Protestant school in **Graz**, where he also
wrote yearly calendars of predictions.

In 1596 he published *Mysterium Cosmographicum*, "the cosmic mystery". He believed God had spaced the six planets'
spheres by fitting the five regular solids of geometry (cube, tetrahedron and so on) between them. The idea was wrong,
but the book showed he was a committed Copernican who wanted physical causes, not just good tables, and it led him to
Tycho.

Kepler lived through religious war. He would not fully accept the Lutheran statement of faith, insisted that
Calvinists were "brothers in Christ", argued that Catholics and Lutherans should be able to take communion together,
and was refused communion by his own church.

## 14.7 The orbit of Mars { #mars }

Tycho gave Kepler **Mars**, the planet whose motion no one could fit. It took him most of ten years. Mars was the right planet to be stuck with: of the planets then known, only Mercury's orbit is
more stretched, and Mercury is hard to see.

**First, the Earth.** To know where Mars is, you must know where you are looking from. Kepler found a clever way to
measure the Earth's own orbit using Mars. Mars returns to exactly the same point in space every **687 days**, its year,
while the Earth is somewhere else each time. Pick observations 687 days apart: Mars is in the same place, so the line
from the Sun to Mars is a fixed baseline, and the measured directions of the Sun and Mars from the Earth fix where the
Earth was. Doing this many times, Kepler mapped the Earth's orbit and found that the Earth, too, speeds up and slows
down.

**Eight minutes.** Kepler built a model of Mars with an off-centre circle and an equant, the "vicarious hypothesis".
It matched Tycho's positions of Mars at opposition to within two minutes of arc, but it got Mars's latitudes and
distances wrong. When he changed it to fix the distances, the positions at opposition were out by **8 minutes of
arc**. With older data nobody could have seen such an error: Copernicus's own Mars observations were out by as much
as 137 minutes (chapter 13, section 13.9). Tycho's data
were good to about one minute, so Kepler would not ignore it: "these eight minutes alone will lead us along a path to
the reform of the whole of Astronomy."

**Equal areas, then the ellipse.** By late 1602 Kepler had a rule for the speed: the line from the Sun to the planet
sweeps out **equal areas in equal times**, so the planet moves fastest when nearest the Sun. The shape took longer.
After about **40 failed attempts**, in late 1604 he found it: an **ellipse**, with the Sun at one focus. He had passed
over the ellipse at first, thinking it too simple for earlier astronomers to have missed.

He published the work in 1609 as *Astronomia nova*, "new astronomy", with the subtitle "commentaries on the motions of
the star Mars, from the observations of the noble Tycho Brahe". In it he also argued that a force from the Sun moved
the planets, perhaps something like magnetism, and that the Moon pulls on the oceans to make the tides.

## 14.8 Harmony, tables and a witch trial { #harmony }

**The third law.** Kepler kept looking for the pattern that linked all the planets. In 1618, the year his little
daughter Katharina died, he found it, and published
it in 1619 in *Harmonices Mundi*, "the harmony of the world", a book that also tried to hear the planets as music: the
square of a planet's year is proportional to the cube of its distance from the Sun (section 14.10). Nearly seventy years
later Newton would show why (chapter 16).

**The Rudolphine Tables.** Kepler finished the tables Tycho had proposed to the emperor in 1627, after years of delay
and war. They held Tycho's **1,005 stars** and used the new logarithms of John Napier. They were far better than anything
before. Using them, the French astronomer Pierre Gassendi saw Mercury cross the Sun on **7 November 1631**, the first transit of a
planet ever observed, and in 1639 Jeremiah Horrocks in England saw Venus do the same.

**A new star of his own.** In October 1604 another supernova appeared, in Ophiuchus. Kepler studied it for months. It
is the most recent supernova in our own galaxy that was certainly seen with the naked eye.

**Optics.** Kepler worked out that the lens of the eye throws an upside-down image onto the retina, and in 1611 he
designed an improved telescope with a convex eyepiece in place of Galileo's concave one, the basis of later
refracting telescopes (chapter 15).

**His mother's trial.** In 1615 his mother, **Katharina**, a healer who sold herbs, was accused of witchcraft. Kepler
spent years defending her. She was held in prison from 1620 to 1621, and was freed on 4 October 1621. Kepler had also
written a story, *Somnium* ("the dream"), in which a boy and his mother learn from a spirit how to travel to the Moon,
which it calls Levania, and what the Earth looks like from there. It may have been used against her. It was printed in
1634, after his death, and is often called one of the first works of science fiction.

Kepler died on **15 November 1630** in Regensburg.

## 14.9 Kepler's laws on Mars { #onmars }

All three laws are easy to see on Mars, which is one reason Kepler found them there.

**An ellipse with the Sun off-centre.** Mars's orbit is stretched only slightly: its narrowest width is 99.6% of its
widest. But the Sun sits 21 million km from the centre, so Mars's distance from the Sun swings from 206.7 million km
to 249.3 million km.

**Equal areas: fast and slow.** At its nearest Mars moves at 26.5 km/s, at its farthest only 22.0 km/s. So the Martian
seasons are very unequal. In the northern hemisphere spring lasts **194 sols** and autumn only **142 sols**; by the
second law, the long season must be the one spent on the far, slow part of the orbit. On Earth the longest season is less than five days longer than the
shortest.

**The third law from Mars.** A Martian Kepler would measure distances in Mars units and years in Mars years, and find
the same law for the Earth (section 14.10).

**Neighbours again.** On Mars, two craters named in 1973 lie about 260 km apart in the southern hemisphere: **Kepler**,
233 km across, and **Tycho Brahe**, 106 km across.

## 14.10 Working it out { #working }

**The third law.** With the distance $a$ in units of the Earth's distance and the period $P$ in Earth years,

$$P^2 = a^3.$$

For Mars, $a = 1.5237$ and $P = 686.98 / 365.256 = 1.8808$ years: $a^3 = 3.537$ and $P^2 = 3.537$.

**The same law, from Mars.** Now use Mars units: the Earth's distance is $1 / 1.5237 = 0.6563$ and its year is
$365.256 / 686.98 = 0.5317$ Mars years. Then $a^3 = 0.2827$ and $P^2 = 0.2827$. The law does not care which planet you
stand on.

**How stretched is an ellipse?** With eccentricity $e$, the shortest width compared with the longest is
$\sqrt{1 - e^2}$, and the Sun sits $ae$ from the centre. By the second law the speeds at nearest and farthest are in the
ratio of the distances the other way round.

| | Mars | Earth |
|---|---|---|
| Eccentricity $e$ | 0.0934 | 0.0167 |
| Narrowest width ÷ widest, $\sqrt{1-e^2}$ | 0.9956 | 0.9999 |
| Sun's distance from the centre, $ae$ | 21.3 million km | 2.5 million km |
| Nearest and farthest distance from the Sun | 206.7 and 249.3 million km | 147.1 and 152.1 million km |
| Fastest ÷ slowest speed | 26.50 ÷ 21.97 = 1.21 | 30.29 ÷ 29.29 = 1.03 |
| Longest and shortest season | 194 and 142 sols | 93.65 and 88.99 days |

The ellipse of Mars is only 0.44% narrower than a circle, far too little to see in a drawing. What gave it away was the
Sun's position off the centre, which makes Mars's speed change by about a fifth.

**Why Tycho could not see Mars's parallax.** Daily parallax is the shift in a planet's direction between two places
on the Earth, at most about one Earth radius apart. At an average opposition Mars is 78.34 million km away, so the
angle is

$$\frac{6{,}371}{78{,}340{,}000} \text{ radians} = 0.0000813 \text{ radians} \approx 17''.$$

Tycho's best measurements were good to about one minute, 60″. A 17″ shift was out of his reach. Seen from Mars, whose
radius is 3,389.5 km, the Earth's daily parallax at the same distance is only about 9″. The parallax of Mars was first
measured in the early 1670s, from Paris and from Cayenne in South America (chapter 16).

## 14.11 How should we judge Tycho and Kepler? { #assessment }

**Data and ideas.** Neither could have done it alone. Tycho's observations, five times better than anything before,
were useless without a theory, and Kepler's theories were useless without data that could tell them apart. The
eight minutes that Kepler refused to ignore were only visible because Tycho had measured to one.

**Getting it wrong on the way.** Tycho rejected the moving Earth for reasons that were sound on the evidence he had: no
parallax and no physics to explain how the Earth could move. Kepler's regular solids and music of the spheres were
wrong. Both were careful enough to be shown wrong by their own measurements, which is what made their work science.

**Not alone.** Taqi al-Din in Istanbul built an observatory in the same years and watched the same comet. In Japan,
Asada Gōryū is sometimes credited with finding the third law for himself in the 18th century (chapter 6, section
6.8). But Kepler's laws, built on Tycho's Mars, were the ones Newton would explain.

**A physics of the sky.** Kepler was one of the first to treat the planets as bodies pushed by a force from the Sun rather
than as points on circles. He guessed the wrong force. The right one, gravity, is in chapter 16.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) What did Tycho's measurements of the new star of 1572 show?

??? note "Answer"
    It had no daily parallax, so it was farther away than the Moon, among the stars. The heavens could change.

**2.** (beginner) In Tycho's system, what goes round what?

??? note "Answer"
    The Earth stands still. The Moon and the Sun go round the Earth, and the five planets go round the Sun.

**3.** (beginner) State Kepler's first two laws.

??? note "Answer"
    Each planet moves on an ellipse with the Sun at one focus. The line from the Sun to the planet sweeps out equal
    areas in equal times, so the planet moves fastest when nearest the Sun.

**4.** (intermediate) Why did Kepler's model with an off-centre circle fail, and why did that matter?

??? note "Answer"
    In parts of the orbit it was out by 8 minutes of arc. Tycho's data were good to about one minute, so the error was
    real, not a mistake in the observations. Kepler took it as a sign that the circle itself was wrong.

**5.** (intermediate) How did Kepler use Mars to find the Earth's orbit?

??? note "Answer"
    Mars returns to the same point every 687 days. In observations 687 days apart, the Sun-to-Mars line is the same,
    while the Earth is at different places. Measuring the directions of the Sun and Mars from the Earth at each time
    fixes the Earth's position, and many such pairs map its orbit.

**6.** (intermediate) Give the reasons Tycho had for rejecting a moving Earth. Which one was correct about the facts?

??? note "Answer"
    No physics could explain a moving Earth, Scripture spoke of the Earth standing still, and no yearly parallax of the
    stars could be seen. The last was a correct observation: the parallax exists but is far too small for his
    instruments. His conclusion from it was wrong.

**7.** (advanced) Jupiter is 5.203 times as far from the Sun as the Earth. Use the third law to find its year.

??? note "Answer"
    $P = a^{3/2} = 5.203^{1.5} = \sqrt{140.85} = 11.87$ years. (NASA gives 4,332.59 days, which is 11.86 years.)

**8.** (expert) In Mars units, Jupiter is $5.203 / 1.5237 = 3.415$ times as far from the Sun as Mars. How many Mars
years does Jupiter take to go round? Check your answer against Jupiter's year in days. Then explain why the northern
spring on Mars is so much longer than the northern autumn.

??? note "Answer"
    $P = 3.415^{1.5} = \sqrt{39.83} = 6.31$ Mars years, and $6.31 \times 686.98 = 4{,}335$ days, close to NASA's
    4,332.59. By the second law Mars moves slowest on the far part of its orbit, so a season spent there takes longer
    to cover its quarter of the path round the Sun. Northern spring must be that season (194 sols against 142).

## Summary

- Tycho Brahe (1546 to 1601) showed that the new star of 1572 and the comet of 1577 were beyond the Moon, so the
  heavens change and the planets are not carried on solid spheres.
- On Hven he built Uraniborg and Stjerneborg and measured stars and planets to about one minute of arc, without a
  telescope.
- His 1588 system kept the Earth still with the planets circling the Sun; Mars's circle crossing the Sun's was the key
  feature.
- He moved to Prague in 1599, met Kepler in 1600, and died in 1601; tests in 2012 found no sign of poisoning.
- Kepler (1571 to 1630) used Tycho's Mars data to map the Earth's orbit by the 687-day method, refused to accept an
  8-minute error, and found equal areas (1602) and the ellipse (1604), published in 1609.
- His third law came in 1619; the Rudolphine Tables of 1627 predicted the first observed transits.
- On Mars the laws show as a Sun 21 million km off-centre, a speed change of a fifth, and seasons of 194 and 142 sols.

**Next:** [chapter 15, Galileo, and the telescope goes round the world](15-Galileo-and-the-Telescope.md), where a lens maker's toy turned to the sky
showed moons round Jupiter and phases of Venus, and within a few decades telescopes reached China, India and Japan.
