# 6. Korea and Japan

*Korea and Japan learned their astronomy from China: the lunar mansions, the lunisolar calendar, the four animals of
the sky, and the idea that the court should watch the heavens. But they did not simply copy. Korea built a stone tower
for the stars in the 7th century, carved a new star chart for its own latitude in 1395, and under King Sejong made
water clocks, armillary spheres and a standard rain gauge two centuries before Europe had one. Japan painted star maps on tomb ceilings, kept
diaries that still help astronomers date supernovae, and in 1684 made its first calendar of its own. This chapter
also shows the arguments, beginning with whether the famous stone tower was an observatory at all.*

## 6.1 A shared sky { #shared }

Over many centuries, ideas, writing, Buddhism and statecraft flowed from China into the Korean kingdoms and on to
Japan, and astronomy came with them. The whole region came to share one way of seeing the sky:

- the **28 mansions** of the Moon and the four animals that guard the quarters of the sky: the Azure Dragon of the east,
  the White Tiger of the west, the Vermilion Bird of the south and the Black Tortoise of the north (chapter 4,
  section 4.4);
- a **lunisolar calendar** issued by the ruler, with leap months to keep it in step with the Sun;
- the **60-day and 60-year count**, which still names years in East Asia;
- and the belief that strange sights in the sky are messages to the ruler, so the court must keep watch and write them
  down.

In Japan, the principles of yin and yang and the five elements, and with them astronomy and calendar-making, came in the
5th and 6th centuries from China and from the Korean kingdom of **Baekje**, carried especially by a Korean monk named
**Gwalleuk**. Korea was often the bridge.

## 6.2 Cheomseongdae: a tower for the stars? { #cheomseongdae }

In Gyeongju, the old capital of the kingdom of **Silla**, stands a stone tower shaped like a bottle. Its name,
**Cheomseongdae**, means "star-gazing tower". It was built in the reign of **Queen Seondeok** (632–647) and has kept
its shape for nearly 1,400 years, though it now leans a little to the north-east. It is often called the oldest surviving
observatory in East Asia, and it is part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

The tower is 9.17 m high. Its curved body is made of 27 tiers of granite blocks, about 365 of them (one survey counted
366). A square opening sits at tiers 13 to 15, facing a little east of south; below it the tower is filled with earth and
rubble, above it the inside is hollow. The ends of stones stick out at the four corners of the top and on tiers 19 to 20
and 25 to 26 inside, where ladders could rest.

**Numbers in the stones.** People have long read meanings into the numbers: about 365 stones for the days of the year;
27 tiers, perhaps because Seondeok was the 27th ruler of Silla; 28 with the base, for the 28 mansions; 12 tiers above and
12 below the window, for the months and the 24 solar terms. Some of this may be intended, and some may be numbers
found because people looked for them.

**Was it an observatory?** Here the experts disagree.

- **Yes.** The name says so. An old record says men climbed up by entering through the middle, which fits an observer
  going in by the window and up a ladder to the platform on top. The astronomer Kim Bong-gyu points out that the early
  observatory of the next dynasty, Goryeo, was also called Cheomseongdae, that the Joseon observatory platform
  (*Gwancheondae*) was similar in size, and that Silla's records of sky events became about five times more numerous
  after the tower was built. Silla's sacrifices to the Sun, Moon and stars, he notes, were made at other places, so the
  tower was not their altar.
- **No, or not mainly.** The tower was first called an observatory in modern times by the Japanese meteorologist Wada
  Yuji in 1910. In the 1960s Yi Yong-beom argued that it was an altar modelled on Mount Sumeru, the mountain at the
  centre of the world in Buddhist cosmology, and Park Seong-rae accepted it as an observatory only in a broad sense,
  seeing it mainly as an altar to the god of farming. No instruments or records from the tower survive to settle it.

A fair view is that Cheomseongdae was built under a ruler who cared about the sky, by people who knew the calendar; that
it was probably used to watch the sky; and that we do not know exactly how.

## 6.3 A star map on a tomb ceiling { #kitora }

In the village of Asuka, south of Nara in Japan, is a small burial mound called **Kitora**. Inside is a stone chamber
just big enough for one coffin, built between the late 7th and early 8th century and found only in 1983. Its walls,
aligned with the compass, carry the four animal guardians and figures with the heads of the twelve zodiac animals. On
the ceiling, found in 1998, is a **circular star map**.

The map holds at least 350 stars in 74 or more constellations, with circles for the sky. It may be the oldest surviving
star chart of this kind, though much older pictures of the sky exist. A nearby tomb, **Takamatsuzuka**, excavated from 1972, has a simpler ceiling:
stars in gold leaf joined by red lines, with the pole-star group in the middle and the 28 mansions around it.

**When and where was it drawn?** Because the sky slowly shifts with precession, the positions on a star map carry the
date of the observations behind it. And a round star map centred on the pole carries the latitude where it was made,
because it shows which stars never set and which never rise (section 6.10). Two studies have measured the Kitora map:

- Measuring the 28 mansions, one team found that the observations date to about **80 BC ± 40 years**. The oldest
  Chinese star catalogue, that of Shi Shen (chapter 4, section 4.4), gives a date of 65 to 43 BC by the same method, so
  they concluded that the Kitora map was copied, at some remove, from a Chinese original based on Shi Shen's catalogue.
- Soma Mitsuru, studying the whole map, found an epoch of about **AD 300 ± 90 years** and a latitude of
  **33.9° ± 0.7°**.

The two dates do not agree, which shows how hard this work is. But both are centuries older than the tomb, and the
latitude fits the old Chinese capitals Chang'an and Luoyang. The painters in Asuka were copying a Chinese map that had
travelled, perhaps through Korea, for hundreds of years.

## 6.4 A star chart for Korea's own sky { #chart1395 }

The kings of the new Joseon dynasty (1392–1910) found a problem: the star maps they had came from China and did not
match the sky as seen from Korea. In December 1395 King Taejo had his astronomers carve a new chart on a slab of black
stone, the **Cheonsang Yeolcha Bunyajido**, "chart of the constellations and the regions they govern".

- The stone is about 211 cm high, 122.5 cm wide and 12 cm thick.
- It shows **1,467 stars** in **264 constellations**, with the ecliptic, the equator and a scale of 365 divisions round
  the edge.
- It was made by combining an older star map from the kingdom of **Goguryeo** with new observations.

A study of the star positions suggests that the stars more than 40° from the pole were placed for an epoch near the
**1st century AD**, while the stars near the pole match **1395**. If that is right, the Joseon astronomers kept an
ancient map and re-measured the part of the sky that changes most with latitude, the stars that never set. (Wikipedia
flags this analysis as the editors' own research, so treat it with care.)

The chart became the standard map of Joseon, copied and printed until Western star maps replaced it in the 19th
century. It is a national treasure of South Korea, and it is on the back of the 10,000-won banknote.

## 6.5 King Sejong's instruments { #sejong }

**Sejong** (1397–1450), the fourth king of Joseon, is best remembered for creating **Hangul**, the Korean alphabet. He
was just as busy with the sky. His reign gave Korea:

- an **armillary sphere** (*honcheonui*), made in 1433 by **Jang Yeong-sil** and turned by a water wheel;
- in 1434, the **Jagyeongnu**, Korea's first self-striking water clock, also by Jang, which marked the hours by itself
  with a bell, a gong and a drum, and kept the official time of Joseon;
- a royal observatory platform, the **Ganuidae**, at Gyeongbok Palace, in 1434 or 1438;
- in 1441, the **cheugugi**, a standard rain gauge, made under the crown prince by Yi Cheon and Jang Yeong-sil; Europe's
  first, Benedetto Castelli's, came in 1639;
- and the **Chiljeongsan**, "calculation of the seven governors" (the Sun, Moon and five planets), Korea's first
  astronomical treatise. It took the Chinese calendars and the Islamic tables used in China (the *Huihui li*) and
  adjusted them to the latitude and longitude of **Hanyang**, today's Seoul.

**Jang Yeong-sil** is a remarkable story in himself: he began as a government slave and rose, through his skill with
metal and machines, to be one of the king's chief engineers.

The Chiljeongsan shows what Korean astronomy was good at. It did not invent a new theory; it made the borrowed one work
for Korea. Section 6.10 shows why that mattered: Seoul's noon comes about 42 minutes before Beijing's.

## 6.6 An armillary clock and an argument over its date { #clock1669 }

In the Korea University Museum is an **armillary clock**, the *Honcheonsigye*: an armillary sphere about 40 cm across
with a globe of the Earth at its centre, driven by clockwork. It is National Treasure No. 230.

The usual story is that it was made in **1669** by **Song I-yeong**, a professor at the Joseon bureau of astronomy, for
King Hyeonjong, using European clock technology of the kind Christiaan Huygens introduced in 1657. The historian of science Jeon Sang-woon,
who examined it in 1962, identified it with Song's clock, and Joseph Needham accepted this and described the mechanism in
detail.

But the historian of Korean maps **Gary Ledyard** argued that it cannot be that early. The map on its little globe gives
a name to part of the southern continent that could not have been known in Korea in 1669. The clock may be a later
copy, or a later instrument.

It is a good example of how a date can be argued from small details: here, not from the stars but from the geography on
the globe.

## 6.7 Japan: the Way of Yin and Yang, and a poet's diary { #onmyodo }

Japan's government in the 7th and 8th centuries copied the Chinese model of offices, and one of them was the **Bureau of
Yin and Yang** (*Onmyōryō*). Its officials, the **onmyōji**, handled astronomy, the calendar, timekeeping and
divination. Over time these blended into **Onmyōdō**, the Way of Yin and Yang, a system that used astronomy and the
calendar to find lucky days, times and directions.

In the 10th century **Kamo no Tadayuki** and his son Kamo no Yasunori advanced both astronomy and the calendar. Their
pupil **Abe no Seimei** (921–1005) became the most famous onmyōji in Japanese history and a hero of later legends. From
the end of the Heian period the Abe and Kamo families between them controlled astronomy and the calendar at court, the
Abe the reading of the sky and the Kamo the calendar.

**A poet's diary.** **Fujiwara no Teika** (1162–1241), one of Japan's great poets, kept a diary, the *Meigetsuki*,
from 1180 until the year he died. In December 1230 a comet set him searching for records of earlier guest stars, and he
copied into his diary the reports of the new stars of 1006, **1054** (the one that made the Crab Nebula, chapter 4,
section 4.7) and 1181. He also recorded auroras. His record of 1054 puts the star a month earlier than the Chinese texts, which shows that even
good records need checking against each other. In 2019 the Astronomical Society of Japan named the diary a Japan
Astronomical Heritage.

**The guest star of 1181.** In August 1181 a new star appeared in Cassiopeia and stayed for 185 days. Chinese and
Japanese astronomers recorded it in eight separate texts. For decades astronomers linked it to a remnant called 3C 58,
though many doubted the match. Recently a small, strange nebula called **Pa 30**, about 800 years old, has been proposed
as the true remnant. East Asian records written eight centuries ago are still settling arguments in modern astrophysics.

## 6.8 Japan makes its own calendar { #calendars }

**Eight hundred years of a Chinese calendar.** In 859 Japan imported the Chinese **Xuanming** calendar, called
**Senmyō** in Japan, and began using it in 861 or 862. China dropped it in 893. Japan kept it for more than eight centuries, and
by the 1680s it was about **two days** out.

**Shibukawa Shunkai** (1639–1715), a professional go player, began in 1669 what were probably the first systematic
astronomical observations in Japan, measuring a gnomon's shadow around the winter solstice. In 1673 he proposed
replacing the Senmyō with the Chinese Shoushi calendar of Guo Shoujing (chapter 4, section 4.10), and compared the two
on the eclipses of 1673 to 1675. The test went against him: the solar eclipse of 1675 agreed better with the old
calendar. He improved his calculations, allowed for the difference in longitude between China and Japan, and proposed
his own calendar in 1683. In 1684 the board of astronomy chose a newer Chinese calendar instead, but he got the decision
reversed. His **Jōkyō calendar**, the first made in Japan, was issued in 1684 and came into use in 1685, and he was
appointed the shogunate's first official astronomer. He also made globes.

**Western astronomy through Dutch books.** In the Edo period Japan restricted contact with Europe, but books came in
through the Dutch trading post at Nagasaki, and a movement called **Rangaku**, "Dutch learning", grew up around them.

- **Asada Gōryū** (1734–1799), a doctor in Osaka, brought Western instruments and methods into his astronomy and is
  sometimes credited with finding Kepler's third law for himself.
- His pupil **Takahashi Yoshitoki** (1764–1804) used Western methods for the **Kansei calendar**, adopted in 1797. In
  1803 he got hold of a book by the French astronomer Jérôme Lalande and began translating it.
- **Shizuki Tadao**, a Nagasaki interpreter, translated a Dutch version of John Keill's book on Newton's physics in 1798,
  and coined Japanese words for "gravity" and "centrifugal force" that are still used.
- **Inō Tadataka** (1745–1818), a sake brewer and rice trader who retired at 49 and studied under Takahashi, surveyed the coasts of Japan from 1800 by
  counting his steps and taking star sights. His team finished the map in 1821, and it remained the standard for nearly
  a century.

In 1873 Japan switched to the Gregorian calendar.

## 6.9 To Mars and its moons { #mars }

East Asia's old sky had Mars as the Fire Star; its modern astronomy has sent machines there.

- Japan's **Nozomi**, launched on 4 July 1998, was meant to study the upper atmosphere of Mars. Solar flares damaged it
  in 2002 on the way, and it could not enter orbit; the mission ended on 31 December 2003.
- Japan's **Martian Moons eXploration** (MMX) was launched on 19 October 2026 on an H3 rocket. It is due to reach Mars
  orbit in August 2027, put a small rover on **Phobos**, collect at least 10 g of its surface, fly past Deimos, and bring
  the samples to Earth in 2031.

Phobos and Deimos are tiny, and, as section 6.10 shows, nothing like our Moon as clocks. A Martian civilisation would
not have built the calendars of this chapter.

At latitude $\varphi$ the pole stands $\varphi$ above the northern horizon. Stars closer to the pole than $\varphi$ never
set; stars further from the north pole than $180^\circ - \varphi$ never rise. The equator is always $90^\circ$ from the
pole. So on the map

$$\varphi = 90^\circ \times \frac{r_\text{inner}}{r_\text{equator}}.$$

Soma's 33.9° ± 0.7° covers a band from 33.2° to 34.6° north:

| Place | Latitude | Inside 33.2° to 34.6°? |
|---|---|---|
| Asuka (the Kitora tomb) | 34.45° N | yes |
| Chang'an (Xi'an) | 34.27° N | yes |
| Luoyang | 34.62° N | at the edge |
| Gyeongju | 35.83° N | no |
| Pyongyang (Goguryeo's capital) | 39.02° N | no |

So the latitude alone cannot tell Asuka from the Chinese capitals; it is the **date** that decides. Between 80 BC and AD
700 the equinox moved along the ecliptic by $779 \times 50.29'' \approx 10.9^\circ$, a shift easy to see on a map drawn
to a few degrees. Observations from long before Japan had astronomers point to a Chinese original. Goguryeo's capital, on
the other hand, is ruled out as the place of observation, though the map may still have passed through Korea.

**Why Seoul needed its own tables.** Seoul is at longitude 126.98° E, Beijing at 116.40° E. The Earth turns 1° in 4
minutes, so local noon in Seoul comes

$$(126.98 - 116.40) \times 4 \text{ min} \approx 42 \text{ minutes}$$

before noon in Beijing. An eclipse predicted for Beijing time would be 42 minutes off in Seoul unless the tables were
recomputed, which is what the Chiljeongsan did.

**How fast did the Senmyō calendar drift?** It divided the day into 8,400 parts and made the year 3,068,055 parts and
the month 248,057 parts:

$$\text{year} = \frac{3{,}068{,}055}{8{,}400} = 365.24464 \text{ days}, \qquad \text{month} = \frac{248{,}057}{8{,}400} = 29.530595 \text{ days}.$$

The true year is 365.2422 days, so the calendar year was 0.0024 days (3.5 minutes) too long. Over the 823 years Japan
used it,

$$823 \times 0.0024 \approx 2.0 \text{ days},$$

exactly the two days Shibukawa found. The month, by contrast, was only half a second longer than the true 29.530589
days. The calendar's Moon was almost perfect; its Sun had slipped.

**Moons as clocks: Mars and Earth.** East Asian calendars count months by the Moon. On Mars that would not work:

| | Mars | Earth |
|---|---|---|
| Main moon | Phobos | the Moon |
| Time to orbit once (against the stars) | 7 h 39 min | 27.32 days |
| Time between one moonrise and the next | about 11.1 hours (Phobos rises in the west) | about 24 h 50 min |
| Cycle of phases | about 7.7 hours | 29.53 days |
| Second moon | Deimos, 30.3 h; about 131 hours (5.3 sols) between risings | none |

Phobos goes round faster than Mars turns, so it rises in the west and sets in the east about twice a sol. A "month" of
Phobos would be shorter than a working day. For how the two moons move, see
[Two moons](https://physics.akadian.ca/topics/spacetime/level-1/06-two-moons.html).

## 6.11 Copying, adapting, recording { #assessment }

It is easy to treat Korea and Japan as footnotes to China. That misses what they did.

**They adapted.** The 1395 chart was made because Chinese maps did not fit Korea's sky. The Chiljeongsan recomputed the
best Chinese and Islamic tables for Seoul. Shibukawa replaced a calendar that had drifted for eight centuries, allowed
for Japan's longitude, and tested his ideas against eclipses even when the test went against him. Adapting a theory to a new place is real astronomy, and it is the same job that every
spacecraft team does when it moves Earth's clocks and maps to Mars.

**They built.** Sejong's reign produced self-striking water clocks, armillary spheres and the first standard rain
gauge, two centuries before Europe's.

**They recorded.** Korean and Japanese records of guest stars, comets and auroras stand beside China's, and when they
disagree, as Teika's month for 1054 does, the disagreement itself is useful. The remnant of 1181 shows those records still
at work.

**They argued.** Cheomseongdae, the Kitora map and the armillary clock all have disputed dates or purposes. In each case
the argument is made from evidence: stones and records, star positions, place names on a globe. That is how history of
astronomy should be done.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Name two things East Asia shared from Chinese astronomy.

??? note "Answer"
    Any two of: the 28 mansions of the Moon; the four animals of the sky's quarters; the lunisolar calendar issued by the
    ruler; the 60-day and 60-year count; the court's duty to watch and record the sky.

**2.** (beginner) What is Cheomseongdae, and why do scholars argue about it?

??? note "Answer"
    A 7th-century stone tower in Gyeongju, built under Queen Seondeok of Silla, whose name means "star-gazing tower".
    Some see it as an observatory (its name, the way in through the window, more sky records after it was built); others
    as an altar, modelled on Mount Sumeru or made for a god of farming. No instruments or records from it survive.

**3.** (beginner) Why did King Taejo order a new star chart in 1395?

??? note "Answer"
    The star maps from China did not match the sky as seen from Korea's latitude, so he had a new chart carved, combining
    an old Goguryeo map with new observations.

**4.** (intermediate) What did the Jagyeongnu do that an ordinary water clock did not?

??? note "Answer"
    It struck the hours by itself, with a bell, a gong and a drum, so no one had to watch the water level and announce
    the time.

**5.** (intermediate) Why can the latitude of the Kitora map not decide between Asuka and the Chinese capitals?

??? note "Answer"
    Asuka (34.45° N), Chang'an (34.27° N) and Luoyang (34.62° N) all lie within the measured 33.9° ± 0.7°. The date of
    the observations, centuries before Japan had astronomers, is what points to a Chinese original.

**6.** (intermediate) Seoul is at 126.98° E and Beijing at 116.40° E. How much earlier is local noon in Seoul?

??? note "Answer"
    $10.58^\circ \times 4$ minutes per degree $\approx 42$ minutes.

**7.** (advanced) On a round star map centred on the pole, the circle of stars that never set has a radius of 30 mm and
the equator a radius of 75 mm. At what latitude was it made?

??? note "Answer"
    $\varphi = 90^\circ \times 30 / 75 = 36^\circ$ north.

**8.** (expert) Phobos orbits Mars in 7.654 hours; Mars turns once in 24.623 hours (both against the stars). Show that
Phobos rises about every 11.1 hours, and explain why it rises in the west.

??? note "Answer"
    Seen from the turning surface, Phobos moves round the sky at the difference of the two rates:
    $1/7.654 - 1/24.623 = 0.1307 - 0.0406 = 0.0900$ turns an hour, so one lap takes $1 / 0.0900 \approx 11.1$ hours.
    Because Phobos goes round faster than the ground turns beneath it, it overtakes the sky's daily motion and moves
    from west to east across the sky, so it rises in the west.

## Summary

- Korea and Japan took China's mansions, four animals, lunisolar calendar and court astronomy, often through Korea, as
  with the monk Gwalleuk of Baekje.
- Cheomseongdae (632–647), Silla's 9.17 m stone tower, is called an observatory; some scholars see an altar. It was
  probably used for watching the sky, but we do not know how.
- The Kitora tomb's ceiling map (late 7th or early 8th century) copies observations from about 80 BC or AD 300 at a
  latitude near 34° N: a Chinese original that had travelled for centuries.
- Korea's 1395 stone chart shows 1,467 stars, re-made for Korea's latitude from an older Goguryeo map.
- Sejong's reign brought armillary spheres, Jang Yeong-sil's self-striking water clock (1434), the rain gauge (1441)
  and the Chiljeongsan, computed for Seoul.
- The armillary clock credited to Song I-yeong (1669) may be later, judging from the map on its globe.
- Japan's onmyōji ran astronomy and the calendar; Teika's diary copies the reports of the guest stars of 1006, 1054 and
  1181; the star of 1181 may have left the nebula Pa 30.
- Japan used a Chinese calendar for over 800 years, slipping 2 days because its year was 3.5 minutes too long, until
  Shibukawa's Jōkyō calendar (1684–1685); then it learned Western astronomy through Dutch books.
- Japan's Nozomi failed to reach Mars orbit; MMX, launched in October 2026, is on its way to Phobos.
