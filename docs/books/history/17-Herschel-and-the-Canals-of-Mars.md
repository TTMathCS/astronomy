# 17. Herschel, and the canals of Mars

*In 1781 a church organist in Bath, England, found a planet that nobody had known was there. Sixty-five years later
another was found by mathematics before anyone looked. Meanwhile Mars, seen better and better, turned out to have polar
caps that grow and shrink with the seasons, a day only a little longer than ours, and, in 1877, two tiny moons. In the
same year an Italian astronomer drew fine lines on Mars and called them "channels". In English they became "canals",
and for the next thirty years the world argued about whether someone had dug them.*

## 17.1 A musician finds a planet { #herschel }

William Herschel was born in 1738 in Hanover, in Germany, and followed his father, an oboist, into the military band. At 19 he moved to
Britain, and by 1766 he was organist at the Octagon Chapel in **Bath** and Director of Public Concerts in the city. He wrote 24
symphonies. Reading a book on musical harmony led him to the same author's book on optics, and he began to build
telescopes, grinding the metal mirrors himself for up to 16 hours a day.

On **13 March 1781**, sweeping the sky from his garden in Bath, he noticed an object in Gemini that was not a point like
a star. He reported it as a comet. But the Finnish-Swedish astronomer Anders Lexell, working in Russia, was the first
to compute its orbit, which turned out almost circular and far beyond Saturn: it was a **planet**, the first found since ancient times.
Herschel wanted to call it the "Georgian star" after King George III. In 1782 Johann Bode in Berlin suggested
**Uranus**, the Greek god of the sky and the father of Saturn, and that name won in the end. It had been seen before,
by John Flamsteed in 1690, who listed it as a star.

The king made Herschel "The King's Astronomer" in 1782, and that year he and Caroline moved to Datchet, near Windsor.
His largest telescope, 12 m (40 feet) long with a mirror about 1.2 to 1.26 m across (sources give 48 and 49½
inches), was the largest scientific instrument built up to then. He found two moons of Uranus and two of Saturn, published catalogues of 2,500 and later 5,000 nebulae, and in 1800 found that sunlight carries
heat beyond the red end of the rainbow, the light we now call **infrared** (chapter 18).

## 17.2 Caroline Herschel { #caroline }

**Caroline Herschel**, William's younger sister, was born in Hanover in 1750. Typhus at the age of ten stunted her
growth, and her mother thought she should train as a house servant rather than be educated. In Bath she ran
William's house, became the main singer at his concerts, and had lessons from him in English, music, mathematics and
astronomy. Soon she was polishing mirrors, writing down his observations through
the night, and sweeping the sky herself.

Between 1786 and 1797 she discovered **eight comets**, the first on 1 August 1786 while William was away. In 1787 King
George III gave her a salary of £50 a year as William's assistant, which made her the first woman to receive a salary as a
scientist. In 1798 she gave the Royal Society an index to Flamsteed's star catalogue with 560 stars he had left out.
In 1828 she became the first woman to receive the Gold Medal of the Royal Astronomical Society. She died in Hanover in
1848, aged 97.

## 17.3 Herschel's Mars { #herschelmars }

From 1777 Herschel watched Mars, especially its white polar caps. By 1784 the southern cap had shrunk a great deal, so
the caps change with the seasons, as ice and snow do on Earth. In 1781 he measured the turning of Mars as 24 hours
39 minutes 21.67 seconds, and the tilt of its axis as 28.5°. So Mars has a day only a little longer than ours, and
seasons for the same reason as ours.

Herschel wrote that Mars had "a considerable but moderate atmosphere, so that its inhabitants probably enjoy a
situation in many respects similar to ours". That idea, with no evidence behind it, would grow much bigger a century later.

## 17.4 A rule for the gaps, and two new worlds { #rule }

**The Titius-Bode rule.** In 1766 Johann Titius, and in 1772 Johann Bode, published a simple rule for the planets'
distances: take 0, 3, 6, 12, 24, 48, 96, add 4 to each and divide by 10. That gives 0.4, 0.7, 1.0, **1.6**, 2.8, 5.2 and
10, close to the real distances of Mercury, Venus, the Earth, **Mars**, (nothing), Jupiter and Saturn, in units of the
Earth's distance. Uranus, at 19.2, fitted the next number, 19.6, so people took the rule seriously and looked for the
missing planet at 2.8.

**Ceres.** On **1 January 1801** Giuseppe Piazzi at Palermo in Sicily saw a faint "star" move. It was lost behind the
Sun before enough was known, but the young German mathematician Carl Friedrich Gauss invented a new way to work out an
orbit from a few observations, and it was found again. **Ceres** goes round the Sun at 2.77, just where the rule said.
It was too small for a planet, and soon others were found nearby: the first of the asteroids, in the belt between Mars
and Jupiter.

**Neptune, found with a pen.** By the 1840s Uranus was drifting away from the positions Newton's law predicted. In 1845
**Urbain Le Verrier** in Paris and **John Couch Adams** in Cambridge each set out to find the unseen planet that must be
pulling it. Le Verrier sent his prediction to the Berlin Observatory, and on the night of **23 to 24 September 1846**
Johann Galle and Heinrich d'Arrest found the planet within one degree of where he said. A French scientist said Le
Verrier had found a planet "with the point of his pen". The British then announced that Adams had predicted it too,
which started an angry argument; some historians doubt the evidence for Adams's early claim. Galileo had seen Neptune
in 1612 and 1613 and taken it for a star.

**The rule fails.** Neptune is at 30.1, not 38.8. Today most astronomers think the Titius-Bode rule is a coincidence,
and one planetary science journal no longer accepts papers trying to improve it.

## 17.5 Two moons in a week { #moons }

In August 1877 Mars came unusually close. At the US Naval Observatory in Washington, **Asaph Hall** searched for moons
with the observatory's 66 cm telescope, then the largest refracting telescope in the world. He thought the chance of
finding a moon was slight, and was ready to give up. His wife, the mathematician and campaigner for women's votes **Angeline Stickney**, urged him to keep going. He
later wrote that he "might have abandoned the search had it not been for the encouragement of my wife". He found
**Deimos** on **12 August** and **Phobos** on **18 August 1877**. Stickney helped with the calculations, but when she
asked to be paid what a man would be, Hall refused, and she stopped. The largest crater on Phobos is named
**Stickney**.

The names, Fear (Phobos) and Fright (Deimos), come from Book 15 of Homer's *Iliad*, where the war god Ares calls on
them. They were suggested by Henry Madan, science master at Eton in England.

**Strange moons.** Phobos goes round Mars in 7 hours 39 minutes, faster than Mars turns, so from the ground it rises in
the **west** and crosses the sky in a few hours, about every 11 hours. Deimos, a little slower than Mars turns, creeps
from east to west and takes about 2.7 days to set.

**Guessed in a story.** In *Gulliver's Travels* (1726), Jonathan Swift wrote that the astronomers of Laputa had found
two moons of Mars, going round in 10 and 21.5 hours. Voltaire mentioned two moons too, in 1752. They were guessing,
perhaps following Kepler, who had unscrambled one of Galileo's coded messages, really about Saturn, as "Hello, furious
twins, sons of Mars". Two craters
on Deimos are named Swift and Voltaire.

## 17.6 Schiaparelli's channels { #canali }

**The first maps.** From 1830 the German astronomers Wilhelm Beer and Johann Mädler watched Mars with a small 95 mm
telescope, and in 1840 drew the first map of it, labelling the markings with letters. In 1858 the Jesuit Angelo Secchi
in Rome called one dark feature the "Atlantic Channel" (*Canale Atlantico*), the patch we call
Syrtis Major. The English writer Richard Proctor drew a map from William Dawes's drawings of 1864, naming its features after
astronomers.

**1877.** During the same close approach in which Hall found the moons, **Giovanni Schiaparelli**, of the Brera
Observatory in Milan, mapped Mars with a 22 cm telescope. He drew a network of long, thin, dark lines joining the dark
areas, and called them ***canali***, the ordinary Italian word for channels or grooves. Over the next years he saw some
of them double. He also gave Mars's features names from the history, myths and geography of the ancient
Mediterranean, such as Syrtis Major, Elysium and Utopia. Those names are still used on Mars today.

In English, *canali* was translated as **canals**, a word that means something dug by engineers. That one word did more
than any observation to start the idea of Martians.

## 17.7 Lowell's canals { #lowell }

**Percival Lowell** (1855 to 1916) came from a rich Boston family. Before astronomy he spent years in East Asia: he
lived in Korea in 1883 to 1884 after serving as counsellor to a Korean diplomatic mission to the United States, and
wrote books about Korea and Japan, such as *Chosön: The Land of the Morning Calm* (1886) and *The Soul of the Far
East* (1888).

Fired by Schiaparelli's maps, he built his own observatory at **Flagstaff, Arizona**, in 1894, more than 2,100 m up
with few cloudy nights. It was the first observatory deliberately put in a remote, high place for steady air. He drew
more and more canals, and in three books, *Mars* (1895), *Mars and Its Canals* (1906) and
*Mars as the Abode of Life* (1908), he set out a story: Mars was a dry, dying world, and its people kept themselves
alive with a vast network of irrigation canals. The dark areas that spread with the seasons were, in this story,
plants growing. The public loved it,
and it filled novels and newspapers for decades.

## 17.8 How the canals faded { #illusion }

**An experiment with schoolboys.** In 1903 the British astronomer Edward Maunder and Joseph Evans asked schoolboys
to copy circular discs marked with faint details. Their tests showed how the eye, straining at the limit of what it
can see, can turn scattered markings into lines that are not there: canals as an optical illusion.

**A naturalist does the sums.** In 1907 **Alfred Russel Wallace**, the naturalist who had found evolution by natural
selection at the same time as Darwin, wrote a short book, *Is Mars Habitable?* He showed that Mars must be much colder
than Lowell thought, and that its air was too thin for water to stay liquid on the surface. His verdict: Mars "is not only uninhabited by intelligent beings such as Mr. Lowell postulates, but is absolutely
uninhabitable".

**A bigger telescope.** In 1909 **Eugène Antoniadi**, a Greek-French astronomer born in Constantinople (Istanbul),
looked at Mars through the 83 cm refractor at Meudon near Paris. At first he had believed in the canals. Now, with a
much larger lens, he saw none, and decided they were an optical illusion. Photographs taken the same year at the new
dome of the Pic du Midi observatory in France also counted against them.

**The end.** The argument went on until 1965, when the American spacecraft **Mariner 4** flew past Mars and sent back
the first close pictures: craters, and no canals (chapter 11). The only real feature that may match a "canal" is the
great canyon Valles Marineris. The seasonal darkening was dust being blown around, not plants.

**Lowell's legacy.** His observatory went on searching for a planet beyond Neptune that he had predicted. In 1930
**Clyde Tombaugh**, a self-taught young man from a Kansas farm, found Pluto there.

## 17.9 A worldwide argument { #world }

The new planets and the canals of Mars drew in astronomers from many countries.

- **Russia.** Anders Lexell, a Finn working in St Petersburg, first showed that Uranus was a planet. Later, at Pulkovo
  near St Petersburg, **Gavriil Tikhov** was one of the first to use colour filters to bring out the markings on planets.
  Later he led "astrobotany" at Alma-Ata (now Almaty) in Kazakhstan, asking whether life could exist on other worlds
  of the Solar System.
- **Peru.** Harvard College Observatory set up a southern station at **Arequipa**, 2,400 m up in the Andes, in 1890.
  There, in 1892, **William Pickering** saw small, round black spots where the canals met, and said the air
  was so steady that it was as good as doubling the size of his telescope. The station moved to Bloemfontein in **South Africa** in 1927.
- **The Andes again.** In 1907 Lowell sent the American astronomer David Todd on an expedition to the Andes to
  observe Mars.
- **Italy and France.** Piazzi worked in Sicily, Schiaparelli in Milan, Secchi in Rome. Antoniadi, born in the Ottoman
  capital, made his name in France, where Camille Flammarion had first brought him.

## 17.10 Working it out { #working }

**The rule against the real distances.** The rule is $a = 0.4 + 0.3 \times 2^n$ with $n = -\infty, 0, 1, 2, \ldots$

| | Rule | Real | Error |
|---|---|---|---|
| Mars ($n = 2$) | 1.6 | 1.524 | 5% too far |
| Earth ($n = 1$) | 1.0 | 1.000 | none |
| Ceres ($n = 3$) | 2.8 | 2.77 | 1% |
| Uranus ($n = 6$) | 19.6 | 19.19 | 2% |
| Neptune ($n = 7$) | 38.8 | 30.07 | 29% |

**What could a telescope see on Mars?** At its very closest, Mars is 25.6″ across and 6,779 km wide, so one second of
arc on Mars is $6{,}779 / 25.6 = 265$ km. A telescope with a lens $D$ mm across can at best separate details
$116 / D$ seconds of arc apart. But even on the best nights, the moving air above most observatories blurs the view to
what a telescope only 10 to 20 cm wide could do in space, about 0.6″ to 1.2″, or 150 to 300 km on Mars.

| Telescope | Lens | Best possible | On Mars |
|---|---|---|---|
| Schiaparelli, 1877 | 22 cm | 0.53″ | 140 km |
| Lowell, Flagstaff | 61 cm | 0.19″ | 50 km |
| Antoniadi, Meudon, 1909 | 83 cm | 0.14″ | 37 km |
| Most nights, any size | | about 1″ | about 265 km |

Lines of spots far apart, near the limit, are exactly what the eye joins up.

**Moons that rise backwards.** A moon that goes round in $P$ hours on a planet that turns in $T$ hours passes overhead
every $1 / |1/P - 1/T|$ hours.

| | Mars | Earth |
|---|---|---|
| Planet turns once (to the stars) | 24.6229 h | 23.9345 h |
| Nearest moon goes round | Phobos, 7.6538 h | the Moon, 655.7 h |
| Nearest moon passes overhead every | $1 / (1/7.6538 - 1/24.6229) = 11.1$ h, moving west to east | $1 / (1/23.9345 - 1/655.7) = 24.8$ h, moving east to west |
| Orbit that keeps pace with the turning | 20,428 km from the centre | 42,164 km from the centre |
| Tilt of the axis | 25.19° (Herschel: 28.5°) | 23.44° |

The orbit that keeps pace comes from Kepler's third law: $r = \sqrt[3]{GM T^2 / 4\pi^2}$, with
$GM = 42{,}828$ km³/s² for Mars and $T = 88{,}642$ s, giving 20,428 km. Phobos is inside it, so it outruns the
turning; Deimos, at 23,459 km, is outside.

## 17.11 How should we judge the canal story? { #assessment }

**Good observers, wrong answer.** Schiaparelli and Lowell were skilled and honest. They saw what they reported. The
mistake lay in trusting the eye at the very edge of what it can see, and in reading a story into the result. Antoniadi
changed his mind when a bigger telescope showed him more, which is how science should work.

**The power of a word.** *Canali* meant channels. "Canals" meant engineers. A careful translation might have saved
thirty years of argument, though the wish to find life on Mars was strong enough that something like Lowell's story
might have grown anyway.

**Real discoveries.** In the same years astronomers found a new planet by chance (Uranus) and another by calculation
(Neptune), the first asteroid, the two moons of Mars, the seasonal polar caps and Mars's day and tilt, all of which
have stood. Women such as Caroline Herschel and Angeline Stickney did much of the work. Caroline in the end was paid and
honoured; Stickney was refused even the pay.

**The question did not go away.** Whether Mars has ever had life is still one of the biggest questions in science. It
is now asked with spacecraft and rovers, not with the eye at a telescope (chapters 21 and 22).

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) What did William Herschel find on 13 March 1781, and what did he first think it was?

??? note "Answer"
    The planet Uranus. He first reported it as a comet.

**2.** (beginner) Why was Caroline Herschel's salary from the king important?

??? note "Answer"
    It made her the first woman to receive a salary as a scientist.

**3.** (beginner) Who found the moons of Mars, when, and who encouraged him to keep searching?

??? note "Answer"
    Asaph Hall, in August 1877 (Deimos on the 12th, Phobos on the 18th), encouraged by his wife Angeline Stickney.

**4.** (intermediate) What did Herschel learn about Mars's polar caps, and what did it tell him?

??? note "Answer"
    By 1784 the southern cap had shrunk a lot, so the caps change with the seasons. That suggested they are made of ice
    and that Mars has seasons like the Earth's.

**5.** (intermediate) How was Neptune found, and why was there an argument afterwards?

??? note "Answer"
    Uranus was drifting from its predicted path. Le Verrier worked out where an unseen planet pulling it must be, and
    Galle and d'Arrest found it in Berlin on 23 to 24 September 1846, within one degree. The British then said Adams had
    predicted it too, and there was a dispute over who deserved the credit.

**6.** (intermediate) Give two pieces of evidence, from before spacecraft, that the canals were not real.

??? note "Answer"
    Maunder and Evans's 1903 experiment with schoolboys showed how the eye can turn faint scattered markings into lines
    that are not there. Antoniadi in 1909, with the large Meudon telescope, saw no canals at
    all. (Wallace's 1907 sums also showed Mars too cold, with air too thin, for liquid water.)

**7.** (advanced) Using the rule $0.4 + 0.3 \times 2^n$, what distance does it give for $n = 8$? Is there a planet there?

??? note "Answer"
    $0.4 + 0.3 \times 256 = 77.2$ times the Earth's distance. No planet is there. Pluto, found in 1930, goes round at
    about 39.5, which is near the rule's number for Neptune, not this one.

**8.** (expert) Deimos goes round in 30.2986 hours and Mars turns in 24.6229 hours. How long does Deimos take to come
back to the same place in a Martian sky, and roughly how long does it stay up? If a moon went round exactly at
20,428 km, what would it look like from the ground?

??? note "Answer"
    $1 / (1/24.6229 - 1/30.2986) = 1 / (0.040613 - 0.033005) = 1 / 0.007608 = 131.4$ hours, about 5.5 days. It is
    above the horizon for roughly half of that, about 2.7 days. A moon at 20,428 km would go round in exactly the time
    Mars turns, so it would hang still in the sky, never rising or setting.

## Summary

- William Herschel, an organist in Bath, found Uranus in 1781; his sister Caroline found eight comets and became the
  first woman paid a salary as a scientist.
- Herschel showed that Mars's polar caps change with the seasons and measured its day (24 h 39 m) and tilt (28.5°).
- The Titius-Bode rule fitted Uranus and led to Ceres in 1801, but failed for Neptune, which Le Verrier found by its
  pull on Uranus in 1846.
- Asaph Hall found Deimos and Phobos in August 1877, urged on by Angeline Stickney. Phobos goes round faster than Mars
  turns and rises in the west.
- Schiaparelli's *canali* (channels) of 1877 became "canals" in English; Lowell built a story of a dying Martian
  civilisation on them.
- Experiments, Wallace's sums and Antoniadi's views through a large telescope showed the canals were an illusion;
  Mariner 4 confirmed it in 1965.
- The argument spread from Italy and the USA to Russia, Peru and France.
