# 18. Light split into colours

*In the 1830s a French philosopher wrote that we would never know what the stars are made of. Within thirty years
astronomers were reading it from their light. Spread starlight out into its colours and it is crossed by fine dark
lines, and each chemical element leaves its own pattern. This chapter follows that idea from a glass globe of water in
Persia to a mountain observatory in India, a room of women at Harvard sorting stars by hand, and the thin, dry air of
Mars.*

## 18.1 Colours in white light { #prism }

**A water globe in Persia.** Around 1300 the Persian scholar **Kamal al-Din al-Farisi**, a pupil of the astronomer
Qutb al-Din al-Shirazi, worked out how a rainbow forms: sunlight is bent as it enters a raindrop, reflected inside it,
and bent again as it leaves. He tested the idea with a clear glass sphere filled with water, inside a darkened room
with a small hole to let the light in. At about the same time, Theodoric of Freiberg in Germany reached a similar
answer. The two never met, but both built on the *Book of Optics* of Ibn al-Haytham, who had worked in Cairo three
centuries earlier (chapter 8).

**Newton's prism.** In the 1660s Isaac Newton passed a beam of sunlight through a glass prism and showed that white
light is a mixture of colours, each bent by its own amount. He named seven: red, orange, yellow, green, blue, indigo and
violet. His book *Opticks* (1704) set this out.

## 18.2 Beyond the red and the violet { #invisible }

In 1800 **William Herschel** (chapter 17) spread sunlight out with a prism and put thermometers in the colours. The
temperature rose towards the red, and went on rising **beyond** the red, where there was no light to see. He had found
**infrared**. More than half of the Sun's energy reaches the Earth in this form.

The next year, in Jena in Germany, **Johann Ritter** looked at the other end. Silver chloride, a chemical that darkens
in light, turned black fastest just past the violet, where again nothing could be seen. That was **ultraviolet**.

Everything warm glows. The hotter it is, the shorter the wavelength where it glows most. The Sun glows most in visible
light; the Earth and Mars, far colder, glow in Herschel's infrared.

## 18.3 Dark lines in sunlight { #lines }

In 1802 the English chemist **William Wollaston** noticed a few dark gaps in the spectrum of the Sun, twelve years
before anyone looked closely at them.

**Joseph von Fraunhofer** was orphaned at 11 and apprenticed to a harsh glassmaker. In 1801 the workshop
collapsed on him. The rescue was led by the ruler of Bavaria himself, who then gave the boy books and made his master
let him study. Fraunhofer went on to make optical glass, lenses and telescopes. In 1814, testing his very pure prisms,
he found that sunlight is crossed by hundreds of dark lines. He mapped 574 of them and labelled the strongest with
letters from A to K. He also invented the spectroscope and made early diffraction gratings. He died in 1826, aged 39.

## 18.4 Fingerprints of the elements { #elements }

**"Never."** In his *Course in Positive Philosophy* (1830 to 1842) the French philosopher Auguste Comte wrote that
people would never learn what the stars are made of. It has been called one of the worst predictions in science.

**Kirchhoff and Bunsen.** In Heidelberg in the 1850s the physicist **Gustav Kirchhoff** and the chemist
**Robert Bunsen**, working together from 1854, heated elements in a flame and looked at their light through a spectroscope. Each element gave its own
set of bright lines, always at the same places, like a fingerprint. Several of these lines sat exactly on dark
Fraunhofer lines in sunlight. In 1859 Kirchhoff showed what this meant: a cooler gas in front of a hot source takes
out the same colours that it would give out when hot. The Sun's dark D lines are made by **sodium** in the Sun's
outer layers. Using the same method, Kirchhoff and Bunsen soon found two new elements on Earth, caesium and rubidium.

So the dark lines are a list of what the Sun is made of. Within thirty years of Comte's "never", astronomers were
reading the make-up of the Sun and stars.

## 18.5 An eclipse in India and Siam, and a new element { #helium }

On **18 August 1868** the Moon's shadow crossed the Earth from Ethiopia and Yemen, over India, Siam (Thailand),
Malaysia and Indonesia to New Guinea. At its longest the Sun was hidden for 6 minutes 47 seconds.

**A king's eclipse.** King **Mongkut** of Siam had predicted the eclipse himself, and his figures were judged more
precise than those of the French astronomers of his day. He watched it with his party from Hua Hin, on
the Gulf of Thailand. There he caught malaria, and he died on 1 October 1868.

**A yellow line.** At **Guntur** in south-east India, the French astronomer **Jules Janssen** pointed a spectroscope at
the red flames, the prominences, around the hidden Sun. He saw bright lines, which showed the prominences are hot gas.
One was a bright yellow line at 587.5 nm, close to sodium's D lines but not the same. Several others saw it too,
among them Norman Pogson, the government astronomer at Madras. The line was so bright that Janssen found he could see
the prominences the next day, with no eclipse at all.

In England **Norman Lockyer** saw the same line from home, without an eclipse. His report and Janssen's were
presented to the French Academy of Sciences on the same day, 26 October 1868, and the two usually share the credit. No known element gave that line, so
Lockyer named a new one after the Greek for the Sun, *helios*: **helium**. It was found on Earth only in 1895, by the
Scottish chemist William Ramsay, in a uranium mineral. Helium is now known to be the second most common element in the
universe, after hydrogen.

## 18.6 Sorting the stars { #stars }

**Secchi's classes.** Angelo Secchi, the Jesuit who ran the observatory of the Roman College for 28 years (and who
named a "channel" on Mars, chapter 17), collected about 4,000 star spectra from 1863 and found they fall into a
few types. His five classes were the first system for sorting stars by their light.

**The Hugginses.** In London **William Huggins** built an observatory at his home at Tulse Hill, where he later worked
with his wife **Margaret**. In 1864 he found that one nebula, NGC 6543, shows only bright lines, like a hot gas. Others,
such as the Orion Nebula, were gas too, while the Andromeda Nebula showed a spectrum like a star's (chapter 19).

**The Harvard computers.** At Harvard College Observatory, the director Edward Pickering hired women to measure and
sort a growing pile of photographic plates of the sky. They were paid 25 to 50 cents an hour, more than factory
workers but less than office clerks.

- **Williamina Fleming**, from Dundee in Scotland, had been abandoned by her husband and was working as a maid in
  Pickering's house. She came to lead the group and hired more than 40 women. In 1890 they published the first
  Henry Draper Catalogue, with more than 10,000 stars sorted by spectrum. She also found the Horsehead Nebula.
- **Annie Jump Cannon**, nearly deaf after scarlet fever, could sort three stars a minute by eye. She put the classes
  in the order still used today, **O, B, A, F, G, K, M**, and in her life classified about 350,000 stars. In 1925 she
  became the first woman to receive an honorary doctorate of science from Oxford.
- Much of the southern sky came from **Arequipa** in Peru, where Harvard had built its southern station in 1890
  (chapter 17). Cannon and Pickering published the bright southern stars in 1901, and the full Henry Draper
  Catalogue of 225,300 stars came out between 1918 and 1924.

## 18.7 Meghnad Saha and what stars are made of { #saha }

Cannon's classes were an order, but nobody knew what the order meant. Why did some stars show strong hydrogen lines and
others strong metal lines?

**Meghnad Saha** was born in 1893 in a village near Dhaka, in Bengal (now Bangladesh), the son of a poor shopkeeper
from a low-caste family. As a schoolboy he was forced out of Dhaka Collegiate School for joining the Swadeshi movement
against British rule. Among his classmates in Calcutta was Satyendra Nath Bose. In **1920** Saha worked out an
equation that tells how many atoms of a gas lose electrons at a given temperature and pressure. An atom that has lost
electrons gives different lines. So the pattern of lines in a star depends mostly on its **temperature**, and Cannon's
O B A F G K M is a sequence from hottest to coolest. The astrophysicist Jayant Narlikar called the work "in the Nobel
Prize class". Saha later founded the Institute of Nuclear Physics in Calcutta and was elected to India's parliament in
1951.

**Cecilia Payne.** Born in England, Payne studied at Cambridge but, as a woman, could not be given a degree there.
At Harvard in 1925 she used Saha's equation on the Harvard spectra and found that the stars are made mostly of
**hydrogen and helium**, not of the same mix as the Earth. The leading astronomer Henry Norris Russell called the
result "spurious", and she added a line saying it was "almost certainly not real". Four years later Russell reached the same answer by another method.
Decades on, Otto Struve called her work "the most brilliant PhD thesis ever written in astronomy".

## 18.8 Lines that move { #doppler }

In 1842, in Prague, the Austrian physicist **Christian Doppler** argued that a wave from a moving source arrives
squeezed if the source is coming towards you and stretched if it is going away. (He hoped this would explain the
colours of double stars. It does not.) For light, the shift is tiny, but spectral lines make it measurable: a line
moved towards the red means the star is moving away; towards the blue, coming closer.

In **1868** Huggins found the lines of Sirius slightly shifted to the red, and saw that this could tell how fast
the star was moving away.
At the Lick Observatory in California, **William Campbell** later built up a catalogue of the speeds of stars measured this way.
In 1909 at Kodaikanal in India (section 18.9), John Evershed used the same shifts to find gas streaming across
sunspots.

## 18.10 What spectra told us about Mars { #mars }

**Clouds and colour.** In 1784 William Herschel saw bright patches move on Mars and took them for clouds. In 1809
Honoré Flaugergues in France saw "yellow clouds", which were probably dust storms. In 1864 William Dawes noticed that
Mars is reddest in the middle of its disc, where we look through the least of its air, so the red is the ground, not
the air.

**Is there water?** Spectroscopes pointed at Mars in the 1860s and 1870s led many astronomers to think its air was
much like the Earth's. The trouble is that the Earth's own air adds water lines to every spectrum.
In **1894**, at the Lick Observatory, **William Campbell** compared Mars with the Moon, which has no air, and found
that in many ways they looked the same: Mars had far less air than many had thought. This was just when Lowell
was writing about canals full of water (chapter 17).

**Measuring the air.** In 1926 Donald Menzel used William Wright's photographs of Mars taken at Lick to find the first
real numbers for its air. **Gerard Kuiper**, a Dutch-born astronomer in the United States, found **carbon
dioxide** in it. In 1964 a team led by Lewis Kaplan, with Hyron Spinrad, found faint **water vapour** lines and showed that
the air of Mars is very much thinner than the Earth's. A year later Mariner 4 flew past and confirmed it: mostly carbon
dioxide, with a pressure of 400 to 700 pascals at the ground.

**Today.** Mars's air is 95% carbon dioxide, at less than 1% of the Earth's pressure, with only about 210 parts per
million of water vapour. Rovers now carry spectrometers to Mars itself: a laser zaps a rock and the rover reads the
light of the spark to find what the rock is made of.

## 18.11 Working it out { #working }

**Where things glow most.** Wien's law gives the wavelength of strongest glow: $\lambda_{\text{peak}} = 2898 / T$
micrometres, with $T$ in kelvin.

**Lines that move.** For speeds much less than light's, a line of wavelength $\lambda$ shifts by
$\Delta\lambda = \lambda \, v / c$, with $c = 299{,}792$ km/s. The edges of a turning planet move towards and away from
us at the equator speed $v = 2\pi R / T$.

| | Mars | Earth |
|---|---|---|
| Average temperature | 214 K | 288 K |
| Strongest glow, $2898 / T$ | 13.5 micrometres | 10.1 micrometres |
| Carbon dioxide in the air | 95.1% | 0.042% |
| Pressure at the ground | 6.36 mb | 1014 mb |
| Equator speed, $2\pi R / T$ | $2\pi \times 3396.2 / 88{,}642 = 0.241$ km/s | $2\pi \times 6378.1 / 86{,}164 = 0.465$ km/s |
| Shift of the red hydrogen line (656.3 nm) at the edge | 0.00053 nm | 0.00102 nm |

The Sun, at 5,772 K, glows most at $2898 / 5772 = 0.502$ micrometres, or 502 nm, in the middle of the visible
colours.

**Telling Mars's water from ours.** If Earth and Mars move in circles, the speed at which the distance between them
changes reaches about 13.9 km/s. Then a water vapour line near 820 nm in Mars's light moves by
$820 \times 13.9 / 299{,}792 = 0.038$ nm away from the same line made by the Earth's own air, a gap a good
spectrograph can measure. When Mars is neither coming nor going, its lines and ours sit on top of each other.

**Balmer's rule for hydrogen.** In 1885 Johann Balmer found that hydrogen's visible lines fit
$\lambda = 364.507 \times n^2 / (n^2 - 4)$ nm, for $n = 3, 4, 5, 6$.

| $n$ | Rule | Measured | Fraunhofer's letter |
|---|---|---|---|
| 3 | 656.1 nm | 656.3 nm | C |
| 4 | 486.0 nm | 486.1 nm | F |
| 5 | 433.9 nm | 434.0 nm | G′ |
| 6 | 410.1 nm | 410.2 nm | h |

## 18.12 How should we judge this story? { #assessment }

**A new kind of astronomy.** Before spectra, astronomers could measure where things are and how they move. After
spectra, they could ask what things are made of, how hot they are, and how fast they move towards or away from us.
That is the start of **astrophysics**.

**Many hands.** The story runs from Persia and Cairo through Munich and Heidelberg to Guntur, Hua Hin, Kodaikanal,
Arequipa and Calcutta. Much of the hardest work was done by people the scientific world kept at the edge: an orphan
apprentice, a maid from Dundee, a nearly deaf woman paid by the hour, a shopkeeper's son from Bengal, and a woman denied a
degree.

**Being wrong in public.** Comte was sure we could never know. Russell was sure Payne was wrong. In the end the evidence
was in the light, and it won. On Mars, the hopeful early spectra were wrong too, and Campbell's
careful comparison with the Moon was right.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) What did Newton show with a prism?

??? note "Answer"
    That white light is a mixture of colours, each bent by a different amount.

**2.** (beginner) What did Herschel find beyond the red end of the spectrum in 1800, and how?

??? note "Answer"
    Infrared, invisible light that warmed a thermometer placed past the red.

**3.** (beginner) Which element was first found in the Sun, and how did it get its name?

??? note "Answer"
    Helium, from a yellow line seen at the eclipse of 1868. Lockyer named it after *helios*, the Greek for the Sun.

**4.** (intermediate) Why do the dark lines in sunlight tell us what the Sun is made of?

??? note "Answer"
    Each element absorbs and gives out light only at its own set of wavelengths. Cooler gas in the Sun's outer layers
    takes out exactly those colours, so the pattern of dark lines matches the elements present, such as sodium for
    the D lines.

**5.** (intermediate) What did Meghnad Saha's equation explain about Cannon's classes O, B, A, F, G, K, M?

??? note "Answer"
    That they are mainly a sequence of temperature, from hottest (O) to coolest (M), because temperature decides how
    many atoms have lost electrons, and that changes which lines appear.

**6.** (intermediate) Why was it hard to tell from the ground whether Mars's air has water vapour, and how did
Campbell tackle it in 1894?

??? note "Answer"
    The Earth's own air adds water lines to every spectrum. Campbell compared Mars with the Moon, which has no air, so
    any extra lines in Mars's spectrum would have to be from Mars. He found the two looked much the same.

**7.** (advanced) Deimos goes round Mars at about 1.35 km/s. By how much would its motion shift the red hydrogen line
at 656.3 nm, at most, as seen from Mars's centre?

??? note "Answer"
    $\Delta\lambda = 656.3 \times 1.35 / 299{,}792 = 0.0030$ nm. (Deimos's speed: $2\pi \times 23{,}459$ km in
    30.30 hours, or $147{,}400 / 109{,}070 = 1.35$ km/s.)

**8.** (expert) Using Balmer's rule, show that hydrogen's lines crowd together towards a limit, and find it. What
happens to the wavelengths for very large $n$?

??? note "Answer"
    $n^2 / (n^2 - 4) = 1 / (1 - 4/n^2)$, which falls towards 1 as $n$ grows. So the lines get closer and closer
    together, and their wavelengths approach $364.5$ nm, in the ultraviolet. For $n = 10$ the rule gives
    $364.507 \times 100 / 96 = 379.7$ nm; for $n = 20$, $364.507 \times 400 / 396 = 368.2$ nm.

## Summary

- Al-Farisi in Persia and Newton in England showed that white light is a mixture of colours.
- Herschel found infrared in 1800 and Ritter ultraviolet in 1801.
- Fraunhofer mapped 574 dark lines in sunlight in 1814; Kirchhoff and Bunsen showed in 1859 that they are the
  fingerprints of elements.
- At the eclipse of 1868, seen from India and predicted by King Mongkut of Siam, a yellow line revealed helium.
- The women computers at Harvard sorted hundreds of thousands of stars, using plates from Peru; Cannon's O B A F G K M
  is still used.
- Saha's equation of 1920 showed that the classes are a temperature sequence, and Payne found that stars are mostly
  hydrogen and helium.
- Doppler shifts measure speed towards or away from us.
- Spectra showed that Mars's air is thin, mostly carbon dioxide, with very little water.
