# Skywatchers of the world, a history of astronomy

*A book in 22 chapters, from notched bones and stone circles to the telescopes of today. Every people on Earth
watched the sky, and most of them wrote down, built or sang what they saw. This book gives each of them its due:
Babylon and Egypt, China, India, Korea and Japan, Greece, Baghdad and Samarkand, Timbuktu, the Maya and the Inca,
the navigators of the Pacific and the oldest astronomers of all, in Australia.*

## How to read it

- **In order, or by region.** The chapters run roughly by time, but each region's chapter stands on its own.
- **Every level.** Each chapter starts with the story, for any reader. Later sections show the evidence (what was
  found, how it was dated, the numbers behind it), and the last ones give the arguments that experts still have.
- **Where the evidence is thin, the chapter says so.** Some famous claims are well supported, some are guesses, and
  some have been shown to be wrong. Each one is marked.
- **The science is in the other books.** When a chapter needs an idea such as a lunar month or a solstice, it explains
  it briefly and links to the physics lessons for the full story.

## Timeline

The turning points, in date order, with the part of the world each one belongs to and a link to the page that
tells the story.

| Year | Region | Event | Why it mattered |
|---|---|---|---|
| c. 43,000–42,000 years ago | Africa | A baboon bone at [Border Cave](01-Before-Writing.md#bones), South Africa, is notched in four sets | Notation this old is rare; it is often called a Moon count, which the notches do not show |
| c. 25,000–16,000 years ago | Africa | The [Ishango bone](01-Before-Writing.md#bones), Congo, is notched in three columns | Arithmetic, a calendar or counting in twelves: still unproven |
| c. 8000 BC | Europe | Hunter-gatherers dig twelve pits at [Warren Field](01-Before-Writing.md#warren), Scotland, facing the midwinter sunrise | Possibly the oldest calendar, a Moon count reset each midwinter |
| c. 4500–4000 BC | Africa | Herders at [Nabta Playa](01-Before-Writing.md#nabta), Egypt, set up a stone circle with a solstice sightline | The Sun watched beside a seasonal lake in the Sahara; star claims unproven |
| c. 4000 BC (disputed) | China | A tomb at [Xishuipo](04-China.md#xishuipo) is laid out with a shell dragon, tiger and perhaps the Dipper | A possible sky picture 3,500 years before the same layout appears on the Zeng chest |
| c. 3200 BC | Europe | [Newgrange](01-Before-Writing.md#solstice-buildings), Ireland, is built with a roof-box for the midwinter sunrise | A feature with no use but to let in the solstice Sun |
| c. 3000 BC | Africa | Egypt keeps records of the Nile flood; the [civil year of 365 days](03-Egypt.md#year) is in use | Twelve months of 30 days plus five: the simplest calendar a state ever used |
| c. 3000–2500 BC | Europe | [Stonehenge](01-Before-Writing.md#solstice-buildings) is laid out on the solstice line | The best-known solar alignment, rebuilt over 1,500 years |
| c. 2500 BC | Africa | The Great Pyramid is set [square to north](03-Egypt.md#pyramids) within about 3.6 minutes of arc | Precise surveying by the stars or the Sun; a disputed way to date the pharaohs |
| c. 2400 BC | Africa | The [Namoratunga pillars](10-Africa.md#namoratunga) are raised by Lake Turkana | Once called an observatory; the alignments are now doubted |
| c. 2100 BC | China | A platform with twelve slots for the sunrise is built at [Taosi](01-Before-Writing.md#taosi) | A horizon calendar marking both solstices |
| c. 2100–1800 BC | Africa | [Diagonal star tables](03-Egypt.md#decans) of 36 decans are painted on coffin lids | Star clocks; a likely origin of the twelve hours of the night |
| c. 1646 BC (disputed) | Middle East | Venus's appearances are recorded for 21 years under King Ammi-saduqa: the [Venus tablet](02-Mesopotamia.md#venus) | The oldest known long run of planet sightings; its date is still argued over |
| buried c. 1600 BC (disputed) | Europe | The [Nebra sky disc](01-Before-Writing.md#nebra) is buried on the Mittelberg, Germany | The oldest known picture of the sky: Moon, Pleiades and the Sun's 82° swing |
| c. 1600–500 BC | Pacific | [Lapita](12-Oceania-and-Australia.md#ocean) voyagers sail through Melanesia to Fiji, Tonga and Samoa | The start of the settlement of the remote Pacific |
| c. 1500–1400 BC | Africa | The oldest known [sundial and water clock](03-Egypt.md#clocks), from the Valley of the Kings and Karnak | Hours by day and by night, of lengths that change with the season |
| c. 1479–1458 BC | Africa | Senenmut's tomb at Thebes gets the oldest known [star ceiling](03-Egypt.md#ceilings) | The sky of north and south painted in full |
| c. 1250–1200 BC | China | [Oracle bones](04-China.md#oracle) record eclipses of the Moon by the 60-day count | The 60-day count has run unbroken since; the eclipses help date King Wu Ding |
| compiled c. 1000 BC | Middle East | [MUL.APIN](02-Mesopotamia.md#mulapin) gathers star lists, first risings, planet rules and calendar rules | The oldest known handbook of the sky |
| 7th century BC | Middle East | Scholars send [sky omen reports](02-Mesopotamia.md#omens) to the kings of Assyria | Sky-watching as a state service; the oldest datable aurora records |
| 652–61 BC | Middle East | Babylon keeps the [Astronomical Diaries](02-Mesopotamia.md#diaries), nightly notes for six centuries | The largest body of observations from the ancient world |
| 28 May 585 BC (disputed) | Mediterranean | Herodotus says [Thales](07-Greece-and-Alexandria.md#ionians) predicted the eclipse that stopped a battle | If true, the first predicted eclipse; modern scholars doubt it |
| c. 500–100 BC (disputed) | India | The [Vedanga Jyotisha](05-India.md#vedanga) sets a five-year calendar of 1,830 days | The first Indian astronomy text; the solstice it describes fits c. 1400–1200 BC |
| 497–380 BC | Middle East | The [19-year cycle](02-Mesopotamia.md#nineteen) of 235 months becomes the rule for leap months | Months and years kept in step; the Jewish calendar still uses it |
| late 5th century BC | Middle East | Eclipse records are arranged by the 223-month [saros](02-Mesopotamia.md#saros) | Lunar eclipses predicted from their own cycle |
| c. 433 BC | China | A chest in the [tomb of Marquis Yi of Zeng](04-China.md#mansions) names all 28 lunar mansions | The oldest full list of the mansions, with the Dipper, dragon and tiger |
| 432 BC | Mediterranean | [Meton](07-Greece-and-Alexandria.md#spheres) brings the 19-year cycle into the Athenian calendar | 19 years = 235 months, as in Babylon |
| 409–398 BC | Middle East | The [zodiac](02-Mesopotamia.md#zodiac) of twelve equal 30° signs is introduced; the first horoscopes follow | The first coordinate system for the sky |
| 350–50 BC | Middle East | Jupiter's motion is computed as the [area of a trapezoid](02-Mesopotamia.md#jupiter) | Distance as the area under a speed-time graph, 1,400 years before Europe |
| c. 330 BC | Mediterranean | [Aristotle](07-Greece-and-Alexandria.md#round) argues the Earth is a sphere from eclipse shadows and travelling stars | The round Earth becomes common knowledge |
| 3rd century BC | Mediterranean | [Aristarchus](07-Greece-and-Alexandria.md#aristarchus) puts the Sun 18 to 20 times farther than the Moon, and proposes that the Earth goes round the Sun | A sound method; the true ratio is about 390 |
| 250–200 BC | Americas | Thirteen towers are built at [Chankillo](01-Before-Writing.md#chankillo), Peru | A complete solar calendar on the horizon, accurate to a day or two |
| c. 240 BC | Mediterranean | [Eratosthenes](07-Greece-and-Alexandria.md#eratosthenes) measures the Earth from shadows at Syene and Alexandria | 250,000 stadia; its accuracy depends on the unknown stadion |
| 240 BC | China | A comet in the Records of the Grand Historian is [Halley's](04-China.md#guests) | The oldest sure sighting of Halley's comet |
| 238 BC | Africa | The [Canopus Decree](03-Egypt.md#leap) orders a leap day every four years; it lapses | The first official year of 365¼ days, two centuries before Caesar |
| sealed 168 BC | China | A silk book at [Mawangdui](04-China.md#guests) draws 29 comets | An atlas of comet shapes, each with its omen |
| 147–127 BC | Mediterranean | [Hipparchus](07-Greece-and-Alexandria.md#hipparchus) on Rhodes finds precession and makes a star catalogue | Builds on Babylonian eclipse records; founder of trigonometry |
| 104 BC | China | The [Taichu calendar](04-China.md#calendar) makes 19 years exactly 235 months | The Han lunisolar calendar, later joined by the 24 solar terms |
| sunk c. 70–60 BC | Mediterranean | The [Antikythera mechanism](07-Greece-and-Alexandria.md#antikythera) goes down with a ship | A geared model of the Sun, Moon, calendars and eclipses |
| c. 50 BC | Africa | The [Dendera zodiac](03-Egypt.md#dendera) is carved | Babylon's zodiac and Egypt's decans on one ceiling |
| 36 BC (uncertain) | Americas | [Stela 2](11-The-Americas.md#count) at Chiapa de Corzo carries one of the oldest Long Count dates | A count of days in base 20, with a zero |
| 7 BC | China | A report of [Mars in the Heart](04-China.md#mars) forces the prime minister to his death | Modern calculation shows Mars was nowhere near; the omen was probably invented |
| AD 79/80 | Middle East | The last dated [cuneiform tablet](02-Mesopotamia.md#clay), an astronomical almanac from Uruk | Writing on clay ends, as it went on, with astronomy |
| AD 139 | Africa | [Sirius rises](03-Egypt.md#sirius) on the Egyptian New Year, as Censorinus records | Fixes the Sothic cycle used to date Egyptian history |
| c. AD 150 | Mediterranean | [Ptolemy](07-Greece-and-Alexandria.md#ptolemy) writes the Almagest in Alexandria | Epicycles and the equant; used for 1,400 years |
| AD 185 | China | A guest star appears in the [Southern Gate](04-China.md#guests) | Probably the earliest supernova on record |
| AD 415 | Mediterranean | [Hypatia](07-Greece-and-Alexandria.md#wider), astronomer and teacher, is murdered in Alexandria | The Library was already gone; the link is a modern myth |
| AD 499 | India | [Aryabhata](05-India.md#aryabhata) writes the Aryabhatiya, aged 23 | The Earth turns on its axis; eclipses by shadows; the oldest sine table |
| AD 525 | Europe | Dionysius Exiguus extends the [Easter table](09-Byzantium-and-Medieval-Europe.md#computus) and counts years from the birth of Christ | The start of AD dating |
| AD 628 | India | [Brahmagupta](05-India.md#brahmagupta) finishes the Brahmasphutasiddhanta | Rules for zero and negative numbers; translated into Arabic in Baghdad |
| AD 632–647 | Korea | [Cheomseongdae](06-Korea-and-Japan.md#cheomseongdae), a 9.17 m stone tower, is built in Gyeongju under Queen Seondeok | Called the oldest surviving observatory in East Asia; some scholars see an altar |
| AD 649–684 | China | The [Dunhuang star atlas](04-China.md#mansions) is drawn | The oldest complete star atlas from any civilisation: 1,339 stars |
| c. AD 700 | Japan | The [Kitora tomb](06-Korea-and-Japan.md#kitora) ceiling is painted with a star map of at least 350 stars | Its stars were observed centuries earlier, near latitude 34° N: a Chinese original |
| AD 718 | India | Gautama Siddha brings [Indian numerals with zero](05-India.md#exchange) to the Tang court | Indian astronomy in China |
| AD 725 | China | Yi Xing builds an [escapement](04-China.md#machines) and surveys shadows from 17° N to 50° N | The key part of later clocks; the survey shows shadows do not grow evenly with distance |
| AD 725 | Europe | Bede writes [The Reckoning of Time](09-Byzantium-and-Medieval-Europe.md#bede) at Jarrow | The round Earth, daylight and the tides, for working out Easter |
| early 770s | India | An Indian treatise reaches Baghdad and becomes the [Zij al-Sindhind](05-India.md#exchange) | Indian methods enter Arabic astronomy |
| AD 818 | Americas | The Dresden Codex [Mars table](11-The-Americas.md#dresden) is set at a backward loop of Mars | A 780-day cycle, three times 260 |
| c. 830 | Middle East | Al-Ma'mun's astronomers [measure a degree](08-The-Islamic-World-and-Persia.md#measuring) on the plain of Sinjar | 56⅔ Arabic miles per degree; the mile's length is uncertain |
| 877–918 | Middle East | [Al-Battani](08-The-Islamic-World-and-Persia.md#measuring) at Raqqa measures the year, the Earth's tilt and the moving solar apogee | Cited 23 times by Copernicus |
| c. AD 900–1200 (debated) | Pacific | Polynesians reach [Hawaiʻi, Rapa Nui and Aotearoa](12-Oceania-and-Australia.md#ocean) | Islands found by steering with the stars |
| 964 | Middle East | Al-Sufi's [Book of Fixed Stars](08-The-Islamic-World-and-Persia.md#stars) records the Andromeda galaxy as a small cloud | The first known record of another galaxy |
| 1006 | Africa | Ali ibn Ridwan in Cairo describes the [new star](08-The-Islamic-World-and-Persia.md#guests) as 2½ to 3 times as large as Venus | The same star was recorded in China, Japan, Iraq and Europe |
| 1006 | Europe | Monks at [St Gallen](09-Byzantium-and-Medieval-Europe.md#skies) see the new star low in the south | The most northerly sighting of the supernova |
| 1017–1037 | Central Asia | [Al-Biruni](08-The-Islamic-World-and-Persia.md#biruni) measures the Earth from a hilltop at Nandana and writes his India | One observer, one place, and a right triangle |
| 1021–1154 | Americas | The [Maya Codex of Mexico](11-The-Americas.md#dresden), a Venus almanac, is made | The oldest surviving book of the Americas |
| 1025–1028 | Africa | Ibn al-Haytham in Cairo writes [Doubts concerning Ptolemy](08-The-Islamic-World-and-Persia.md#doubts) | The equant cannot be a true picture of the heavens |
| 1054 | China | A [guest star](04-China.md#guests) is seen by day for 23 days | It left the Crab Nebula and its pulsar |
| 1054 | Middle East | Ibn Butlan in Baghdad writes of [a spectacular star in Gemini](08-The-Islamic-World-and-Persia.md#guests) | The only known Arabic report of the star of 1054 |
| 1054 (disputed) | Americas | A painting at Chaco Canyon may show the [guest star](11-The-Americas.md#north) | A lovely idea that cannot be proved |
| 1066 | Europe | Halley's Comet appears; the [Bayeux Tapestry](09-Byzantium-and-Medieval-Europe.md#skies) shows it | A comet remembered with a conquest |
| 1088–1094 | China | [Su Song](04-China.md#machines) builds his clock tower at Kaifeng | A water-driven sphere and globe with an escapement and chain drive |
| 1150 | India | Bhaskara II, head of the [Ujjain observatory](05-India.md#brahmagupta), writes the Siddhanta Shiromani | Ujjain, the meridian of Indian astronomy |
| c. 1175 | Europe | Gerard of Cremona translates the [Almagest](09-Byzantium-and-Medieval-Europe.md#translations) from Arabic in Toledo | Ptolemy reaches Latin through Arabic |
| 1181 | Japan | A [guest star](06-Korea-and-Japan.md#onmyodo) in Cassiopeia stays for 185 days | Its remnant may be the nebula Pa 30 |
| 1230 | Japan | Fujiwara no Teika copies [guest star reports](06-Korea-and-Japan.md#onmyodo) of 1006, 1054 and 1181 into his diary | His 1054 date differs from China's by a month |
| 1252 | Europe | The [Alfonsine Tables](09-Byzantium-and-Medieval-Europe.md#translations) are compiled in Toledo | Europe's most popular tables for three centuries |
| 1259 | Middle East | Al-Tusi begins the [Maragha observatory](08-The-Islamic-World-and-Persia.md#maragha) for Hulagu | A Chinese astronomer on the staff; the Tusi couple replaces the equant |
| 1276–1281 | China | Guo Shoujing builds the [Gaocheng gnomon](04-China.md#guo) and the Shoushi calendar | A year of 365.2425 days, 300 years before the Gregorian calendar |
| 1277 | Europe | The Bishop of Paris condemns [219 teachings](09-Byzantium-and-Medieval-Europe.md#universities) | Historians still argue whether it freed science from Aristotle |
| 1304–1375 | Middle East | [Ibn al-Shatir](08-The-Islamic-World-and-Persia.md#shatir), timekeeper in Damascus, builds models without the equant | Mathematically the same as Copernicus's for the Moon and Mercury |
| 1327 | Europe | Richard of Wallingford designs the [St Albans clock](09-Byzantium-and-Medieval-Europe.md#clocks) | One of Europe's first astronomical clocks |
| c. 1340–1425 | India | [Madhava](05-India.md#kerala) of Kerala finds infinite series for the sine and π | Power series some 250 years before Europe |
| 1377 | Europe | Oresme weighs a [turning Earth](09-Byzantium-and-Medieval-Europe.md#universities) and decides against it | No argument for a fixed Earth is conclusive |
| 1395 | Korea | King Taejo has a [star chart](06-Korea-and-Japan.md#chart1395) of 1,467 stars carved in stone | Made because Chinese maps did not fit Korea's sky |
| 1410 | Europe | The [Prague astronomical clock](09-Byzantium-and-Medieval-Europe.md#clocks) is first recorded | Still running in the Old Town Square |
| 1420s–1449 | Central Asia | Ulugh Beg's [Samarkand observatory](08-The-Islamic-World-and-Persia.md#samarkand) makes a new catalogue of 1,018 stars | The year to within a minute; al-Kashi's π to 16 places |
| 1433–1441 | Korea | Under King Sejong, [Jang Yeong-sil](06-Korea-and-Japan.md#sejong) builds an armillary sphere and a self-striking water clock; a standard rain gauge follows | The Chiljeongsan adjusts Chinese and Islamic tables to Seoul |
| 1472 | Europe | Regiomontanus prints Peuerbach's [planet lectures](09-Byzantium-and-Medieval-Europe.md#printing) in Nuremberg | The first printed astronomy textbook |
| 1500–1501 | India | Nilakantha's [Tantrasangraha](05-India.md#kerala) has five planets circling the Sun | A model like Tycho's, 80 years earlier |
| 1562 | Americas | Diego de Landa burns Maya books at [Maní](11-The-Americas.md#peoples) | Only four Maya books survive |
| 1577–1580 | Middle East | Taqi al-Din's [Istanbul observatory](08-The-Islamic-World-and-Persia.md#samarkand) is built and pulled down | Built at almost the same time as Tycho's |
| 1582 | Europe | Thursday 4 October is followed by Friday 15 October: the [Gregorian calendar](09-Byzantium-and-Medieval-Europe.md#gregorian) | Ten days dropped; 97 leap years in 400 |
| 1583 | Africa | Mahmud Kati in [Timbuktu](10-Africa.md#timbuktu) records a meteor shower | Stars flew 'as if fire had been kindled in the whole sky' |
| 1614 | Americas | Claude d'Abbeville records [Tupinambá](11-The-Americas.md#plains) star names and tide lore | The Moon linked to the tides before Galileo |
| 1669 (disputed) | Korea | Song I-yeong is credited with an [armillary clock](06-Korea-and-Japan.md#clock1669) driven by clockwork | The map on its globe suggests it may be later |
| 1673 | China | Verbiest re-equips the [Beijing observatory](04-China.md#jesuits) | European instruments and methods in the Qing bureau |
| 1684–1685 | Japan | Shibukawa Shunkai's [Jōkyō calendar](06-Korea-and-Japan.md#calendars) replaces the Senmyō, used for over 800 years | The old year was 3.5 minutes too long and had slipped 2 days |
| 1724–1734 | India | Jai Singh builds the [Jantar Mantar](05-India.md#jantar) observatories | A stone sundial 27 m high at Jaipur |
| 1751–1752 | Africa | Lacaille maps nearly 10,000 [southern stars](10-Africa.md#telescopes) from the Cape | Fourteen new constellations still in use |
| 1769 | Pacific | Cook times the [transit of Venus](12-Oceania-and-Australia.md#tupaia) at Tahiti; Tupaia draws his chart | A measure of the distance to the Sun, and Pacific knowledge on paper |
| 1800–1821 | Japan | Inō Tadataka and his team [survey Japan's coasts](06-Korea-and-Japan.md#calendars) by pacing and star sights | Western astronomy learned through Dutch books |
| 1820 | Africa | The [Royal Observatory](10-Africa.md#telescopes) at the Cape of Good Hope is founded | The first scientific institution in Africa |
| 1840s | Australia | The Boorong see the outburst of [Eta Carinae](12-Oceania-and-Australia.md#aboriginal) | A changing star kept in oral tradition |
| 1880 | Americas | Förstemann publishes the [Dresden Codex](11-The-Americas.md#dresden) and decodes its numbers | Maya astronomy can be read again |
| 1957 | India | India adopts a [national calendar](05-India.md#calendars) from Saha's committee | A modern solar calendar beside the traditional ones |
| 1961 | Australia | The [Parkes](12-Oceania-and-Australia.md#telescopes) dish is finished; later named Murriyang | Apollo 11 pictures, quasars, fast radio bursts |
| 1965 | Americas | [Mariner 4](11-The-Americas.md#mars) sends the first close pictures of Mars | Craters and thin air, not canals |
| 1976 | Pacific | [Hōkūleʻa](12-Oceania-and-Australia.md#hokulea) sails from Hawaiʻi to Tahiti with no instruments | Planned voyaging shown to work |
| 2000 | Americas | Satellite data confirm the Andean [Pleiades forecast](11-The-Americas.md#pleiades) | Dim Pleiades in June warn of El Niño |
| 2003 | Europe | Europe's [Mars Express](09-Byzantium-and-Medieval-Europe.md#mars) reaches Mars; Beagle 2 is lost | Still working more than twenty years later |
| 2005 | Africa | [SALT](10-Africa.md#telescopes) sees first light near Sutherland | The largest optical telescope in the southern hemisphere |
| 2011 | Africa | The [Tissint](10-Africa.md#mars) Mars meteorite falls in Morocco | Only the fifth Mars meteorite seen to fall |
| 2012 | Americas | The Long Count [rolls over](11-The-Americas.md#count); the Xultun tables are published | No end of the world |
| 2014 | India | India's [Mars Orbiter Mission](05-India.md#exchange) reaches Mars orbit | The first Asian nation at Mars, on its first attempt |
| 2021 | Americas | Perseverance lands in [Jezero Crater](11-The-Americas.md#mars) | Its helicopter makes the first powered flight on another planet |
| 2021 | Middle East | The UAE's [Hope](08-The-Islamic-World-and-Persia.md#mars) probe goes into orbit around Mars | Studies Martian weather from a 55-hour orbit |
| 2022 | Pacific | [Matariki](12-Oceania-and-Australia.md#matariki) becomes a public holiday in New Zealand | A star rising marks a national holiday |
| 2022 | Mediterranean | Part of [Hipparchus' lost star catalogue](07-Greece-and-Alexandria.md#hipparchus) is read in a palimpsest | Accurate to about a degree, and not what Ptolemy copied |
| 2025 | Australia | The [SKA](12-Oceania-and-Australia.md#telescopes) on Wajarri land makes its first picture | Inyarrimanha Ilgari Bundara, sharing sky and stars |
| 2026 | Japan | Japan launches [MMX](06-Korea-and-Japan.md#mars) to bring back samples of Phobos | Due back on Earth in 2031 |
| date not known; living tradition | Australia | The Wathaurung build [Wurdi Youang](01-Before-Writing.md#australia); the Emu in the Sky is read in the Milky Way's dark clouds | Solstice and equinox sunsets marked twice over; knowledge still held today |
