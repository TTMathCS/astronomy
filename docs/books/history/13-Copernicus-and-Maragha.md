# 13. Copernicus and his debt to Maragha

*In the early 1500s a church official on the Baltic coast put the Sun in the middle of the planets and set the Earth
moving. Nicolaus Copernicus kept Ptolemy's circles, and some of his most important tools had been built two and three
centuries earlier by astronomers at Maragha in Iran and in Damascus. This chapter follows his life and his book of
1543, weighs the argument over how he came to use the Maragha tools, follows the book's slow reception from Wittenberg
to Beijing and Edo, and shows how a moving Earth explains the strange backward loops of Mars.*

## 13.1 A canon on the Baltic { #life }

Nicolaus Copernicus was born on **19 February 1473** in Toruń (in German, Thorn), a trading city in Royal Prussia,
part of the Kingdom of Poland. His father was a merchant from Kraków. When his father died, around 1483, his uncle **Lucas
Watzenrode**, later the bishop of Warmia, took charge of his education.

- In 1491 he began studying at the **University of Kraków**, then strong in astronomy and mathematics.
- From 1496 he studied church law at **Bologna**, where he lodged with the astronomer Domenico Maria Novara and helped
  him observe. On **9 March 1497** they watched the Moon pass in front of the bright star Aldebaran.
- In 1500 he spent the jubilee year in Rome. From 1501 to 1503 he studied medicine at **Padua**, and on 31 May 1503
  he received a doctorate in church law at **Ferrara**.

He then went home to Warmia and lived there for the remaining forty years of his life. He was a **canon**, one of the
officials of the cathedral chapter at Frombork (Frauenburg), and he did many jobs. He was his uncle's secretary and
doctor. From 1516 to 1521 he ran the chapter's lands from Olsztyn castle, and when the Teutonic Knights besieged it in
war he directed its defence. He wrote about money: in 1519 he set down the rule now called **Gresham's law**, that bad
(debased) coins drive good ones out of circulation, decades before Thomas Gresham. Astronomy he did in what time was
left, from a tower he bought in 1514 in the walls of the cathedral at Frombork.

**German or Polish?** In the 19th and 20th centuries German and Polish writers argued over him. His parents spoke
German, and at Bologna he joined the "German nation" of students, a club for German speakers. But he was born, worked
and died a subject of the Polish king, and is thought to have spoken Polish as well as German and Latin. Historians
such as Danuta and Norbert Kasparek say a 16th-century man cannot be described with 19th- and 20th-century ideas of
nation, and the Polish poet Czesław Miłosz called the argument absurd: people then felt they belonged to their home
region, not to a nation.

## 13.2 What was wrong with Ptolemy? { #problem }

The astronomy Copernicus learned in Kraków was Ptolemy's (chapter 7), taught through a commentary on Peuerbach's
textbook, and later he read Regiomontanus's *Epitome* (chapter 9, section 9.9). It worked well enough, but it had two kinds of fault.

**It broke its own rule.** Every motion in the heavens was supposed to be uniform motion on a circle. Ptolemy's
**equant** (chapter 7, section 7.8) made each planet's circle turn at a steady rate around a point that was not its
centre, so the motion on the circle itself was not uniform. Copernicus found this unacceptable, exactly as the
astronomers of Maragha had done 250 years before him (chapter 8).

**Its parts did not fit together.** Ptolemy built a separate model for each planet. Nothing in the system fixed the
order of the planets or their distances, and in each model of Mars, Jupiter and Saturn the epicycle happened to turn
once a year, in step with the Sun (chapter 7, section 7.11). In his preface Copernicus compared the result to a
figure put together from hands, feet and a head taken from different people: a monster, not a man.

And the tables were not even very good for Mars. In 1504 Copernicus noted in his copy of the *Alfonsine Tables* that
Mars was ahead of the numbers by more than two degrees (chapter 7, section 7.9).

## 13.3 The little commentary { #commentariolus }

Some time before **1514** Copernicus gave a few friends a short handwritten sketch of a new system. It had no title
and no author's name, and is now called the ***Commentariolus***, "little commentary". We know it was circulating by
then because a library catalogue made on 1 May 1514 by the Kraków historian Matthew of Miechów lists it. It opens with
seven assumptions. In plain words:

1. The heavenly bodies do not all move round one centre.
2. The Earth's centre is the centre of the Moon's orbit, not of the universe.
3. All the planets go round the Sun, which is near the centre of the universe.
4. The distance from the Earth to the Sun is tiny compared with the distance to the stars.
5. The daily turning of the sky is caused by the Earth spinning on its axis.
6. The Sun's yearly path among the stars is caused by the Earth going round the Sun; the Earth has more than one
   motion.
7. The planets' backward motions are caused by the Earth's motion, not their own.

Assumption 4 matters. If the Earth goes round the Sun, the nearer stars should seem to shift a little through the
year. Nobody could see this shift, so Copernicus had to put the stars enormously far away. The shift, called
**parallax**, was not measured until the 1830s; Friedrich Bessel published the first good measurement in 1838.

The sketch still used only perfect circles, and the planets still rode on small epicycles. The *Commentariolus* was
never printed in his lifetime, but handwritten copies were passed around; Tycho Brahe got one in 1575. A copy found in
Vienna was finally printed in 1878.

## 13.4 The tools from Maragha { #maragha }

**The match.** When historians compared Copernicus's models with Arabic and Persian astronomy, they found that some of
his key tools had been invented before:

- the **Urdi lemma** of Mu'ayyad al-Din al-Urdi, who built the instruments at Maragha (chapter 8, section 8.8);
- the **Tusi couple** of Nasir al-Din al-Tusi, first described in 1247, two circles that turn circular motion into
  straight-line motion (chapter 8, section 8.8, with a figure);
- the models of **Ibn al-Shatir** of Damascus (died about 1375): his models for the Moon and for Mercury are
  identical to Copernicus's, and Copernicus's way of replacing the equant with two small epicycles in the
  *Commentariolus* is the same as Ibn al-Shatir's (chapter 8, section 8.9).

In 1957 Victor Roberts published Ibn al-Shatir's Sun and Moon theory under the title "a pre-Copernican Copernican
model", and in the same year Otto Neugebauer argued that the Maragha work must somehow have reached Copernicus. The one
large difference: everyone at Maragha and in Damascus kept the Earth at the centre.

**The case for a link.**

- The likeness is not just in the general idea but in the same devices used for the same planets.
- There were possible routes. Around 1300 the Byzantine scholar **Gregory Chioniades** studied in Tabriz with a pupil
  of the Maragha school and translated its works into Greek, including a description of the Tusi couple (chapter 9).
  Several Greek manuscripts with the Tusi couple are still in Italy, and a Byzantine manuscript with a Sun model using
  a second epicycle was in Italy in Copernicus's time. A 14th-century Hebrew treatise, *Sefer Meyasher
  ʻAqov* ("straightening the curved"), has been suggested as another text Copernicus might have met in Italy.
- The idea could have travelled with few traces, by word of mouth or a single manuscript, without any Arabic book being
  translated into Latin. Historians such as **George Saliba** have traced possible pathways, and there is a growing
  agreement that Copernicus became aware of the Tusi couple in some way.

**The case against, or for caution.**

- No manuscript has been found that Copernicus is known to have read, and no exact chain from Maragha to Frombork has
  been traced.
- Copernicus names the Islamic astronomers he used: al-Battani (23 times), Thabit ibn Qurra, al-Zarqali, Ibn Rushd
  (Averroes) and al-Bitruji. He never names al-Tusi, al-Urdi or Ibn al-Shatir, and shows no sign of knowing them.
- A good mathematician who wanted to get rid of the equant could have found the same devices again. Some historians
  point to a passage in the Greek writer Proclus, which Copernicus cited, and in the 14th century the Paris scholar
  Nicole Oresme described something similar, though it is unclear whether he invented it or half-understood an Arabic
  source.

**A moving Earth.** One more echo: at Samarkand and Istanbul, Ali Qushji (chapter 8, section 8.10) argued from comets
that a turning Earth was just as possible as a still one, and his arguments, like those of al-Tusi, resemble ones
Copernicus used, as the historian F. Jamil Ragep has shown. Whether there was any influence here is uncertain.

The fairest summary is that Copernicus stood on Maragha's mathematics, by a route not yet found or by finding it again,
and that the step of moving the Earth round the Sun was his own.

## 13.5 Rheticus and the book of 1543 { #book }

Copernicus kept working on a full version for nearly thirty years, but held back from printing it, perhaps for fear of
criticism. Word spread anyway. In 1533 Johann Widmanstetter, secretary to Pope Clement VII, explained the new
system to the Pope and two cardinals in Rome, and in 1536 Cardinal Nikolaus von Schönberg wrote to Copernicus urging him
to publish. Not everyone liked what they heard. At dinner on 4 June 1539, Martin Luther is reported to
have said of "that fellow" who wanted to turn astronomy upside down: "Joshua commanded the sun to stand still and not
the earth."

That same year a young mathematician from Lutheran Wittenberg, **Georg Joachim Rheticus**, came to Frombork, a
Protestant visiting a Catholic canon. He stayed two years, and in 1540 he published at Danzig (Gdańsk) a first report
of the theory, the *Narratio prima*. Copernicus at last agreed to let the book go. Rheticus took it to the printer
**Johannes Petreius** in Nuremberg, but had to leave for a post in Leipzig, and the Lutheran theologian **Andreas
Osiander** finished seeing it through the press.

***De revolutionibus orbium coelestium*** ("On the revolutions of the heavenly spheres") came out in 1543 in six books.
Copernicus dedicated it to **Pope Paul III**, hoping to quiet "babblers" who knew nothing of astronomy. At its
heart is the line: "But the Sun resides at the centre of everything."

Osiander added an unsigned note to the reader saying that the book's hypotheses "need not be true nor even probable":
it was enough if they gave calculations that matched the observations. Many readers took this to be Copernicus's own
view. His friend Bishop Tiedemann Giese was furious and asked the Nuremberg council to correct it, without success.
Kepler later revealed who had written it (chapter 14).

Copernicus had a stroke at the end of 1542 and died on **24 May 1543**. Giese wrote that he had lost his memory for
many days, and "only saw his completed book at the last moment, on the day he died". His handwritten manuscript
survives in the Jagiellonian University Library in Kraków.

## 13.6 Who read it? { #reception }

**"The book that nobody read."** The first printing of about 400 copies did not sell out, and in 1959 the writer Arthur
Koestler called *De revolutionibus* "an all-time worst seller". The astronomer and historian **Owen Gingerich** spent
35 years tracking down every surviving copy of the first two editions (1543 and Basel, 1566): 276 of the first and 325
of the second. Many are full of notes in their owners' hands, and nearly every leading astronomer of the time owned
and read it. Koestler was wrong.

But what they read is telling. Most readers skipped the moving Earth at the start of the book and worked through the
later chapters, where Copernicus's models without the equant gave a new way to compute. **Erasmus Reinhold** of
Wittenberg used them for new tables, the *Prutenic Tables* (1551), named after Prussia, which spread Copernicus's
methods widely. You could use the tables without believing that the Earth moved.

**Was it better?** Not in accuracy. Both Copernicus's and Ptolemy's systems needed more than thirty circles, and the
new one gave results about as good as the old one, no better. (The popular claim that Ptolemy needed 80 circles and
Copernicus only 34 does not hold up.) Otto Neugebauer even called Copernicus's Sun theory "a step in the wrong
direction". What it gave instead was **order**: one system in which the order of the planets and their distances from
the Sun follow from observations (section 13.10).

**Believers and opponents.** In England, **Thomas Digges** in 1576 published the first account of the system in
English and went further, removing the shell of fixed stars and scattering them out to infinity. Opponents came from
Protestant and Catholic churches alike. Luther's colleague Philipp Melanchthon wrote in 1541 against "that Polish astronomer who makes the earth
move and the sun stand still". In **March 1616**, during the Galileo affair (chapter 15), the Catholic Church's
Congregation of the Index suspended *De revolutionibus* "until corrected", calling the moving Earth a "false Pythagorean
doctrine, altogether contrary to the Holy Scripture". The corrections removed passages that spoke of the Earth's motion
as fact. The general ban on books defending it was dropped in 1758, and *De revolutionibus* left the list in 1835.

## 13.7 The idea goes round the world { #world }

**At the same moment, in India.** In 1500–1501, while Copernicus was a student, Nilakantha Somayaji in Kerala finished
his *Tantrasangraha*, with a model in which the five planets go round the Sun and the Sun goes round the Earth
(chapter 5, section 5.7). There is no sign that either knew of the other.

**The Philippines.** Copies of *De revolutionibus* travelled far. One is kept today in the archives of the University
of Santo Tomas in Manila.

**China.** The Jesuits who ran the Beijing observatory taught Tycho Brahe's system, with the Earth still at the centre
(chapter 4, section 4.11). Earlier Jesuits either ignored Copernicus or gave contradictory accounts. After the Church's
general ban was lifted, the French Jesuit **Michel Benoist**, an adviser to the Qianlong Emperor, was the first to
present the Copernican system in China.

**Japan.** Through Dutch books (chapter 6), the painter and scholar **Shiba Kōkan** learned of the new astronomy. His
world map of 1792 carries diagrams of the Copernican Earth, Sun and Moon, and he wrote an illustrated explanation of
Copernicus's astronomy.

## 13.8 The lost grave { #grave }

Copernicus was buried in Frombork Cathedral, but nobody knew exactly where. In August 2005 archaeologists scanned
beneath the cathedral floor and found remains that might be his. A forensic expert rebuilt a face from the skull
that looked like a self-portrait of Copernicus, broken nose and all. The test came from Sweden. During the
Swedish invasion of Poland in the 1650s, his books had been carried off as war booty, and they are still kept at
**Uppsala University**. Hairs found in one of them gave DNA that matched the bones, and the find was announced on
**3 November 2008**. On 22 May 2010 Copernicus was buried a second time, in the same cathedral, with a new gravestone.

## 13.9 Mars against the stars { #mars }

**The backward loop.** Most of the time Mars drifts slowly east against the stars. But about every 780 days, around
the time it is opposite the Sun in our sky, it stops, goes west for about **72 days**, stops again, and carries on east.
Ptolemy needed an epicycle for each planet to make this happen. Copernicus's seventh assumption explains it at once:
the Earth, on the inside track, moves faster and overtakes Mars, just as a faster car on a motorway seems to make a
slower one slide backwards.

The same picture explains why Mars is brightest at opposition: that is when the Earth passes closest to it.

**Copernicus's own Mars.** His observations were not very precise. Four of his recorded observations of Mars have
errors of 2, 20, 77 and 137 minutes of arc, the worst more than two degrees. Better data had to wait for Tycho, and it was Mars
again that forced Kepler to give up circles (chapter 14).

**Copernicus on Mars.** A crater close to 300 km across on Mars was named after him in 1973.

**Poland on Mars.** NASA's **InSight** lander reached Mars on 26 November 2018 with a heat probe from the German Aerospace Center, the "mole",
meant to hammer itself 5 m down. Its hammering mechanism was designed by the Polish company **Astronika** and the
Space Research Centre of the Polish Academy of Sciences. The Martian soil did not give it enough grip; after nearly two
years of trying, the team gave up on 9 January 2021 with the mole only about 40 cm deep.

## 13.10 Working it out { #working }

With the Sun at the centre, two measurements give each planet's true period and distance. These are the methods
Copernicus used, with modern numbers.

**From the synodic period to the true year.** The synodic period $S$ is the time between one opposition (or one
greatest elongation) and the next, which can be seen from Earth. For a planet outside the Earth's orbit, the Earth
gains one full lap on it in time $S$:

$$\frac{1}{P} = \frac{1}{E} - \frac{1}{S}.$$

For Mars, $S = 779.94$ days and $E = 365.256$ days:
$\frac{1}{P} = 0.0027378 - 0.0012821 = 0.0014557$, so $P = 686.98$ days, Mars's year against the stars.

**From the greatest elongation to the distance.** An inner planet never strays far from the Sun. At its greatest
elongation $\varepsilon$, the line from us to the planet just touches its orbit, so the triangle Sun, planet, observer
has a right angle at the planet:

$$r = \sin \varepsilon,$$

with $r$ in units of our own distance from the Sun. Venus's greatest elongation from Earth is about 46°, and
$\sin 46.3° = 0.723$. For outer planets the method is longer: it uses the time from opposition to the moment the planet
stands at right angles to the Sun.

**The same rules on Mars.** An astronomer on Mars could do it all again. For them the Earth is an inner planet:

| | Mars | Earth |
|---|---|---|
| Year against the stars | 686.98 days | 365.256 days |
| Time between oppositions of Mars and Earth | 779.94 days | 779.94 days |
| Inner planets | Mercury, Venus, Earth | Mercury, Venus |
| Greatest elongation of Venus | 28.3° | 46.3° |
| Greatest elongation of the Earth | 41.0° | (we are on it) |
| Synodic period of Jupiter | 816.4 days | 398.9 days |

From Mars, the Earth never gets more than 41° from the Sun, so it is always a morning or evening star. A Martian
Copernicus would find $r = \sin 41.0° = 0.656$ for the Earth's orbit in Martian units, and $1 / 0.656 = 1.524$ for
Mars's own distance in Earth units: the same answer, worked from the other end.

## 13.11 How should we judge Copernicus? { #assessment }

**What was new.** One system in place of separate models: the order of the planets, their periods and their relative
distances all follow from it, and the backward loops, the year in every epicycle and the brightness of Mars at
opposition stop being coincidences. Others had let the Earth spin or the planets circle the Sun, but nobody before
him, in Greece, India, Maragha or Samarkand, had built a working astronomy on an Earth that goes round the Sun.

**What was old.** Perfect circles, epicycles, more than thirty of them, and no gain in accuracy. He had no physics to
explain why the Earth moves without our feeling it, and no proof, since the parallax of the stars could not be seen.
On the evidence of 1543, a careful astronomer could reasonably stay unconvinced.

**What was borrowed.** His models stand on the work of al-Urdi, al-Tusi and Ibn al-Shatir, whether he knew it or
found their tools again, and his data on centuries of observation from Babylon to al-Battani. Calling his work a
lonely leap of genius leaves out Maragha; calling it a copy of Maragha leaves out the moving Earth.

**What came next.** The real test of the new system was Mars. Tycho measured it better than anyone before, and Kepler,
using his numbers, found that its orbit is not a circle at all (chapter 14).

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Where was Copernicus born, and what was his main job?

??? note "Answer"
    In Toruń, in Royal Prussia, part of the Kingdom of Poland, in 1473. He was a canon of the cathedral chapter of
    Warmia at Frombork, and also worked as a doctor, administrator and adviser on money.

**2.** (beginner) What did Osiander's unsigned note to the reader say, and why did it matter?

??? note "Answer"
    That the book's hypotheses need not be true or even probable, only useful for calculation. Many readers thought it
    was Copernicus's own view, so it softened the claim that the Earth really moves.

**3.** (beginner) Why does Mars sometimes seem to move backwards against the stars?

??? note "Answer"
    The Earth, moving faster on the inside track, overtakes Mars around opposition, so for about 72 days Mars seems to
    slide back to the west.

**4.** (intermediate) Name two devices in Copernicus's models that appear earlier in Islamic astronomy, and give one
argument for and one against the view that he learned them from that tradition.

??? note "Answer"
    The Urdi lemma and the Tusi couple (also Ibn al-Shatir's Moon and Mercury models). For: the same devices are used
    for the same planets, and Greek manuscripts with the Tusi couple were in Italy. Against: no chain of manuscripts
    has been traced, and Copernicus never names al-Tusi, al-Urdi or Ibn al-Shatir.

**5.** (intermediate) Koestler called *De revolutionibus* "the book that nobody read". How did Gingerich show this was
wrong, and what did he find people actually read?

??? note "Answer"
    He examined surviving copies of the first two editions, 276 and 325 of them, and found many full of readers' notes,
    owned by nearly every leading astronomer. Most readers studied the equant-free models in the later chapters and
    skipped the moving Earth.

**6.** (intermediate) Was Copernicus's system more accurate than Ptolemy's? If not, what did it offer?

??? note "Answer"
    No: both used more than thirty circles and gave results of about the same accuracy. It offered order: the
    sequence of the planets and their relative distances follow from observations, and the backward loops and the
    yearly epicycles are explained by the Earth's own motion.

**7.** (advanced) Jupiter's synodic period is 398.88 days. Use Copernicus's method to find its year against the stars.

??? note "Answer"
    $\frac{1}{P} = \frac{1}{365.256} - \frac{1}{398.88} = 0.0027378 - 0.0025070 = 0.0002308$, so $P \approx 4{,}333$
    days, about 11.9 years. (NASA gives 4,332.59 days; the small difference comes from rounding.)

**8.** (expert) An astronomer on Mars measures the Earth's greatest elongation as 41.0°. Find Mars's distance from the
Sun in Earth units. Then find the greatest elongation of Venus (0.723 Earth units from the Sun) as seen from Mars.

??? note "Answer"
    The Earth's orbit has radius $\sin 41.0° = 0.656$ in Mars units, so Mars is $1 / 0.656 = 1.524$ Earth units from
    the Sun. For Venus, $\sin \varepsilon = 0.723 / 1.524 = 0.474$, so $\varepsilon = 28.3°$, much closer to the Sun
    than the 46.3° we see from Earth.

## Summary

- Copernicus (1473 to 1543), a canon of Warmia trained in Kraków and Italy, was also a doctor, administrator and writer
  on money; arguments over whether he was German or Polish project modern nations onto him.
- He objected to Ptolemy's equant and to a system without a fixed order of planets; his *Commentariolus*, before 1514,
  put the Sun at the centre in seven assumptions.
- His tools, the Urdi lemma, the Tusi couple and Ibn al-Shatir's models, appeared first at Maragha and Damascus. A
  route through Byzantine and other manuscripts is likely but unproven, and independent discovery is possible.
- Rheticus pushed him to publish; *De revolutionibus* appeared in 1543 with Osiander's unsigned preface, as he died.
- It was widely read for its calculations, not its cosmology, and was no more accurate than Ptolemy; it was suspended
  by the Catholic Church in 1616.
- The idea reached China with Benoist and Japan through Dutch books; Nilakantha in Kerala had a partly Sun-centred
  model at the same time.
- His remains were identified in 2008 by DNA from hairs in his books at Uppsala.
- The Earth overtaking Mars explains its backward loop; synodic periods and elongations give the planets' periods
  and distances, from Earth or from Mars.

**Next:** chapter 14, Tycho and Kepler, where a Danish nobleman measured the planets better than anyone before the
telescope, and his assistant spent years on the orbit of Mars until he found it was an ellipse.
