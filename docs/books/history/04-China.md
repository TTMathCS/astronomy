# 4. China

*In China the sky was a government department. For more than two thousand years an office of the court watched the
heavens day and night, wrote down what it saw, and issued the calendar in the emperor's name. A strange sight in the
sky was read as Heaven's comment on how the country was being ruled, so nothing was too small to report: eclipses,
comets, dark spots on the Sun, and "guest stars" that flared up and faded. Those records, kept up dynasty after
dynasty, are still used by astronomers today. This chapter follows them from the oracle bones of the Shang kings to the
bronze instruments the Jesuits built in Beijing, and shows where the records can be trusted and where they were bent.*

## 4.1 A sky run by the state { #bureau }

In Chinese political thought the ruler was the Son of Heaven, and he ruled because Heaven allowed it. If he ruled
badly, Heaven would say so, and one of the ways it spoke was through the sky: an eclipse, a comet, a planet in the wrong
place. So the court needed people who watched the sky all the time, reported anything unusual, and explained what it
meant.

Under the Han dynasty (206 BC to AD 220) that job belonged to the **Taishiling**, the Grand Historian or Grand
Astrologer, who kept the calendar, made the observations and recorded the omens. The office changed its name many times.
Under the Sui and Tang it was the Bureau or Directorate of the Grand Historian; under the Ming and Qing it was the
**Qintianjian**, the "Bureau for Revering Heaven". In one form or another it lasted until the fall of the Qing dynasty in
1912.

The bureau had two jobs that pulled in different directions:

- **The calendar.** Giving the people the calendar was one of the duties of the emperor. A good calendar showed that
  the dynasty was in tune with Heaven, and a calendar that predicted an eclipse on the wrong day was an embarrassment.
  This side of the work wanted prediction.
- **Watching for omens.** The other side wanted the unexpected. An eclipse that came when the calendar said it would
  was less alarming than one that did not, and a new star or a comet was news that had to reach the emperor.

Babylon also read the sky for its kings (chapter 2, section 2.4), but there the scholars wrote letters. In China the
watching was an office with a staff, a budget and a duty to write everything down, and each dynasty's official history
gave the sky its own chapters. That is why China has one of the longest unbroken runs of sky records anywhere, and why
astronomers today still look things up in them.

## 4.2 Shells in a Neolithic tomb { #xishuipo }

Chapter 1 described Taosi, where a platform of about 2100 BC framed the sunrise at the solstices (section 1.7). An
older and stranger find comes from **Xishuipo**, near Puyang in Henan province, a village of the Yangshao culture.

In a grave known as tomb M45, dug in 1987 and dated to around 4000 BC, a tall man lies with two pictures made of white
clam shells beside him: a **tiger** on one side and a **dragon** on the other. At his feet is a third, smaller pattern, a
triangle of shells with two long human bones.

**The sky reading.** The Chinese archaeoastronomer Feng Shi argued in 1990 that the grave is a map of the sky. The
dragon is on the east side and the tiger on the west, and in later Chinese astronomy the Azure Dragon and the White
Tiger are the eastern and western quarters of the sky (section 4.4). The shells and bones at the feet, on the north
side, would be the **Northern Dipper**, the seven bright stars of the Big Dipper. The historian Yasukatsu Maeyama went
further: the triangle points south, and he suggested its tip stands for the pole star of that time, which he takes to
be Thuban in Draco.

**The case for.** The arrangement matches something we know for certain much later. A lacquer chest from 433 BC
(section 4.4) has the character for the Dipper in the middle, the names of the 28 mansions around it, and a dragon and a
tiger on either side: the same three elements in the same places.

**The case against.** The chest and the tomb are more than 3,500 years apart, and nothing written links them. A dragon
and a tiger could be powerful animals placed with an important man, with no thought of the sky. The "Dipper" is a small
heap of shells and two bones, and we read it as the Dipper partly because we already know the later picture. The
Wikipedia article on the site asks for better sources, and the Maeyama reading is one scholar's hypothesis.

So Xishuipo is a possible early sky picture, like many of the sites in chapter 1: interesting, worth knowing, and not
proven.

## 4.3 Oracle bones and the 60-day count { #oracle }

The oldest Chinese writing is on **oracle bones**. The late Shang kings, at their capital near modern Anyang, asked their
ancestors questions by heating ox shoulder blades and turtle shells until they cracked, and reading the cracks. The
question, and sometimes the answer and what really happened, were carved on the bone. Thousands survive.

**The 60-day count.** Almost every inscription is dated by the **sexagenary cycle**, a count of 60 named days made by
pairing 10 "heavenly stems" with 12 "earthly branches". The stems and branches advance together, so the pair names
repeat every 60 days (60 is the smallest number that both 10 and 12 divide into). One bone even carries a table of all
60 day names, like a calendar page.

The count was never reset. The *Spring and Autumn Annals*, a chronicle of the state of Lu from 722 to 481 BC, dates
eclipses by the same 60 names, and those eclipses can be computed today; they show the count has run without a break
since then, and probably since the Shang. A day named in the Annals can be counted forward, 60 at a time, to a day in
this year.

**Eclipses that date a king.** Some of the bones, from a group of diviners who worked for King Wu Ding, mention
**eclipses of the Moon**, each with its 60-day name. An eclipse of the Moon can only happen at full Moon, and the same day
name only comes round every 60 days, so a full-Moon eclipse on a named day is rare. Scholars search the computed
eclipses of the 13th century BC for ones that fall on the right day names, and the matches fix Wu Ding's reign. The
answer agrees with radiocarbon: 26 oracle bones from his reign date to 1254–1197 BC.

**Stars on the bones.** Some star names that later belong to the 28 mansions (section 4.4) have been found on oracle
bones, so part of the Chinese sky was named by about 1300 BC.

!!! note "A famous eclipse that may be a story"
    The *Book of Documents*, one of the Chinese classics, tells of two court astronomers, **Xi and He**, who were too
    drunk to predict an eclipse in the time of the Xia dynasty, more than 4,000 years ago, and were punished for it. It is
    often called the oldest eclipse record in the world. Astronomers have proposed dates for it from 2165 BC to 1905 BC;
    Theodor von Oppolzer's 22 October 2137 BC is the best known, because it puts the Sun in the right mansion.

    The trouble is the text. The chapter that tells the story, the "Punitive Expedition of Yin", is one of the "Old
    Script" chapters, which most scholars since Yan Ruoqu in the 17th century have regarded as a reconstruction made in
    the 3rd or 4th century AD. A date computed for a story written down 2,500 years after the event proves only that
    eclipses happen. The oracle bones are the oldest Chinese eclipse records we can trust.

## 4.4 Mansions of the Moon and the oldest star atlas { #mansions }

Babylon built its sky around the path of the Sun, the zodiac (chapter 2, section 2.9). China built its sky around the
**pole** and the **equator**. Around the sky ran a ring of 28
**mansions** (*xiu*), star groups of unequal width that the Moon passes through. The Moon goes round the sky in 27.3
days, so it moves through about one mansion a night.

The mansions come in four sets of seven, each with an animal and a season:

| Quarter | Symbol | Season |
|---|---|---|
| East | Azure Dragon | spring |
| North | Black Tortoise | winter |
| West | White Tiger | autumn |
| South | Vermilion Bird | summer |

**The Zeng chest.** In 1977 soldiers levelling a hill at Leigudun, in Suizhou, Hubei province, found the tomb of
Marquis Yi of Zeng, sealed around 433 BC. Among thousands of objects was a lacquered clothes chest. On its lid is a big
character *dou*, the Dipper, with the names of all 28 mansions in a ring around it, and a dragon on one side and a tiger
on the other. It is the oldest object in China with the full list of mansions, so the system is at least that old. Next
to the mansion Kang is a short date, and some scholars have computed from the picture that it shows the evening sky of
the third day of the first month in 433 BC.

**The first star lists.** In the 4th century BC, during the Warring States period, **Shi Shen** and **Gan De** wrote
the first Chinese star catalogues. With a third, legendary astronomer, Wu Xian, they became the three "schools" of
Chinese constellations, and later star maps sometimes colour each school's stars differently.

**The Dunhuang star atlas.** In 1907 the explorer Aurel Stein carried a paper scroll from the Buddhist caves at Dunhuang,
on the Silk Road, to London, where it is in the British Library. It holds a complete atlas of the sky: twelve maps of
strips of the sky by hour, and one map of the stars round the pole. In 2009 Jean-Marc Bonnet-Bidaud, Françoise Praderie
and Susan Whitfield studied it closely:

- it shows **1,339 stars** in **257 star groups**, in three colours for the three schools;
- the brightest stars are placed to within **1.5° to 4°**, which needs real mathematical methods of projection;
- it was most likely made between **AD 649 and 684**, early in the Tang dynasty.

They call it the oldest complete star atlas preserved from any civilisation.

**The Suzhou planisphere.** A star map drawn by Huang Shang in 1193 was carved in stone in 1247 at Suzhou. It shows
1,434 stars in 280 groups, and it still stands. Su Song's printed star maps of 1092 come in section 4.9.

## 4.5 Months, solar terms and leap months { #calendar }

The Chinese calendar is **lunisolar**, like the Babylonian one (chapter 2, section 2.8). Each month starts at new Moon
and has 29 or 30 days, but the year has to stay in step with the Sun for farming. Twelve months make only about 354
days, eleven short of a year, so some years get a thirteenth, **leap month**.

**The Taichu calendar.** In 104 BC Emperor Wu of the Han brought in the *Taichu* ("Grand Beginning") calendar. It set
the month at $29\tfrac{43}{81}$ days and the year at $365\tfrac{385}{1539}$ days. These two numbers were chosen so that 19
years come out to exactly 235 months, the same 19-year cycle of 7 leap months that Babylon used (section 4.12 checks
the arithmetic).

**The 24 solar terms.** The Sun's year is split into 24 **solar terms** (*jieqi*), each about 15 days long, with names
that read like a farmer's diary: Start of Spring, Rain Water, Awakening of Insects, Grain in Ear, Major Heat, Frost's
Descent, Winter Solstice. Every other term is a **major term**, so there are twelve in a year. The old way to set the
terms simply cut the year into 24 equal lengths of time. The Shixian calendar of the Qing instead used 24 equal steps
of 15° in the Sun's position, which makes the terms unequal in length, because the Earth moves faster near the Sun
(section 4.12 shows how much more this would matter on Mars). In 2016 UNESCO listed the 24 solar terms as part of the
intangible heritage of humanity.

**Which month is the leap month?** The rule in the modern calendar is neat: if a year (solstice to solstice) holds
thirteen new Moons, the first month that has **no major term** in it is the leap month. Because the months (29.5 days)
are a little shorter than the spacing of the major terms (about 30.4 days), every so often one month falls between two
major terms, and that month is the extra one.

**What about Mars?** A lunisolar calendar on Mars would make no sense. Phobos goes round Mars in under eight hours and
Deimos in about 30, so neither gives anything like a month (see the physics lesson
[Two moons](https://physics.akadian.ca/topics/spacetime/level-1/06-two-moons.html)). But solar terms would work on Mars,
and they would be very unequal, as section 4.12 shows.

- **Gai tian**, the "canopy heaven". The sky is a dome over a flat Earth and turns about the north pole once a day. The
  Sun moves in a circle on the dome, and the size of the circle changes with the seasons.
- **Hun tian**, "the entire sky". The sky is a whole sphere around the Earth. The astronomer **Zhang Heng**
  (78–139) wrote in AD 120 that the universe is like an egg, "as round as a crossbow pellet", with the stars on the
  shell and the Earth like the yolk in the middle. The armillary spheres of section 4.9 are models of a sphere like
  this.
- **Xuan ye**. The heavens have no edge, and the Sun, Moon and stars float in empty space, each
  moving at its own speed because it is not fixed to anything.

The third idea had a long life. When the Jesuits arrived in the 17th century with the European picture of solid
crystal spheres carrying the planets, Chinese scholars objected, because they had long held that the heavenly bodies
float in an infinite void (section 4.11).

**The Moon's light.** Zhang Heng also explained the Moon's phases and eclipses: "The sun is like fire and the moon like
water. The fire gives out light and the water reflects it." The side of the Moon facing the Sun is lit, the side away
from it is dark. Nine hundred years later **Shen Kuo** (1031–1095) was asked by the head of the observatory whether the
Sun and Moon were balls or flat like fans. He said they were balls, because of the way the Moon waxes and wanes, and
compared the Moon to a ball of silver that shines only when light falls on it.

## 4.7 Guest stars, broom stars and spots on the Sun { #guests }

Because the bureau had to report anything new, Chinese records hold events that nobody else wrote down, or that others
wrote down only in passing.

**Guest stars.** A star that appears where none was before, stays a while and leaves is a *kexing*, a **guest star**.
Many were novae, and a few were **supernovae**, stars blowing themselves apart.

- **AD 185.** The *Book of the Later Han* says that on a day that works out as 7 December 185 a guest star appeared
  "in the middle of the Southern Gate", two stars we call α and ε Centauri, as big as half a bamboo mat, and stayed for
  eight months. It is probably the earliest supernova on record; a shell of gas called RCW 86, in that part of the sky,
  is probably what is left of it.
- **AD 1006.** The brightest supernova in recorded history. It was seen in China, where the astrologer Zhou Keming told
  the emperor it was a yellow, lucky star, and in Egypt, where Ali ibn Ridwan described it as two and a half to three
  times as large as Venus (chapter 8), and by a monk at St Gallen in Switzerland (chapter 9).
- **AD 1054.** On 4 July 1054 a guest star appeared near the star Tianguan, which we call ζ Tauri. It was visible in
  daylight, like Venus, for 23 days, and at night until 6 April 1056, 642 days in all. It is also mentioned in a
  later Japanese diary (chapter 6) and by the doctor Ibn Butlan in Iraq (chapter 8).

**From a guest star to the Crab.** In Taurus, near ζ Tauri, is the **Crab Nebula**, a cloud of glowing gas. In 1921
Knut Lundmark listed the guest stars in the Chinese chronicles and noted that the star of 1054 lay close to it. In 1928
Edwin Hubble saw that the cloud was growing, and suggested it was the remains of the explosion the Chinese had seen;
running the expansion backwards gives a start about 900 years ago. In 1939 Nicholas Mayall argued that the star of
1054 had been a supernova, and in 1942 he and Jan Oort, with the sinologist J. J. L. Duyvendak, went back over the
records. At the centre of the nebula is a **pulsar**, a neutron star
some 30 km across spinning about 30 times a second: what is left of the star the Song astronomers saw explode.

One puzzle remains, and it is a good example of how carefully the records must be read. The Chinese text puts the guest
star "south-east of Tianguan, perhaps several inches away", but the Crab Nebula is north-west of ζ Tauri, and the
mismatch still puzzles astronomers.

**Broom stars.** Comets were *huixing*, **broom stars**, and were bad news. A silk book found in tomb 3 at
**Mawangdui**, sealed in 168 BC, draws 29 comets with different tails, each with the disaster it foretold. The *Records
of the Grand Historian* note a comet in 240 BC that astronomers identify as **Halley's comet**, its oldest sure sighting.
Calculations that follow Halley's orbit far into the past have had to lean on the Chinese sightings to stay on track.

**Spots on the Sun.** The *Book of Han* records a dark spot on the Sun in 28 BC, and from then on sunspots were noted in
the official records. A claim that Gan De saw one in 364 BC rests on later quotations of his lost book.

## 4.8 Mars in the Heart { #mars }

The old Chinese name for Mars was **Yinghuo**, which joins a word for a faint, flickering fire with a word for confusion.
Today it is *Huoxing*, the Fire Star. Mars brought war, disease and disaster, and the worst sign of all was **"Mars
staying in the Heart"**: Mars pausing in the mansion called the Heart, in the constellation we call Scorpius. Every
planet pauses now and then, when it turns round to start or end its backward loop. Mars stopping in the Heart meant
the ruler would die or his chief minister would fall.

**The prime minister of 7 BC.** The *Book of Han* tells how, in 7 BC, the astronomers reported Mars staying in the Heart.
Emperor Cheng, to turn the danger away from himself, blamed his prime minister, **Zhai Fangjin**, and made him take his
own life. The emperor died within a month anyway.

In 1990 the Taiwanese historians **Chang Chia-feng** and **Huang Yi-long** computed where Mars really was. It was nowhere
near Scorpius; around that time it was between Corvus and Virgo, far to the west. They concluded that the omen had been
invented by someone at court who wanted the prime minister gone. People at the time could not easily check, and they
believed in any case that a planet might stray from its predicted place if Heaven willed it.

**How often were the records wrong?** The Chinese astronomers Liu Ciyuan and Wu Limin went through 25 reports of "Mars
staying in the Heart" in the official histories and found more than half to be wrong: a few years off, miscopied, or
not true at all. Whether the false ones were made up on purpose, or are simply mistakes, is still argued over.

This matters for everything else in this chapter. The Chinese records are a treasure, but they were written by
officials whose careers depended on what the sky was said to mean. The safest records are the ones a modern astronomer
can check by calculation, like eclipses, or against something still in the sky, like the Crab Nebula.

## 4.9 Machines that turn with the sky { #machines }

**Zhang Heng's armillary sphere.** An armillary sphere is a set of metal rings standing for the equator, the Sun's path
and other circles of the sky. Zhang Heng, the same man who described the cosmic egg, was the first we know of to make
one turn by water power, so that it kept pace with the sky. (He also built the first seismoscope, which showed the
direction of an earthquake hundreds of kilometres away.)

**Yi Xing's escapement and survey.** In 725 the Buddhist monk **Yi Xing** (683–727) and the official Liang Lingzan
built a water-driven celestial globe with an **escapement**, a device that lets a wheel move forward one step at a time,
the key part of later mechanical clocks. Yi Xing also led a survey for the Tang court with thirteen stations from
Jiaozhou, in what is now Vietnam, at latitude 17° N, to near Lake Baikal at 50° N. At each station the observers
measured the height of the pole and the noon shadows at midsummer and midwinter. The survey overturned an old practice
of turning the difference in shadow lengths between two places directly into the distance between them (section 4.12
shows why it fails), and it gave a fairly good length for one degree of latitude.

**Su Song's clock tower.** In Kaifeng, the Song capital, **Su Song** (1020–1101) built a tower about 12 m tall: a wooden
model in 1088, the bronze parts by 1090, the whole finished in 1094. A water wheel with an escapement drove an armillary
sphere on the roof, a celestial globe inside, and a clockwork that told the time. It used the earliest known
chain drive. His book on it, of 1092, also printed a star atlas in five maps, drawn in a projection like a modern
rectangular map, with the hour lines of the mansions running up and down. In 1127 an invading Jurchen army took Kaifeng
and took the tower apart. The armillary sphere was carried north, but the tower was never rebuilt, not even by Su
Song's son. His book survives, so we know how it worked.

**Shen Kuo and the pole.** Shen Kuo, Su Song's rival at court, watched the pole star every night for three months and
found that the true pole of the sky was a little over 3° away from it; the pole had shifted since an earlier
astronomer's time. Su Song used Shen's corrected pole on one of his star maps. Shen also planned, with his colleague
Wei Pu, five years of daily positions of the Moon and the planets, but his political enemies stopped it.

## 4.10 Guo Shoujing's great gnomon { #guo }

After the Mongols conquered China, **Kublai Khan** ordered a new calendar, and the work went to **Guo Shoujing**
(1231 to about 1315), an engineer who had already built canals and water works, with Wang Xun. Their team set up 27
observing stations across the empire, and in 1276 built a great tower at **Gaocheng**, near Dengfeng in Henan, a place
that tradition held to be the centre of heaven and earth, where the Duke of Zhou was said to have measured the Sun's
shadow more than 2,000 years before. The tower is now part of a UNESCO World Heritage Site.

The building is 9.46 m high, 12.62 m with the two small rooms on top, and between those rooms
a horizontal bar served as the top of a giant **gnomon**, a shadow stick. Running north from the foot of the tower is the
**shigui**, the "sky-measuring scale": 36 stone blocks, 31.19 m long and 0.53 m wide, with two water channels cut along
it to check that it is level. At noon the bar's shadow falls on the scale, and its length tells how high the Sun is.

Why so tall? The edge of a shadow is blurred, because the Sun is a disc, not a point. A taller gnomon makes a longer
shadow but the blur stays about the same size, so the error in the Sun's angle shrinks. Guo also used a small device pierced
with holes that threw a sharp image of the bar onto the scale; the shadow could then be read, it is said, to within
about 2 mm (section 4.12 works out what that means).

**The Shoushi calendar.** From these shadows Guo found the moments of the solstices more exactly than ever before, and
from them the length of the year: **365.2425 days**. The *Shoushi* ("Season-Granting") calendar of 1281 used it, and
China kept that calendar for 364 years. It is exactly the average length of the Gregorian year that Europe adopted in
1582, three centuries later.

**Persian instruments in Beijing.** Guo was not working alone. In 1267 the Persian astronomer **Jamal al-Din**, who had
worked at the Maragha observatory, brought Kublai Khan seven instruments, among them a terrestrial globe and an
armillary sphere, and in 1271 he became the first head of an **Islamic Astronomical Bureau** in Beijing. It worked side
by side with the Chinese bureau for about four centuries, and its tables were translated into Chinese in 1384. The
Islamic side of this story is in chapter 8.

## 4.11 The Jesuits and the Beijing observatory { #jesuits }

The Beijing observatory, a platform on the old city wall, was finished in 1442 under the Ming. By the end of the Ming
the official calendar was going wrong, and the court turned to some unusual helpers: Jesuit missionaries from Europe.

- **Xu Guangqi** (1562–1633), a Chinese mathematician and agricultural scientist who worked with the Jesuit **Matteo Ricci**, added 23 new
  constellations near the south pole of the sky, taken from European star catalogues.
- In 1630 the Jesuits **Johann Adam Schall von Bell** and Giacomo Rho came to Beijing to work with Xu on a reformed
  calendar, the Chongzhen calendar, which predicted eclipses better than the old one.
- In 1644 the Ming fell to the Manchus, who founded the Qing. Schall gained the trust of the new emperor, and European
  methods became official in the Shixian calendar. In 1659 the Qing officially dropped the Islamic tradition.
- In 1664 the Chinese astronomer **Yang Guangxian**, who had attacked the Jesuits in print, challenged them. Schall was
  imprisoned and condemned to death, though the sentence was not carried out, and Yang took over the bureau.
- Then errors were found in Yang's calendar. The young Kangxi Emperor set a public test with three tasks: predict the
  length of a gnomon's shadow at noon on a given day, the positions of the Sun and planets on a given date, and the
  time of a coming eclipse of the Moon. The Belgian Jesuit **Ferdinand Verbiest** got all three right, and was put in
  charge of the bureau and the observatory.

In 1673 Verbiest rebuilt the observatory with new bronze instruments made in the European style: an ecliptic armillary
sphere, an altazimuth, a sextant, an equatorial armillary sphere and others. In 1900, when troops of the Eight-Nation
Alliance occupied Beijing, they carried some of them off; France and Germany gave them back
after the First World War. They stand on the platform in Beijing today.

The Jesuits did not bring the idea of a moving Earth. They taught Tycho Brahe's system, with the planets going round the
Sun and the Sun going round the Earth (chapter 14), and some Chinese scholars disliked the solid crystal spheres that
came with European astronomy, because the old xuan ye picture (section 4.6) had the stars floating free.

## 4.12 Working it out { #working }

**How good was the Taichu month?** The Taichu month was $29\tfrac{43}{81} = 29.530864$ days. The real average month,
new Moon to new Moon, is 29.530589 days (chapter 2, section 2.7). The difference is

$$29.530864 - 29.530589 = 0.000275 \text{ days} \approx 24 \text{ seconds a month}.$$

That sounds tiny, but it adds up. A whole day of error builds up in $1 / 0.000275 \approx 3{,}600$ months, about 290
years. After a few centuries the calendar's new Moons came a day late, and a new calendar was needed. China changed its
official calendar many times for this kind of reason.

**Why 19 years are exactly 235 Taichu months.** Notice that $1539 = 19 \times 81$. Then

$$19 \times 365\tfrac{385}{1539} = 6{,}939\tfrac{61}{81}, \qquad 235 \times 29\tfrac{43}{81} = 6{,}939\tfrac{61}{81}.$$

The numbers were built so the cycle closes exactly: $235 - 19 \times 12 = 7$ leap months in 19 years. In reality 19 years
hold $19 \times 365.2422 / 29.530589 = 234.997$ months, very close but not exact.

**Guo Shoujing's year.** Guo's 365.2425 days is longer than the year of the seasons, 365.2422 days, by 0.0003 days, about
26 seconds. That is one day in about 3,300 years.

**Why Yi Xing's survey broke the shadow rule.** At noon on the winter solstice the Sun's height is
$90^\circ - \text{latitude} - 23.6^\circ$ (the Earth's tilt in Yi Xing's time), and a stick of height $h$ casts a shadow of
length $h \tan(\text{latitude} + 23.6^\circ)$. For a stick 1 m high:

| Latitude | Winter noon shadow | Growth for each 1° further north |
|---|---|---|
| 17° N (Jiaozhou) | 0.86 m | 3.0 cm |
| 34.4° N (Gaocheng) | 1.60 m | 6.2 cm |
| 50° N (near Lake Baikal) | 3.40 m | 21.9 cm |

One degree of latitude is always about 111 km of ground, but the shadow grows seven times faster in the north than in
the south. So no fixed rule of "so many centimetres of shadow for so many kilometres" can work; the shadow follows the
tangent, not a straight line. For angles like these, see the physics lesson
[Angles in the sky](https://physics.akadian.ca/topics/spacetime/level-5/01-angles-in-the-sky.html).

**The Gaocheng shadows, and the same tower on Mars.** Gaocheng is at latitude 34.40° N. The Earth's tilt in 1279 was
about 23.53° (it shrinks slowly; today it is 23.44°). With the bar 12.6 m above the scale, the noon Sun is
$90 - 34.40 + 23.53 = 79.13^\circ$ high at midsummer and $90 - 34.40 - 23.53 = 32.07^\circ$ high at midwinter, and the
shadow is $12.6 / \tan(\text{height})$. Mars is tilted 25.19°, so a tower at the same latitude on Mars would see:

| | Mars | Earth (1279) |
|---|---|---|
| Tilt of the axis | 25.19° | 23.53° |
| Noon Sun at midsummer | 80.79° | 79.13° |
| Shadow at midsummer | 2.04 m | 2.42 m |
| Noon Sun at midwinter | 30.41° | 32.07° |
| Shadow at midwinter | 21.47 m | 20.11 m |

At Gaocheng at midwinter a change of 1 cm in the shadow means a change of about 0.8′ (minutes of arc) in the Sun's
height. With a stick one fifth as tall, 2.5 m, the same 1 cm would be about 3.8′. That is the point of a tall gnomon.

**Solar terms on Mars.** Under the Qing method each solar term is a 15° step of the Sun. A planet moves fastest at its
closest point to the Sun (perihelion) and slowest at its farthest (aphelion), so the terms there are shortest and
longest. Mars's orbit is much more stretched than the Earth's:

| | Mars | Earth |
|---|---|---|
| Eccentricity of the orbit | 0.0934 | 0.0167 |
| Year | 668.59 sols | 365.24 days |
| Average solar term (1/24 of a year) | 27.9 sols | 15.2 days |
| Shortest term, near perihelion | about 23.0 sols | about 14.7 days |
| Longest term, near aphelion | about 33.5 sols | about 15.7 days |

On Earth the terms differ by a day; on Mars by more than ten sols. A Martian farmer's diary of 24 terms would have some
weeks much longer than others. More on Mars's year in the physics lessons
[The Mars year](https://physics.akadian.ca/topics/spacetime/level-4/04-the-mars-year.html) and
[A calendar for Mars](https://physics.akadian.ca/topics/spacetime/level-4/06-a-calendar-for-mars.html).

**How bright was the star of 1054?** It reached about magnitude −6, as bright as Venus at its best. The Crab is 6,500
light-years away, about 2,000 parsecs. A star's brightness at a standard distance of 10 parsecs, its absolute magnitude,
is

$$M = m - 5 \log_{10}\frac{d}{10\text{ pc}} = -6 - 5 \log_{10} 200 \approx -17.5.$$

The Sun's absolute magnitude is 4.8, and each 5 magnitudes is a factor of 100, so the supernova shone like
$10^{0.4 \times (4.8 + 17.5)} \approx 860$ million Suns, and more than that once we allow for dust between us and it.

## 4.13 How good are the Chinese records? { #assessment }

**What China did superbly.** Few places kept watch for so long without a break. The 60-day count has run since the
Shang; the official histories record eclipses, comets, guest stars and sunspots for two thousand years. Modern
astronomers use these records to date supernova remnants, to trace Halley's comet back to 240 BC, and, together with
Babylonian and Arab eclipse records, to measure how the Earth's spin has slowed (chapter 2, section 2.12). The
instruments were superb too: Su Song's clockwork, Guo Shoujing's gnomon and his year of 365.2425 days.

**What the records are not.** They were made for omens, so they say *that* something happened and roughly where, but
rarely give careful positions. They were copied and recopied, and errors crept in. Some were bent for politics, as the
Mars report of 7 BC was. A record that cannot be checked by calculation should be treated as a clue, not a fact.

**A different kind of astronomy.** Chinese calendar makers predicted the Sun, Moon and planets with numbers and periods,
much as the Babylonians did (chapter 2), rather than with geometric models of circles in space like the Greeks
(chapter 7). They were very good at it, and the method suited a state that wanted reliable calendars.

**Never sealed off.** Chinese astronomy took in ideas from outside again and again: Persian instruments and tables under
the Mongols, European methods under the Jesuits. And it gave the world records that no other culture kept.

A warning about "firsts". Popular books often say China was first with this or that. Some claims are sound, like the
supernova of 185. Others rest on a single late quotation, like the sunspot of 364 BC, or on a story written
down long after the event, like the eclipse of Xi and He. Ask, each time, how old the text really is.

    - The use of ancient Chinese and other eclipse records to measure the Earth's slowing spin: see chapter 2,

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Why did the Chinese court employ astronomers, and what were their two main jobs?

??? note "Answer"
    Events in the sky were read as Heaven's comments on the ruler, so the court needed people to watch for them. Their
    two jobs were making the calendar, which the emperor gave to the people, and watching for and reporting omens such as
    eclipses, comets and guest stars.

**2.** (beginner) What is a guest star, and what did the guest star of 1054 leave behind?

??? note "Answer"
    A star that appears where none was before, stays for a while and fades. Some were novae or comets; the one of 1054
    was a supernova. It left the Crab Nebula, an expanding cloud of gas, with a spinning neutron star (a pulsar) at its
    centre.

**3.** (beginner) Name the four animals of the Chinese sky and the directions they belong to.

??? note "Answer"
    The Azure Dragon (east), the Black Tortoise (north), the White Tiger (west) and the Vermilion Bird (south). Each has
    seven of the 28 mansions.

**4.** (intermediate) Today is day 1 of the 60-day cycle. Which day of the cycle will it be 1,000 days from now?

??? note "Answer"
    $1{,}000 = 16 \times 60 + 40$, so after 16 full cycles there are 40 days left over. Day 1 plus 40 days is day 41.

**5.** (intermediate) Why do historians think the report of Mars in the Heart in 7 BC was invented?

??? note "Answer"
    Mars's position can be calculated backwards for any date. Chang Chia-feng and Huang Yi-long found that Mars was far
    from Scorpius at that time, between Corvus and Virgo. The report was false, and it gave the emperor a reason to force
    his prime minister to die. The lesson is that records written for omens must be checked against calculation where
    possible.

**6.** (intermediate) Why did Guo Shoujing build a gnomon about 12.6 m high instead of an ordinary one?

??? note "Answer"
    The edge of a shadow is blurred because the Sun is a disc. A taller gnomon gives a longer shadow with about the same
    blur, so the Sun's height can be measured more exactly. At Gaocheng 1 cm of shadow at midwinter is about 0.8′ of the
    Sun's height for a 12.6 m gnomon, but about 3.8′ for one 2.5 m tall.

**7.** (advanced) How long is the noon shadow of Guo's 12.6 m gnomon at Gaocheng (34.40° N) on the day of an equinox?

??? note "Answer"
    At an equinox the noon Sun is $90^\circ - 34.40^\circ = 55.60^\circ$ high. The shadow is
    $12.6 / \tan 55.60^\circ = 12.6 / 1.460 \approx 8.63$ m.

**8.** (expert) On Mars, a solar term near perihelion lasts about 23.0 sols and one near aphelion about 33.5 sols. Show
where the ratio comes from.

??? note "Answer"
    By Kepler's second law the angular speed of a planet round the Sun is proportional to $1/r^2$. At perihelion
    $r = a(1 - e)$ and at aphelion $r = a(1 + e)$, so the ratio of the speeds is $\left(\frac{1 + e}{1 - e}\right)^2$.
    With $e = 0.0934$ that is $(1.0934 / 0.9066)^2 \approx 1.45$. A 15° step takes 1.45 times as long at aphelion, and
    $23.0 \times 1.45 \approx 33.4$ sols, matching the table to rounding.

## Summary

- In China, astronomy was a government office from the Han to 1912. It made the calendar and reported omens, and so
  kept one of the longest unbroken runs of sky records anywhere.
- A Neolithic tomb at Xishuipo (about 4000 BC) may show the Dipper with a dragon and tiger, but the reading is disputed.
- The oracle bones (about 1250–1200 BC) use a 60-day count that has never been broken, and their eclipses of the Moon
  help date King Wu Ding. The story of Xi and He is a late text, not a trustworthy record.
- The sky was organised around the pole and the equator, with 28 mansions of the Moon in four quarters. The Zeng chest
  of 433 BC lists all 28; the Dunhuang atlas (649–684) is the oldest complete star atlas.
- The calendar is lunisolar, with 24 solar terms and a leap month wherever a month has no major term. The Taichu
  calendar of 104 BC made 19 years exactly 235 months.
- Three pictures of the universe: a canopy, a sphere like an egg, and bodies floating in empty space.
- Guest stars of 185, 1006 and 1054 were supernovae; the star of 1054 left the Crab Nebula and its pulsar. Comets and
  sunspots were recorded too, including Halley's comet in 240 BC.
- The report of Mars in the Heart in 7 BC was almost certainly invented; many such omens were wrong.
- Zhang Heng's water-driven sphere, Yi Xing's escapement and survey, Su Song's clock tower, and Guo Shoujing's 12.6 m
  gnomon and year of 365.2425 days show the craft of the instrument makers.
- Persian astronomers in the Yuan and Jesuits in the Qing brought outside methods; Verbiest's bronze instruments of
  1673 still stand in Beijing.
