# 20. Radio astronomy

*For all of history, astronomy meant looking. Then, in 1932, an engineer listening for static on a telephone line
heard the Milky Way. Radio waves opened a second sky, full of things no eye could see: hydrogen gas tracing the arms
of our galaxy, stars that tick like clocks, and a faint glow left over from the Big Bang. Today radio dishes spread across
continents work together as one telescope, and radio is also how we talk to every machine at Mars.*

## 20.1 Static on the line { #jansky }

In 1928 a young engineer named **Karl Jansky**, born in the Oklahoma Territory, joined Bell Telephone Laboratories.
Bell Labs wanted to send telephone calls across the Atlantic by short-wave radio, and Jansky's job was to find out
what caused the static that might spoil them. Because of a kidney illness he was sent to the field station at
**Holmdel, New Jersey**, where the air was healthier.

He built an antenna for waves 14.6 m long and mounted it on four wheels from a Ford Model T, so it could turn
round in a circle. People called it **Jansky's merry-go-round**. After months of listening he sorted the static into
three kinds: nearby thunderstorms, distant thunderstorms, and a faint, steady **hiss** of unknown origin.

At first the hiss seemed to peak about once a day, so he suspected the Sun. But it came back every **23 hours and
56 minutes**, four minutes short of a day. A friend, the astrophysicist Albert Skellett, pointed out that this is a
**sidereal day**, the time the Earth takes to turn once against the stars. The hiss came from beyond the Solar System.
Comparing with star maps, Jansky found it was strongest towards **Sagittarius**, the direction of the centre of the
Milky Way.

He announced it in Washington, D.C., in April 1933, to a small audience who could not see why it mattered. He wanted
to go on, but after 1935 Bell Labs moved him to another project. He died in 1950, aged 44. The unit of radio brightness, the **jansky**, is named after him.

## 20.2 A dish in the back yard { #reber }

Hardly any professional astronomer followed up Jansky's discovery. A radio amateur did. **Grote Reber**, an
electrical engineer from Wheaton, near Chicago, built a radio telescope in his own back yard in the summer of 1937.
It was a curved metal **dish 9 m across**, the first dish ever used as a radio telescope.

All the while he worked for radio makers in Chicago. In 1941 he finished the first **radio map
of the sky**, and extended it in 1943. It showed bright radio sources that nobody had known of, including **Cygnus A**
and **Cassiopeia A**. In 1954 he moved to **Tasmania**, where he went on studying low-frequency radio waves, and he died there
in 2002.

## 20.3 Radar, the Sun and a cliff in Sydney { #war }

**Britain, 1942.** **James Stanley Hey** was working on ways to stop German jamming of British radar. On 27 and 28
February 1942, radar sites across Britain reported strong noise jamming. Hey noticed that the noise followed the Sun,
and the Royal Observatory at Greenwich told him a very active sunspot was crossing the Sun's face. The jamming was the
**Sun itself**, sending out radio waves. In February 1946 Hey's team also found a strong radio source in **Cygnus** that
flickered rapidly.

**Australia, 1946.** At the Radiophysics division of CSIR in Sydney, **Joseph Pawsey**, **Ruby Payne-Scott** and
Lindsay McCready used an air force radar antenna at Collaroy in northern Sydney, and then the cliffs of **Dover
Heights**, looking out to sea. Radio waves from the Sun reached the antenna two ways, straight and reflected off the
water, and the two together let them tie the Sun's radio waves to large groups of sunspots. On **26 January 1946** this was the first use of a radio
**interferometer**, two paths or dishes working as one, for astronomy. Their data also showed that some regions of the Sun
are as hot as a million degrees. Paul Wild later called Pawsey the father of radio astronomy in Australia.

**Ruby Payne-Scott** had been the third woman to graduate in physics at the University of Sydney. In 1944 she married
in secret, because a law barred married women from permanent jobs in the public service. When this came out in 1950
she lost her permanent post, and in 1951, a few months before her first child was born, she resigned, since there
was no maternity leave. Since 2021 the Australian Academy of Science has given a Ruby Payne-Scott Medal for women
scientists.

## 20.4 The song of hydrogen { #hydrogen }

Dust hides much of the Milky Way from ordinary telescopes, but radio waves pass through it. The Dutch astronomer
**Jan Oort** saw that a radio spectral line would be a powerful tool, and put the question to the young **Hendrik van
de Hulst**. In 1944 van de Hulst predicted that cold **hydrogen** atoms should give out radio waves at exactly
**1420.4 MHz**, a wavelength of **21 cm**. A single atom does this only about once in 11 million years, but space
holds so much hydrogen that the line can be found.

In 1951 **Harold Ewen** and **Edward Purcell** found the line at Harvard with a horn antenna. They waited to publish
until two other groups had found it too: **Lex Muller and Oort** in the Netherlands, and **Christiansen and Hindman**
in Australia. Like the lines of chapter 18, the 21 cm line is shifted by the Doppler effect, so it shows how fast the
gas is moving. From 1952 maps of hydrogen across the Milky Way showed its **spiral arms** for the first time.

## 20.5 Big dishes and great arrays { #dishes }

**Jodrell Bank, England.** In 1957 Bernard Lovell's team finished a dish **76.2 m** across, the largest steerable
dish in the world. It was ready just in time to track **Sputnik 1**, the first artificial satellite, and it went on to
follow Soviet and American probes to the Moon, Venus and Mars. In 1966 it picked up the first pictures from the
Moon's surface, sent by the Soviet **Luna 9**.

**Cambridge, England.** **Martin Ryle** was the driving force behind ways of combining the signals of several dishes,
called interferometry and **aperture synthesis**, which made radio pictures far sharper. In 1974 he
shared the Nobel Prize in Physics with Antony Hewish, the first Nobel prize given for astronomy.

**Elsewhere.** In Australia, Bernard Mills built a cross of antennas at Fleurs near Sydney in 1954, and the 64 m dish
at Parkes followed in 1961 (chapter 12). In Puerto Rico the **Arecibo** dish, 305 m across and set in a natural
hollow, was finished in 1963; in 1964 it showed that Mercury turns once in 59 days, not 88 as had been thought. In
southern Russia the **RATAN-600**, a ring of reflectors 576 m across, began work in 1974.

## 20.6 Quasars and pulsars { #quasars }

**A star that was not a star.** The radio source **3C 273** looked, in an ordinary telescope, like a faint star. **Cyril
Hazard** used the Parkes dish to time the moment the Moon passed in front of it, which gave its exact
position. In 1963 **Maarten Schmidt** found that its spectral lines are shifted a long way to the red: 15.8 per cent.
By Hubble's law (chapter 19) it is about 2.4 billion light-years away, and yet it shines 16 times brighter than the
whole galaxy around it. 3C 273 was the first **quasar**. It is now known to be the centre of a galaxy, lit up by gas
falling into a black hole about 900 million times the mass of the Sun.

**A star that ticks.** In 1967 **Jocelyn Bell**, a research student from Lurgan in Northern Ireland, was checking
the paper charts from a radio array at Cambridge that she had spent two years helping to build, sometimes 29 m of
chart a night. On **28 November 1967** she found a signal that pulsed every **1.337 seconds**, as steady as a clock.
For a while it was called **LGM-1**, for "little green men". It turned out to be a **pulsar**, a rapidly spinning
neutron star.

**The prize.** The 1974 Nobel Prize went to Hewish, her supervisor, and Ryle. Bell was left out. Fred Hoyle
criticised the decision at the time. Bell herself said in 1977 that she thought it would "demean Nobel Prizes if they
were awarded to research students, except in very exceptional cases, and I do not believe this is one of them". In
2018 she won the Special Breakthrough Prize, worth three million dollars, and gave it all to a fund to help women,
minority and refugee students become physicists.

## 20.7 The afterglow of the Big Bang { #afterglow }

In 1948 **Ralph Alpher**, a student of George Gamow, and **Robert Herman** predicted that space should still be
faintly warm, at about **5 degrees above absolute zero**. In the spring of 1964 the Soviet
physicists **Andrei Doroshkevich** and **Igor Novikov** suggested that this glow could be found with microwave
receivers. At Princeton, Robert Dicke's group began building one.

They were beaten. Also in 1964, at Bell Labs in **Holmdel**, the same town where Jansky had heard the Milky Way,
**Arno Penzias** and **Robert Wilson** were using a big horn antenna for radio astronomy and satellite work. On 20 May
1964 they measured a faint extra warmth, about 4 degrees above absolute zero, that they could not account for. After
a phone call from them, Dicke said, "Boys, we've been scooped." It was the **afterglow of the Big Bang**,
now called the **cosmic microwave background**. Penzias and Wilson shared the Nobel Prize in 1978.

The glow was set free about **380,000 years** after the Big Bang, when the universe had cooled to about 3,000 degrees
and became clear. Since then space has stretched so much that the glow is now at just **2.725 K**. Spacecraft such as
COBE, WMAP and Planck have mapped its tiny ripples. The glow fills all of space, so a radio receiver on Mars would
see almost exactly the same sky.

## 20.8 Around the world { #world }

- **India.** **Govind Swarup**, born in Thakurdwara in 1929, learned to build radio telescopes with Pawsey in Sydney
  and then went to Stanford. Back in India he designed the **Ooty Radio Telescope**, finished in 1970: a trough
  530 m long and 30 m wide laid on a hillside at a slope of 11°, the same as the latitude, so that it follows the stars
  as the Earth turns. With it his team timed the Moon passing in front of hundreds of radio galaxies and quasars to
  measure their shapes. He went on to lead the **Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope** at Khodad, 80 km north of Pune:
  thirty dishes 45 m across, the largest and most sensitive array in the world for long radio waves.
- **China.** In Guizhou the **FAST** dish, 500 m across, lies in a natural hollow in the limestone hills. It was
  pushed for by the astronomer Nan Rendong, saw first light in September 2016, and found its first two new pulsars in
  August 2017. It is the largest single dish in the world, and took that title from Arecibo, which collapsed in 2020.
- **Chile.** **ALMA**, 66 antennas on the Chajnantor plateau at 5,000 m in the Atacama desert, works at millimetre
  wavelengths, between radio and infrared. It was built by partners from Europe, North America and East Asia.
- **Russia.** The RATAN-600 ring (section 20.5).
- **Australia and South Africa.** Parkes (chapter 12) and MeerKAT (chapter 10); and both share the SKA (section 20.9).

## 20.9 Many dishes as one { #arrays }

The sharpness of a telescope depends on the wavelength divided by its width. Radio waves are hundreds of thousands of
times longer than light, so a single radio dish sees a blurred sky: even a 76 m dish working at 21 cm, looking at
Mars, sees only a smudge 27 times wider than the planet. The answer has been to spread dishes apart and combine their signals, as
Payne-Scott, Pawsey and Ryle first did.

**A picture of a black hole.** The **Event Horizon Telescope** joins dishes from Hawaii, Arizona, Mexico and Chile to
France, the South Pole and Greenland, so that together they act like a dish as wide as the Earth. Data are flown on
hard drives to Germany and the United States and combined there. Working at a wavelength of 1.3 mm, they can make out
details as small as about 25 millionths of a second of arc. On **10 April 2019** they showed the first picture of a
black hole, in the galaxy M87, a dark shadow inside a bright ring. On **12 May 2022** they showed the black
hole at the centre of our own Milky Way, **Sagittarius A***, in the direction where Jansky's hiss had been loudest.

**The Square Kilometre Array.** Building began on **5 December 2022** in South Africa and Australia. In the Karoo in
South Africa, SKA-Mid will have 197 dishes, including MeerKAT's 64. In Western Australia, SKA-Low will spread 100,000
antennas across 74 km on the traditional lands of the **Wajarri** people, who named the site **Inyarrimanha Ilgari
Bundara**, "sharing sky and stars". Eight other African countries joined South Africa's bid, the headquarters is at
Jodrell Bank, and India agreed to join in 2024.

## 20.10 Radio and Mars { #mars }

**Talking to Mars.** Every picture from Mars reaches us by radio. NASA's **Deep Space Network** has three stations,
at **Goldstone** in California, near **Madrid** in Spain and near **Canberra** in Australia, placed around the world so
that as the Earth turns, one of them can always see a spacecraft. Each has a 70 m dish and several 34 m dishes. So many
craft now orbit Mars that one dish often listens to several of them at once. The Canberra station opened in 1965, and its 70 m
dish is the only antenna on Earth that can send commands to Voyager 2. India's own **Deep Space Network** at Byalalu, near Bengaluru,
has a 32 m dish and tracked India's Mars Orbiter Mission (chapter 21). A message takes between about 3 and 22 minutes
to cross (chapter 16).

**Radar under the ice.** Radio can also look *into* Mars. The Italian radar **MARSIS** on Europe's **Mars Express**
sends radio pulses down from orbit through a 40 m antenna and listens for echoes from below the surface. In 2004 it
found that the south polar cap is fairly clean ice, up to 3.7 km thick.

- **For a lake.** In July 2018, 22 Italian scientists reported a bright radar echo 1.5 km under the south polar ice,
  about 20 km across, which they read as a lake of very salty water. In 2020 more radar studies reported three
  smaller patches around it.
- **Against.** Other researchers doubted it. A 2022 study suggested that the bright echo could come from volcanic
  rock instead of water. Another 2022 study found salty brines the best explanation,
  but perhaps soaked between grains of ice or rock rather than forming an open lake. The question is still open.

## 20.11 Working it out { #working }

**Wavelength.** Radio waves travel at the speed of light, $c = 299{,}792$ km/s, so the wavelength is $\lambda = c/f$.

- Jansky's 20.5 MHz: $299{,}792{,}458 / 20{,}500{,}000 = 14.6$ m.
- Hydrogen's 1420.4 MHz: $299{,}792{,}458 / 1{,}420{,}400{,}000 = 0.211$ m, or 21 cm.

**The afterglow.** Chapter 18's rule says a glowing body is brightest at a wavelength of $2898/T$ micrometres, with
$T$ in kelvins. At 2.725 K that is $2898 / 2.725 = 1063$ µm, or **1.06 mm**. At 3,000 K, when the glow was set free,
it was $2898 / 3000 = 0.97$ µm, just past red light. The universe has stretched by about $3000 / 2.725 \approx 1100$
times since.

**Sharpness.** The smallest angle a telescope of width $D$ can make out at wavelength $\lambda$ is about
$1.22\,\lambda / D$ radians. Multiply by 206,265 for seconds of arc.

| Telescope | $\lambda$ | $D$ | Smallest detail |
|---|---|---|---|
| Jodrell Bank dish | 21.1 cm | 76.2 m | $1.22 \times 0.211 / 76.2 \times 206{,}265 = 697$″ |
| FAST | 21.1 cm | 500 m | 106″ |
| GMRT | 21.1 cm | 25 km | 2.1″ |
| Hooker telescope | 550 nm | 2.5 m | 0.055″ |
| Event Horizon Telescope | 1.3 mm | 12,756 km | 0.000026″ |

Mars at its closest is 25.6″ wide (chapter 17), so the Jodrell Bank dish blurs it 27 times over.

**Mars and the Earth.**

| | Mars | Earth |
|---|---|---|
| Widest array that fits on the planet (its diameter) | 6,792 km | 12,756 km |
| Smallest detail at 1.3 mm | $1.22 \times 0.0013 / 6{,}792{,}000 \times 206{,}265 = 0.000048$″ | 0.000026″ |
| Jansky's hiss returns every (one turn against the stars) | 24 h 37 min | 23 h 56 min |
| Brightest wavelength of its own heat glow | 13.5 µm | 10.1 µm |
| Temperature of the afterglow sky | 2.725 K | 2.725 K |

## 20.12 How should we judge this story? { #assessment }

**Outsiders first.** Radio astronomy was started by an engineer at a telephone company, an amateur in his back yard
and radar scientists in wartime. Professional astronomers were slow to see what radio could do.

**Who gets the credit.** Payne-Scott lost her job for being married, and Bell was left out of a Nobel Prize for a
discovery she made, though she herself defended the decision. Penzias and Wilson found the afterglow by accident,
while the people who had predicted it and the team building a receiver to find it got less credit. Asking who did the
work, and who was thanked, is part of reading any history of science.

**Weighing a claim.** The Mars lake shows how a single bright radar echo can be read in more than one way. Until a
second method, perhaps seismic waves or drilling, gives the same answer, the honest position is "not yet known".

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) What was Karl Jansky trying to find, and what did he find instead?

??? note "Answer"
    The causes of static that might spoil trans-Atlantic radio telephone calls. Besides thunderstorms, he found a faint
    steady hiss of radio waves from the Milky Way, strongest towards Sagittarius.

**2.** (beginner) Why was the first pulsar nicknamed LGM-1?

??? note "Answer"
    Its pulses were so regular, every 1.337 seconds, that the team half-jokingly called it "little green men". It turned
    out to be a spinning neutron star.

**3.** (beginner) Which two countries host the Square Kilometre Array's telescopes?

??? note "Answer"
    South Africa (SKA-Mid, in the Karoo) and Australia (SKA-Low, in Western Australia).

**4.** (intermediate) Why did Jansky's hiss return every 23 hours 56 minutes and not every 24 hours? How often would
it return for a radio telescope on Mars?

??? note "Answer"
    It came from the stars, and the Earth turns once against the stars in 23 h 56 min, a sidereal day. Mars turns
    once against the stars in 24.6229 h, so it would return every 24 h 37 min.

**5.** (intermediate) Why do radio telescopes need to be so much bigger than optical ones, or spread out in arrays?

??? note "Answer"
    Sharpness depends on wavelength divided by width. A 21 cm radio wave is about 400,000 times longer than visible
    light, so a radio dish must be that much wider, or its dishes that much farther apart, to see the same detail.

**6.** (intermediate) Give one reason for and one reason against the idea of a lake under Mars's south polar ice.

??? note "Answer"
    For: MARSIS saw a very bright echo 1.5 km down, which its team read as salty liquid water. Against: a 2022 study
    suggested the echo could come from volcanic rock; another found that brine may be soaked between grains rather
    than forming a lake.

**7.** (advanced) The Hooker telescope, 2.5 m wide, makes out 0.055″ in visible light of 550 nm. How wide would a
single radio dish working at 21.1 cm need to be to see as sharply?

??? note "Answer"
    The width must grow by the same factor as the wavelength: $0.211 / (550 \times 10^{-9}) = 383{,}600$. So
    $2.5 \times 383{,}600 = 959{,}000$ m, about **960 km**. That is why radio astronomers build arrays.

**8.** (expert) Suppose one dish on Mars and one on the Earth, 100 million km apart, could be combined at 1.3 mm.
What is the smallest angle they could make out, and how big a detail is that on the Andromeda Galaxy, 2.5 million
light-years away? (One light-year is $9.461 \times 10^{15}$ m.)

??? note "Answer"
    Angle: $1.22 \times 0.0013 / (1 \times 10^{11}) = 1.59 \times 10^{-14}$ radians, or about $3.3 \times 10^{-9}$″.
    Distance to Andromeda: $2.5 \times 10^{6} \times 9.461 \times 10^{15} = 2.37 \times 10^{22}$ m. Detail:
    $1.59 \times 10^{-14} \times 2.37 \times 10^{22} = 3.8 \times 10^{8}$ m, about **380,000 km**, close to the
    distance from the Earth to the Moon. (Keeping such an array in step would be far beyond today's technology.)

## Summary

- Jansky heard radio waves from the Milky Way in 1932; Reber built the first radio dish in 1937 and mapped the radio
  sky.
- Wartime radar led Hey to radio waves from the Sun, and Pawsey, Payne-Scott and McCready made the first radio
  interferometer observation in Sydney in 1946.
- The 21 cm hydrogen line, predicted in 1944 and found in 1951, showed the Milky Way's spiral arms.
- Big dishes and arrays in England, Australia, Puerto Rico, Russia, India, China and Chile found quasars, pulsars and
  much more; Bell's pulsar of 1967 led to a Nobel Prize that left her out.
- Penzias and Wilson found the 2.725 K afterglow of the Big Bang in 1964, at the same place where Jansky worked.
- The Event Horizon Telescope imaged black holes in 2019 and 2022, and the SKA is being built in South Africa and
  Australia.
- Radio carries every message from Mars, and the MARSIS radar has found possible salty water under the south polar
  ice, a claim still debated.
