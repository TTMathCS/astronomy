# 9. Byzantium and medieval Europe

*For most of the Middle Ages, Latin Europe was a quiet corner of astronomy. Its scholars had lost most Greek books and
could not read the rest. What kept astronomy alive there was a church problem: finding the date of Easter. Byzantium,
the Greek-speaking eastern half of the Roman Empire, kept the Greek books and later borrowed from Persia. Then, from
the 11th century, Arabic science poured into Latin through Spain, the new universities made astronomy a required
subject, and clockmakers and printers carried it further. This chapter follows that road from Bede's monastery to the
calendar reform of 1582, and clears up one popular myth on the way.*

## 9.1 Easter and the calendar problem { #computus }

Easter is tied to the Moon. It falls at the time of the Jewish Passover, which is set by a lunar calendar, while the
Roman world used a solar one. In 325 the Council of Nicaea decided that all churches should keep Easter on the same
day. The rule that came to be used is:

> Easter is the first Sunday after the first full moon on or after 21 March.

The 21st of March stood for the spring equinox. To use the rule years ahead you need to know when full moons will
fall, so the Church needed a lunar table that fits the solar calendar. The answer came from Alexandria: the 19-year
cycle in which 235 lunar months almost exactly equal 19 years (chapter 7, section 7.3). Working out Easter this way
was called the **computus**.

Different churches used different tables, and they did not always agree. In about 525 a monk in Rome, **Dionysius
Exiguus**, extended the Alexandrian table and, in doing so, began counting years from the birth of Christ, the system
we still use (AD). In Britain and Ireland, Irish and Roman ways of finding Easter clashed until the **Synod of Whitby**
in 664 decided for the Roman way in northern England.

For centuries, a monastery that wanted to keep the feasts on the right day needed someone who understood the motions of
the Sun and Moon.

## 9.2 Bede, a monk who reckoned time { #bede }

**Bede** (672/673 to 735) spent nearly all his life in the twin monastery of Monkwearmouth and Jarrow in the north of
England. In 725 he wrote ***The Reckoning of Time***, a textbook of the computus. In it:

- He explained that the Earth is a **sphere**, and that this is why the length of daylight changes with the seasons and
  from place to place.
- He linked the **tides** to the Moon: the twice-daily tides follow the Moon's daily motion, and the monthly cycle of
  spring and neap tides follows its phases.
- He used Dionysius's counting from the birth of Christ, and his widely read history of the English Church helped make
  AD dating normal in Europe.

Late in the 9th century Notker, a monk at St Gallen in Switzerland, wrote that God had made Bede "rise from the West as a new Sun to
illuminate the whole Earth".

## 9.3 The myth of the flat Earth { #flat }

Many people today believe that in the Middle Ages everyone thought the Earth was flat, and that Columbus had to prove
it round. This is false. Educated people in medieval Europe knew the Earth is a sphere, from Greek books and from
writers like Bede, and the standard university textbook (section 9.7) taught it. The historian Jeffrey Burton Russell
put it strongly: "with extraordinary few exceptions no educated person in the history of Western Civilization from
the third century B.C. onward believed that the Earth was flat".

The few exceptions were two early Christian writers, **Lactantius** (around 300) and **Cosmas Indicopleustes** (6th
century), who had little influence on astronomy.

So where did the myth come from? Mostly from the 19th century:

- In 1828 **Washington Irving** published a romantic life of Columbus, with an invented scene in which churchmen object
  that the Earth is not round. Many readers took it as history.
- In 1874 **John William Draper** and in 1896 **Andrew Dickson White** wrote books that described a long war between
  science and religion, and used the flat Earth as an example.

The real problem with Columbus's plan was **distance**. He made the Earth too small and Asia too wide, and reckoned
Japan to be only about 5,000 km to the west; the true figure is about 20,000 km. He survived only because the Americas
were in the way.

## 9.4 Byzantium keeps the Greek books { #byzantium }

The eastern Roman Empire, with its capital at Constantinople, lasted until 1453. Its scholars read Greek, so Ptolemy
and the other Greek astronomers were never lost there.

- **John Philoponus** (about 490 to 570, Alexandria) wrote the oldest surviving Greek treatise on the astrolabe. He also
  argued against Aristotle that the heavens are made of the same kind of stuff as the Earth and that the stars are
  not divine, and he proposed an early idea of **impetus**, a force given to a thrown object that keeps it moving.
  Galileo later cited him.
- **Gregory Chioniades** (around 1300) travelled to Persia, studied in Tabriz under Shams al-Din al-Bukhari, a pupil
  who had worked at Maragha, and in 1295–1296 served as Orthodox bishop there. He translated Persian and Arabic astronomical
  tables into Greek. His Greek books include a description of the **Tusi couple** (chapter 8, section 8.8).
- **Nikephoros Gregoras** (14th century) predicted the total solar eclipse of 16 July 1330 using Ptolemy's tables. In
  1326 he proposed a reform of the calendar; the emperor refused it for fear of unrest. More than 250 years later Rome
  carried out a reform on almost the same lines (section 9.10).

Greek books and Greek scholars also travelled west. The most important for astronomy was **Cardinal Bessarion**, a
native Greek speaker in the service of the Pope, who set out to spread the Greek classics in Europe and owned the
largest private library in Europe. He asked two Viennese astronomers to make a new Latin summary of the *Almagest* (section 9.9).

## 9.5 What European monks saw { #skies }

European chronicles are thinner than Chinese ones, but they have some fine moments.

**The star of 1006.** The monks of the Abbey of St Gallen, in Switzerland, recorded the supernova of 1006 (chapter 4,
section 4.7, and chapter 8, section 8.5). At 47.5° north they were the most northerly people known to have seen it, and
it never rose far above their southern horizon (section 9.12):

> In a wonderful manner this was sometimes contracted, sometimes diffused, and moreover sometimes extinguished ... It
> was seen likewise for three months in the inmost limits of the south, beyond all the constellations which are seen
> in the sky.

**The comet of 1066.** In 1066 Halley's Comet was seen in England. That autumn King Harold was killed at the
Battle of Hastings and William of Normandy took the throne. The **Bayeux Tapestry**, which tells the story, shows the
comet, with a caption that calls it a star. A monk named Eilmer of Malmesbury may have seen the same comet twice, in 989 and
in 1066.

**The Moon of 1178.** The chronicler **Gervase of Canterbury** wrote that on 18 June 1178, shortly after sunset, five
men saw "the upper horn [of the Moon] split in two", and fire and sparks spring from it. In 1976 the geologist Jack Hartung suggested
they had seen the impact that made the young lunar crater **Giordano Bruno**, which is in about the right place. Others
doubt it: an impact that size would have thrown about 10 million tonnes of debris, causing a week of
spectacular meteors on Earth, and no record anywhere mentions one. A meteor exploding in the line of sight to the Moon
is a likelier answer.

??? note "Sources"
    - St Gallen, 47.5° north, the most northerly sighting, and the quotation from the *Annales Sangallenses maiores*:
      Wikipedia, [SN 1006](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/SN_1006) (weaker).
    - The comet of 1066, Harold and William, the Bayeux Tapestry, and Eilmer of Malmesbury: Wikipedia,
      [Halley's Comet](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Halley%27s_Comet) (weaker).
    - Gervase, the five witnesses and the quotation, the Giordano Bruno idea, the missing meteor storm and the exploding

      [Giordano Bruno (crater)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Giordano_Bruno_(crater)) (weaker).

## 9.6 Arabic books in Latin { #translations }

**Gerbert.** In 967 a monk from France, **Gerbert of Aurillac** (about 946 to 1003), was sent to study in
Catalonia, close to Muslim Spain, to learn mathematics and something of Arabic learning. He brought back to Latin Europe the **abacus**, the **armillary sphere** and
the Indian–Arabic digits (probably without the zero), and he may have written one of the first Latin descriptions of
the astrolabe. In 999 he became Pope Sylvester II.

**Hermann of Reichenau** (1013 to 1054), a monk on Reichenau Island in southern Germany known as Hermann the Cripple,
wrote Latin instructions for building an astrolabe (figure).

**Toledo.** In 1085 Toledo, a great city of Muslim Spain, was taken by Christian Castile. From the 1120s, under Archbishop Raymond, teams of Mozarab Christians, Jewish scholars and monks turned Arabic books into Latin.
The best-known translator was **Gerard of Cremona** (about 1114 to 1187), an Italian who went to Toledo to learn
Arabic just so he could read the *Almagest*. His Latin version, made around 1175, was the one Europe used until the
Renaissance. Earlier, in England, **Adelard of Bath** had translated al-Khwarizmi's tables and Euclid's *Elements* from
Arabic (chapter 8, section 8.2).

**The Alfonsine Tables.** In the 13th century King **Alfonso X** of Castile gathered astronomers in Toledo, among them
the Jewish scholars Yehuda ben Moshe and Isaac ibn Sid, to update the older Toledan tables. Their tables start from
1 June 1252, Alfonso's coronation, and became the most popular tables in Europe, updated for three hundred years. Copernicus
bought a copy as a student in Kraków and had it bound in wood and leather.

## 9.7 The universities and a turning Earth { #universities }

From about 1200, the new universities taught the liberal arts. Four of them, the **quadrivium**, were mathematical:
arithmetic, geometry, music and astronomy. So students of the arts met astronomy.

Their textbook was ***On the Sphere*** by **John of Sacrobosco**, written in Paris around 1230: a short, clear account of
the round Earth, the celestial sphere and the circles of the sky. It was still being taught centuries later; after its
first printing in 1472 at least 84 editions followed in two hundred years. Sacrobosco also pointed out the faults of the
Julian calendar and proposed a fix much like the one adopted in 1582.

**1277.** In 1277 the Bishop of Paris, Étienne Tempier, condemned a list of 219 teachings, many drawn from Aristotle
and his followers. Some of them limited what God could do. Once those were banned, things Aristotle had called
impossible, such as a vacuum, had to be treated as at least possible. Historians
argue about the effect. The physicist and historian Pierre Duhem argued that it freed scholars from Aristotle and opened
the way to modern science; others think that claim is far too strong. Most agree it let scholars at least consider ideas
that Aristotle had ruled out.

**Could the Earth turn?** At Paris, **Jean Buridan** (about 1301 to about 1360) developed the idea of impetus, a first
step towards our idea of inertia. **Nicole Oresme** (about 1320 to 1382), who studied at Paris with Buridan and later became Bishop of Lisieux,
wrote in French for King Charles V a careful discussion of whether the Earth spins daily (1377). He showed that none of
the arguments for a fixed Earth is conclusive: all the motions astronomers compute "would appear exactly the same" if
the Earth turned and the heavens stood still, and a Bible passage about the Sun moving simply "conforms to the
customary usage of popular speech". Then he concluded: "everyone maintains, and I think myself, that the heavens do move
and not the Earth." In the next century **Nicholas of Cusa** (1401 to 1464) went further and wrote that the Earth is a
star like the others, not at rest, and not the centre of the universe. Neither of them built an astronomy on these
ideas. That was left to Copernicus (chapter 13).

## 9.8 Clocks and astrolabes { #clocks }

The first all-mechanical clocks were built in Europe around 1300 (China had water-driven clocks with an escapement
much earlier, chapter 4, section 4.9). Almost at once, clockmakers used them to show the sky.

- **Richard of Wallingford** (1292 to 1336), the son of a blacksmith, became abbot of St Albans in England in 1327 and
  designed for the abbey an astronomical clock that showed the phases of the Moon and even the height of the tide at
  London Bridge. He also made the **Albion**, a
  calculating instrument that could predict eclipses without tables. He did all this while suffering from a disease
  then thought to be leprosy.
- **Giovanni Dondi** (about 1330 to 1388) spent sixteen years, from 1348 to 1364, building in Padua his **Astrarium**,
  a clock that also showed the motions of the Sun, Moon and planets.
- The **astronomical clock of Prague** was first recorded on 9 October 1410, built by the clockmaker Mikuláš of Kadaň
  and the university astronomer Jan Šindel. It still runs in the Old Town Square, though much of its mechanism has been
  renewed over the centuries.

In 1391 the poet **Geoffrey Chaucer** wrote *A Treatise on the Astrolabe* for his ten-year-old son Lewis. It is written
in English, not Latin, and is called the oldest work in English about an elaborate scientific instrument.

## 9.9 Vienna, Nuremberg and the printing press { #printing }

In Vienna, **Georg von Peuerbach** (born about 1423, died 1461) gave lectures on the planets that became famous.
In 1460 Cardinal Bessarion came to Vienna and asked him for a new Latin summary of the *Almagest*, made from the Greek.
Peuerbach died the next year with only six of its thirteen books done, and begged his student to finish it.

That student was **Johannes Müller**, called **Regiomontanus** (1436 to 1476). He travelled in Italy with Bessarion,
copying Greek manuscripts, and finished the *Epitome of the Almagest*. In 1471 he moved to Nuremberg and set up the
world's first printing press for science. In 1472 he printed Peuerbach's planet lectures, the first printed astronomy
textbook. His printed tables of the positions of the Sun, Moon and planets, the *Ephemerides*, were used by Columbus and
Vespucci at sea to estimate longitude.

When the *Epitome* was printed in Venice, one passage in it caught the eye of a student at Bologna. His name was Nicolaus
Copernicus.

## 9.10 Ten days that never happened { #gregorian }

The Julian calendar, set up by Julius Caesar in 46 BC with the help of the astronomer Sosigenes of Alexandria, has a
leap year every four years, so its average year is 365.25 days. The real year of the seasons is about 365.2422 days,
11 minutes shorter. The calendar gains a day every 128 years, and so the spring equinox crept earlier and earlier:
by the 16th century it fell around 11 March, ten days before the date the Easter rule assumed.

Gregoras and Sacrobosco had already seen the problem. The solution adopted came from an Italian doctor, **Aloysius
Lilius**, and was defended in an 800-page book by the astronomer **Christopher Clavius**. In his decree *Inter
gravissimas* of 24 February 1582, Pope **Gregory XIII** ordered two changes:

1. **Ten days were dropped.** Thursday 4 October 1582 was followed by Friday 15 October 1582. The days of the week ran on
   unbroken.
2. **Fewer leap years.** A century year, such as 1700, 1800 and 1900, is a leap year only if it divides by 400 (so 1600
   and 2000 were). That leaves 97 leap years in every 400, an average year of 365.2425 days.

Philip II of Spain, who also ruled Portugal and much of Italy, ordered the change in September 1582. Protestant and
Orthodox countries came later: Britain and its colonies in 1752,
Greece, the last in Europe, in 1923 for civil purposes. Many Orthodox churches still keep the Julian calendar for their
feasts, which is why their Christmas falls on 7 January.

## 9.11 Mars in the week, and Europe at Mars { #mars }

**Mars in the week.** The seven-day week of the Roman world named each day after one of the seven moving lights of the
sky. Tuesday was the day of Mars, *dies Martis* in Latin, which is still *mardi* in French and *martes* in Spanish.
English took the Germanic god Tiw as a match for Mars: Tīwesdæg, Tuesday.

**Europe at Mars.** The European Space Agency's **Mars Express** was launched on 2 June 2003, when Mars and Earth were
closer than they had been in about 60,000 years, and went into orbit around Mars on 25 December 2003. More than twenty
years later it is still working. Its radar, MARSIS, sees under the ice: in 2018 its team reported a bright radar echo
from about 1.5 km beneath the south polar cap, a patch some 20 km wide that they read as a lake of liquid water. A
2022 study argued that very salty water, brine, is the best explanation of the echo.

It carried a small British lander, **Beagle 2**, which was due to land on Christmas Day 2003 and was never heard from. In
January 2015 NASA's Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter found it on the surface: it had landed safely, but two of its four
solar panels had not unfolded and they blocked its antenna.

## 9.12 Working it out { #working }

**How fast did the calendar drift?** The Julian year is 365.25 days and the tropical year about 365.24219 days:

$$365.25 - 365.24219 = 0.00781 \text{ days a year} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad \frac{1}{0.00781} \approx 128 \text{ years per day}.$$

From 325 to 1582 is 1,257 years: $1257 / 128 \approx 9.8$, so about **10 days**, which is what Gregory dropped. The
Gregorian year of 365.2425 days is still too long, but only by $0.00031$ days a year, one day in about 3,200 years.

**The Moon in the Easter tables.** The 19-year cycle sets 235 lunar months equal to 19 Julian years:

| | Days |
|---|---|
| 19 Julian years, $19 \times 365.25$ | 6,939.75 |
| 235 lunar months, $235 \times 29.530589$ | 6,939.69 |
| Difference | 0.06 |

So the table's full moons slip by about 0.06 days every 19 years, one day in about 310 years. By the 16th century the
Easter tables' "full moon" was several days off the real one, and the Gregorian reform corrected the Moon as well.

**How high did the star of 1006 rise?** A star's highest altitude, when it crosses the south, is
$90^\circ - \text{latitude} + \text{declination}$. In 1006 the supernova's declination was about $-37.6^\circ$ (it is
$-42^\circ$ today; precession has moved it).

| Place | Latitude | Highest altitude |
|---|---|---|
| Cairo (Ali ibn Ridwan) | 30.0° N | 22.4° |
| Baghdad | 33.3° N | 19.1° |
| St Gallen | 47.5° N | 4.9° |

At St Gallen the star crept along the southern skyline, about ten times the width of the full Moon above it: "the
inmost limits of the south".

**A calendar for Mars.** A Mars year of seasons lasts about 668.59 Martian days (sols). A calendar of whole sols needs leap days, just
like ours:

| | Mars | Earth |
|---|---|---|
| Year of the seasons | 668.594 sols | 365.2422 days |
| Normal year | 668 sols | 365 days |
| Simple leap rule | 669 sols in 3 years out of 5 → 668.6 | 366 days in 1 year out of 4 → 365.25 |
| Simple rule too long by | 0.006 sols a year: 1 sol in about 170 years | 0.0078 days a year: 1 day in 128 years |

See the physics lesson [A calendar for Mars](https://physics.akadian.ca/topics/spacetime/level-4/06-a-calendar-for-mars.html).

## 9.13 How should we judge medieval European astronomy? { #assessment }

**Not a dark age, but a slow one.** Between 500 and 1100, Latin Europe produced little new astronomy compared with
Baghdad, Cairo, India or China. But astronomy was not forgotten. The computus kept the Sun and Moon in every monastery's
school; Bede explained the round Earth and the tides; and monks recorded what they saw.

**A borrower.** Almost everything that revived astronomy in Europe came from outside: Greek books through Byzantium and
Arabic ones through Spain, with Arabic star names, numerals, the astrolabe and the tables. Europe's own strength, from
the 12th century on, was its **institutions**: universities with a fixed curriculum, and later the printing press, which
put the same tables and textbooks into thousands of hands.

**Ideas without astronomy.** Buridan, Oresme and Cusa questioned Aristotle's physics and allowed a turning Earth, but
none of them built a working astronomy on it. Whether their ideas led to Copernicus and Galileo, or only prepared the
ground, is still argued by historians.

**The myth.** The story of a flat-Earth Middle Ages tells us more about the 19th century, and its wish to set science
against religion, than about the Middle Ages.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) What is the rule for the date of Easter, and why did it need astronomy?

??? note "Answer"
    Easter is the first Sunday after the first full moon on or after 21 March. To find it years ahead you need to
    predict full moons, so you need a table that fits the Moon's months to the solar calendar.

**2.** (beginner) Name two things Bede explained in *The Reckoning of Time*.

??? note "Answer"
    Any two of: the Earth is a sphere, which is why daylight changes with season and place; the tides follow the Moon
    (twice daily, and spring and neap tides through the month); how to work out Easter.

**3.** (beginner) Did medieval scholars think the Earth was flat? Where does the idea come from?

??? note "Answer"
    No. Educated people knew it was a sphere, and the university textbook taught it. The myth comes mostly from 19th-century
    writers: Washington Irving's life of Columbus (1828), and the books of Draper (1874) and White (1896).

**4.** (intermediate) How did the *Almagest* reach Latin Europe?

??? note "Answer"
    Mainly through Arabic: Gerard of Cremona went to Toledo, learned Arabic and translated it into Latin around 1175.
    Later, Greek copies from Byzantium, through Bessarion, led to Peuerbach and Regiomontanus's *Epitome*.

**5.** (intermediate) What did Oresme argue about the Earth's rotation, and what did he finally conclude?

??? note "Answer"
    He showed that no argument proves the Earth stands still: all the motions of the sky would look the same if the
    Earth turned, and the Bible speaks in everyday language. Even so he concluded that the heavens move and not the
    Earth.

**6.** (intermediate) What two changes did the Gregorian reform make?

??? note "Answer"
    It dropped ten days (4 October 1582 was followed by 15 October), and it removed three leap years every 400 years:
    century years are leap years only if they divide by 400.

**7.** (advanced) Was 1900 a leap year? Was 2000? How many days long is the average Gregorian year?

??? note "Answer"
    1900 was not (it divides by 100 but not by 400); 2000 was (it divides by 400). There are 97 leap years in 400 years,
    so the average year is $365 + 97/400 = 365.2425$ days.

**8.** (expert) Toledo is at about 39.9° N. What was the highest altitude of the star of 1006 seen from there? A Mars
calendar has 668 sols with a leap sol in 3 years out of 5. The Mars year of the seasons is 668.594 sols. After how many
Mars years is the calendar a whole sol out, and how many Earth years is that?

??? note "Answer"
    $90 - 39.9 + (-37.6) = 12.5^\circ$. On Mars, the rule gives an average of 668.6 sols, too long by 0.006 sols a
    year, so it is a whole sol out after about $1/0.006 \approx 170$ Mars years, about $170 \times 1.881 \approx 320$
    Earth years. A Gregorian-style fix, dropping a leap sol now and then, would cure it.

## Summary

- The date of Easter, tied to the Moon, kept astronomy alive in the Church; the 19-year cycle came from Alexandria, and
  Dionysius's table of 525 gave us AD dates.
- Bede (725) taught the round Earth, the changing daylight and the Moon's control of the tides.
- The flat-Earth Middle Ages is a 19th-century myth.
- Byzantium kept the Greek books, borrowed tables and the Tusi couple from Persia, and passed Ptolemy to the West
  through scholars such as Bessarion.
- Monks recorded the star of 1006 at St Gallen, Halley's comet of 1066, and a strange Moon in 1178.
- Gerbert and Hermann brought the astrolabe; in Toledo, Gerard of Cremona translated the *Almagest* from Arabic; the
  Alfonsine Tables became Europe's standard.
- Universities taught Sacrobosco's *Sphere*; Oresme and Cusa allowed that the Earth might turn but did not follow it up.
- Astronomical clocks, Chaucer's astrolabe book and Regiomontanus's printing press spread astronomy further.
- In 1582 the Gregorian reform dropped ten days and changed the leap-year rule.
- Mars gave its name to Tuesday; Europe's Mars Express has orbited Mars since 2003.
