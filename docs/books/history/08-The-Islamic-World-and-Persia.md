# 8. The Islamic world and Persia

*From the 8th century to the 16th, the most active astronomy in the world was written in Arabic and Persian. It began
in Baghdad, where Indian, Persian and Greek books were translated and compared. It went on in observatories from Cairo
to Samarkand, where astronomers measured the year to within seconds, mapped the stars again for the first time since
Ptolemy, and found Ptolemy's models wanting. Some of their new models reappear, line for line, in Copernicus. This
chapter tells that story, and also the arguments about it: how much was translation and how much invention, and why
these astronomers, for all their criticism, kept the Earth at the centre.*

## 8.1 Astronomy for every day { #why }

Islam gave astronomy work to do every day.

- **Prayer times.** The five daily prayers are set by the Sun: dawn, noon, afternoon, sunset and nightfall. Working
  them out for each city and each day of the year is a problem in spherical astronomy.
- **The qibla.** Prayer faces the Kaaba in Mecca. From a distant city that direction is found on the curved Earth, not on
  a flat map, which needs spherical trigonometry.
- **The calendar.** The Islamic calendar is purely lunar. Traditionally each month begins at sunset on the day the new
  crescent, the *hilal*, is first seen, and astronomers worked hard on predicting when that would be.

Large mosques came to employ a timekeeper, the **muwaqqit**. Astrolabes were made in great numbers, many of them chiefly
as aids to finding the qibla. Religion did not only tolerate astronomy here; it needed it.

## 8.2 Baghdad gathers the books { #baghdad }

In Baghdad, the capital of the Abbasid caliphs, astronomers started not with the Greeks but with **India and Persia**.

- After 770, with the help of Indian astronomers at the court of the caliph al-Mansur, **al-Fazari** and **Ya'qub ibn
  Tariq** turned an Indian astronomical work into the *Zij al-Sindhind*. A *zij* is a handbook of astronomical tables
  with rules for using them, and this was the model for hundreds more.
- The *Zij al-Shah* came from tables compiled in Sasanian Persia.
- **Al-Khwarizmi** (about 780 to 850) wrote his own *Zij al-Sindhind*, about 37 chapters and 116 tables, including a
  table of sines. It reached Europe in a Latin translation, probably by Adelard of Bath in 1126. His book on Indian
  numerals, in Latin, gave Europe the decimal place-value system, and his Latinised name, *Algoritmi*, gave us the word
  "algorithm".

Then came the Greek books. Under the caliph **al-Ma'mun** (ruled 813 to 833) Ptolemy's *Almagest* was translated into
Arabic; one story even says al-Ma'mun asked the Byzantine emperor for a copy as a condition of peace. Much of
this work is linked with the **House of Wisdom** in Baghdad. How grand that institution really was is debated: the
historian Dimitri Gutas argues that the name may be a later label for a wider scholarly community rather than a single
academy.

The translators came from many faiths. **Thabit ibn Qurra** (about 836 to 901) belonged to the Sabians of Harran, a
star-worshipping religion, and spoke Syriac. Copernicus quotes his sidereal year of 365 days 6 hours 9 minutes 12
seconds, only about 2 seconds off.

**How "sine" got its name.** The Sanskrit word for the half-chord was *jya* or *jiva*, "bowstring" (chapter 5).
Arabic writers copied it as *jiba*, a word with no meaning in Arabic, written with just the consonants *j-b*. Later
readers took *j-b* as *jayb*, "fold" or "pocket". When the Arabic books of al-Khwarizmi and al-Battani were translated
into Latin in the 12th century, *jayb* became the Latin *sinus*, "fold", and so our "sine".

## 8.3 Measuring the Earth and the year { #measuring }

**Al-Ma'mun's astronomers.** Al-Ma'mun built an observatory in Baghdad, and others followed in other cities, where his
astronomers checked Ptolemy's numbers. Around 830 they also measured the Earth. Teams with sighting pegs paced out a
straight north–south line across a flat desert until the stars showed one degree of change in latitude; one team,
including Ali ibn Isa al-Asturlabi, worked on the **plain of Sinjar** in today's Iraq. The reports differ: 56, 56¼ or
56⅔ Arabic miles per degree, and another account has a measurement between Palmyra and Raqqa in Syria. The astronomer
al-Farghani gave the Earth's circumference as 20,400 miles, that is $360 \times 56\tfrac{2}{3}$. How good was it? The
Arabic mile was 4,000 cubits, but there were several cubits, so the mile was somewhere between about 1.8 and 2.2 km
(section 8.12).

**Al-Battani** (before 858 to 929), known in Latin as Albategnius, worked at Raqqa on the Euphrates from 877 to about
918. He measured:

- the tropical year as **365 days 5 hours 46 minutes 24 seconds**, about 2 minutes 21 seconds short;
- the tilt of the Earth's axis as **23°35′**, which is 23.58°. Today it is 23.44°, but the tilt shrinks by about
  0.013° a century, so in 900 it really was about 23.58°: he had it almost exactly;
- and the Sun's **apogee**, the point where it looks smallest, which had moved by 16°47′ since Ptolemy. Ptolemy had
  thought it fixed.

His book was translated into Latin, and Copernicus cites him 23 times.

**Ibn Yunus** (about 950 to 1009) worked at an observatory near Cairo for the Fatimid caliphs and wrote the *Hakimi Zij*. He
recorded the solar eclipses of 993 and 1004 and the lunar eclipses of 1001 and 1002 so carefully that in the 18th and
19th centuries Richard Dunthorne and Simon Newcomb used them to measure how the Moon's motion is slowly speeding up.

## 8.4 Stars, names and astrolabes { #stars }

**Al-Sufi's star book.** **Abd al-Rahman al-Sufi** (903 to 986), at the Buyid court in Isfahan, checked Ptolemy's star
catalogue against the sky and in 964 finished the *Book of Fixed Stars*, with drawings of each constellation as seen on
the sky and as seen on a globe. In it is the earliest known mention of the **Andromeda galaxy**, a "small cloud". It is
sometimes said he also described the Large Magellanic Cloud, but that seems to be a misreading of a note about stars
south of Canopus that he says he did not see.

**Names in our sky.** Many bright stars still carry Arabic names:

| Star | Arabic | Meaning |
|---|---|---|
| Aldebaran | *al-Dabaran* | the follower (of the Pleiades) |
| Altair | *al-Ta'ir* | the flying (eagle) |
| Betelgeuse | *Yad al-Jawza'* | hand of al-Jawza' (Orion) |
| Deneb | *Dhanab al-Dajajah* | tail of the hen |
| Rigel | *Rijl* | foot |

**An astrolabe maker of Aleppo.** In the 10th century a woman known as **al-'Ijliyya**, daughter of an astrolabe maker
called al-'Ijli, made astrolabes for Sayf al-Dawla, the first emir of Aleppo (ruled 944 to 967). She and her father had
trained under an astrolabe maker in Baghdad. Popular books often call her "Mariam al-Asturlabiyya", but the name Mariam
is not in the one early source about her. An asteroid, 7060 Al-'Ijliya, is named after her.

## 8.5 Guest stars in Cairo and Baghdad { #guests }

**The star of 1006.** In spring 1006 a very bright new star appeared low in the southern sky (chapter 4,
section 4.7). In Cairo, the physician and astrologer **Ali ibn Ridwan** (about 988 to 1061) described it in a commentary
on Ptolemy's astrology: a large round body "2½ to 3 times as large as Venus", shining with a little more than a quarter
of the Moon's light. He saw it, as everyone did, low on the southern horizon.

**The star of 1054.** Only one Arabic account of the 1054 star is known, found by historians in 1978. It comes from
**Ibn Butlan**, a Christian doctor of Baghdad, quoted in a 13th-century book of biographies of physicians: "a spectacular
star appeared in Gemini" in the year 446 of the Islamic calendar (12 April 1054 to 1 April 1055), and he linked it, as an
astrologer would, to epidemics in Constantinople and Egypt. By chance, Ibn Butlan and Ali ibn Ridwan were rivals who
attacked each other in a famous exchange of pamphlets.

No Arabic report of the fainter guest star of 1181 has been found.

## 8.6 Al-Biruni measures the Earth from a hill { #biruni }

**Abu Rayhan al-Biruni** (973 to about 1050), from Khwarazm in Central Asia, was one of the great scholars of any age.
From 1017 he lived at Ghazni, in today's Afghanistan, at the court of Mahmud of Ghazni, and went with Mahmud's campaigns
into India. Out of this came his *India* (about 1030), a careful and fair-minded description of Indian religion,
customs and astronomy, including Aryabhata's work.

**A moving Earth?** Al-Biruni took seriously the Indian idea that the Earth turns (chapter 5). He wrote that it "does in
no way impair the value of astronomy, as all appearances of an astronomic character can quite as well be explained
according to this theory as to the other". He says he settled the question in a book on astronomy, but that book is
lost.

**The Earth from a hill.** Eratosthenes needed two cities and the Sun. Al-Biruni found a way to measure the Earth alone
from one place. At Nandana, in today's Pakistan, he first measured the height of a hill. Then from its top he measured
how far the horizon lay below the level, the **dip**.

The line of sight to the horizon just touches the Earth, at a right angle to the radius, so a right triangle links the
height, the radius and the dip (section 8.12). He used a dip of 34 minutes of arc and gave the radius as 12,803,337
cubits, about **6,340 km** with the cubit some historians use; the true mean radius is 6,371 km. But nobody knows his
cubit exactly, and he did not know that air bends light near the horizon (refraction), which changes the dip. Al-Biruni
himself did not think his method beat the older one, and he accepted the value of al-Ma'mun's astronomers. His great
handbook, the *Mas'udi Canon* (1037), gave coordinates for some six hundred places.

## 8.7 Doubts about Ptolemy { #doubts }

By the 11th century, astronomers in the Islamic world knew Ptolemy well enough to see the problems.

**Ibn al-Haytham** (about 965 to about 1040), born in Basra and working in Cairo, is best known for his great book on
optics. Between 1025 and 1028 he wrote *Doubts concerning Ptolemy*, listing contradictions in Ptolemy's books. His main
target was the **equant** (chapter 7, section 7.8): a real heavenly body, he argued, cannot move uniformly about an
imaginary point that is not the centre of its circle. Ptolemy's models predicted well, but they could not be a true
picture of the heavens.

**In al-Andalus.** In Toledo, in Muslim Spain, **al-Zarqali** (died 1100), known in Latin as Azarquiel, built
instruments, including a simpler astrolabe used by navigators until the 16th century. He was the first to show that the Sun's apogee moves against the
stars, and he designed an **equatorium**, a disc instrument that worked out planetary positions from Ptolemy's models
without long calculation. Toledo later passed to Christian Castile and became a great centre for translating Arabic
science into Latin (chapter 9).

## 8.8 Maragha: an observatory for a Mongol khan { #maragha }

In 1258 the Mongols under **Hulagu**, grandson of Genghis Khan, sacked Baghdad. Its libraries were destroyed; the story
goes that the Tigris ran black with ink. Yet within a year the same Hulagu was paying for the greatest observatory yet
built.

**Nasir al-Din al-Tusi** (1201 to 1274), from Tus in north-east Iran, had worked for the Nizari Ismailis at the
mountain fortress of Alamut until the Mongols took it in 1256. It is widely believed, though disputed, that he was with
Hulagu at the fall of Baghdad. He persuaded Hulagu, who was interested in astrology, that new tables were needed, and
in **1259** building began at **Maragha**, in north-west Iran.

- It had a large library and instruments including a great **mural quadrant** and an **armillary sphere**, built under
  the engineer **al-Urdi** of Damascus.
- Its staff came from far apart: al-Urdi from Damascus, Qutb al-Din al-Shirazi, Muhyi al-Din al-Maghribi, and a
  **Chinese astronomer**, Fao Munji, who brought Chinese methods.
- After about twelve years of work it produced the *Zij-i Ilkhani*, the Ilkhanid tables.
- Its knowledge travelled east as well: in 1267 the Persian astronomer Jamal al-Din, who had worked at Maragha, took
  instruments to Kublai Khan in China (chapter 4, section 4.10).

**The Tusi couple.** Al-Tusi's best-known idea solves a puzzle: how to make straight-line motion out of circles. Roll a
circle inside another circle twice its size. A point on the small circle's rim then moves back and forth along a
straight line.

With this device, first described in 1247, al-Tusi and later astronomers could produce the varying distances that
Ptolemy had needed the equant for, using only uniform circular motions. Al-Urdi found another way to replace the
equant, now called the **Urdi lemma**. Al-Tusi also wrote that the Milky Way is made of a great many small stars packed
close together, three and a half centuries before Galileo saw them through a telescope.

In time the observatory fell into ruin, through earthquakes and lack of money.

## 8.9 Ibn al-Shatir of Damascus { #shatir }

**Ibn al-Shatir** (1304 to 1375) was the timekeeper (muwaqqit) of the Umayyad Mosque in Damascus. In 1371/72 he built a
great sundial for its minaret. In his book on planetary theory he went further than anyone before him in removing the
equant.

- For the **Moon**, Ptolemy's model made its distance change far more than it really does; Ibn al-Shatir saw that
  the observations did not allow this. He replaced Ptolemy's mechanism with a pair of epicycles that
  gave a much more realistic range of distances.
- For the planets he used the Urdi lemma and the Tusi couple to build models with only uniform circles.

His models for the Moon and for Mercury are **mathematically the same** as those Copernicus used in the 16th century,
and Copernicus's replacement of the equant by two epicycles in his first sketch, the *Commentariolus*, matches Ibn
al-Shatir's of more than a century earlier. In 1957 the historian Otto Neugebauer argued that this work must somehow
have reached Copernicus. How it did, and whether it did at all, is still argued (chapter 13). The difference that
matters is that Ibn al-Shatir's models kept the Earth at the centre.

## 8.10 Samarkand and Istanbul { #samarkand }

**Ulugh Beg** (1394 to 1449), a prince of the Timurid dynasty, became governor of Samarkand, in today's Uzbekistan,
at sixteen. He was a ruler who did astronomy himself.

- In the 1420s he built an observatory at Samarkand. Its main instrument, the **Fakhri sextant**, was a
  curved track set into a trench along the meridian, with a radius of **40.04 m**, the largest astronomical instrument
  in the world at the time.
- His astronomers made the *Zij-i Sultani*, with a new **catalogue of 1,018 stars**, measured with the observatory's
  own instruments, eleven fewer than Ptolemy's and one of the most original of the Middle Ages. Oxford published it in
  1665.
- In 1437 they found the sidereal year to be 365 days 6 hours 10 minutes 8 seconds, only 58 seconds too long.
- **Jamshid al-Kashi** (about 1380 to 1429), who worked at Ulugh Beg's institute, calculated π to 16 decimal places and made sine tables
  correct to about eight decimal places.

In 1449 Ulugh Beg was overthrown and killed on the orders of his own son. The observatory was destroyed, and its
astronomers scattered. In 1908 the Russian archaeologist Vassily Vyatkin found its remains, including the trench of the
great sextant.

One of his pupils, **Ali Qushji** (1403 to 1474), moved to the Ottoman court in Istanbul. He argued that astronomy
should rest on observation and mathematics rather than on Aristotle's physics, and that observation could not prove the
Earth stands still, so a moving Earth was just as possible. Like al-Biruni, he did not take the step to the Sun at the
centre.

**Istanbul.** In 1577 **Taqi al-Din** completed an observatory in Istanbul for Sultan Murad III, at almost the same time
as Tycho Brahe's in Denmark (chapter 14). A great comet appeared that year, and Taqi al-Din predicted good fortune and a
victory over Persia. In 1580 the observatory was pulled down.

## 8.11 Hope at Mars { #mars }

The astronomers of this chapter tabulated Mars for centuries. In the 21st century an Arab country sent a spacecraft
there.

The United Arab Emirates' **Hope** probe (*Al-Amal*) was launched on 20 July 2020 and went into orbit around Mars on
9 February 2021, the first of three missions that arrived that month (the others were China's Tianwen-1 and NASA's
Perseverance). Its science lead was **Sarah Al Amiri**. Hope studies the Martian weather: daily and seasonal cycles,
dust storms, and how the weather differs from region to region. Its orbit is unusually high, about 20,000 km above the
surface at its lowest and 43,000 km at its highest, taking 55 hours. On 10 March 2023 it flew close to the little moon
**Deimos** and took the first close-up images showing the whole of it.

## 8.12 Working it out { #working }

**Al-Biruni's triangle.** In the figure in section 8.6 the triangle centre–horizon point–mountain top has a right angle
at the horizon point, so with radius $R$, height $h$ and dip $\theta$:

$$\cos\theta = \frac{R}{R+h} \quad\Longrightarrow\quad R = \frac{h\cos\theta}{1-\cos\theta}.$$

For a small angle, $1 - \cos\theta \approx \theta^2/2$, so $R \approx 2h/\theta^2$ with $\theta$ in radians. The radius
depends on the **square** of a tiny angle. If the dip were measured a sixth too small, the radius would come out
$1/(5/6)^2 = 1.44$ times too big. That is why refraction matters so much.

The same method on Mars:

| | Mars | Earth |
|---|---|---|
| Mean radius | 3,389.5 km | 6,371.0 km |
| Height that gives a dip of 34′ | 166 m | 312 m |
| Dip seen from a 300 m hill | 45.7′ | 33.4′ |

On a smaller planet the ground curves away faster, so the horizon drops more for the same height.

**Why the Tusi couple makes a straight line.** Let the big circle have radius $2r$ and centre at the origin. The small
circle's centre goes round at angle $t$: it is at $(r\cos t,\ r\sin t)$. Rolling without slipping makes the small circle
turn backwards relative to that arm, so the point $P$ sits at angle $-t$ from the small circle's centre:

$$P = (r\cos t + r\cos t,\ r\sin t - r\sin t) = (2r\cos t,\ 0).$$

The second coordinate is always zero: $P$ slides along a diameter, from $+2r$ to $-2r$ and back. Two uniform circular
motions make one straight back-and-forth motion.

**How long was al-Ma'mun's degree?** With 56⅔ miles per degree and a mile of 4,000 cubits, the answer depends on the
cubit:

| Cubit | Mile | One degree | Earth's circumference | Error |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| surveying, 48.25 cm | 1.93 km | 109.4 km | 39,370 km | −1.6% |
| legal, 49.8 cm | 1.99 km | 112.9 km | 40,640 km | +1.6% |
| black, 54.04 cm | 2.16 km | 122.5 km | 44,100 km | +10% |

The true degree of latitude is about 111.1 km, and the circumference about 40,008 km pole to pole. Like Eratosthenes'
stadion, the unit decides whether the answer was brilliant or merely good.

**Years, then and now.**

| Who | Year measured | Their value | Modern value | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Thabit ibn Qurra (9th century) | sidereal | 365 d 6 h 9 min 12 s | 365 d 6 h 9 min 10 s | +2 s |
| Al-Battani (about 900) | tropical | 365 d 5 h 46 min 24 s | 365 d 5 h 48 min 45 s | −2 min 21 s |
| Ulugh Beg (1437) | sidereal | 365 d 6 h 10 min 8 s | 365 d 6 h 9 min 10 s | +58 s |

**Hope's orbit, checked with Kepler's third law.** Hope's lowest and highest heights are about 20,000 km and 43,000 km.
Adding Mars's radius, the semi-major axis is $a = 31{,}500 + 3{,}389.5 \approx 34{,}890$ km. The period is
$T = 2\pi\sqrt{a^3/GM}$:

| | Mars | Earth |
|---|---|---|
| $GM$ | 42,828 km³/s² | 398,600 km³/s² |
| Period of an orbit with $a$ = 34,890 km | 55.0 hours | 18.0 hours |

The formula gives Hope's 55 hours. Mars's weaker gravity makes the same orbit three times slower than it would be
around the Earth.

## 8.13 How should we judge this astronomy? { #assessment }

**More than a storehouse.** An old picture says the Islamic world "kept Greek science safe" until Europe was ready for
it. The record in this chapter does not fit that picture. Its astronomers combined Indian, Persian and Greek methods;
measured the year, the tilt of the Earth's axis and the motion of the Sun's apogee better than Ptolemy; mapped the stars
again; took trigonometry far beyond its Greek and Indian start and computed π to 16 places; and built
observatories as institutions with staff, libraries and instruments, the ancestors of today's.

**No golden age and decline.** Another old picture has a golden age that ended with the sack of Baghdad in 1258. But
Maragha was founded the year after, Ibn al-Shatir worked in the 14th century, Samarkand in the 15th and Istanbul in the
16th. Observatories did keep closing when patrons died or turned against them, and that fragility was a real weakness.

**Critics, but still geocentric.** These astronomers attacked the equant and rebuilt Ptolemy's models with uniform
circles, and some, from al-Biruni to Qushji, admitted that the Earth might turn. But as historians note, the Maragha
school "never made the big leap" to a Sun-centred system. That step, using in part the very same mathematics, was
Copernicus's (chapter 13).

**The road to Europe.** Arabic books reached Latin Europe mostly through Spain in the 12th century, with al-Khwarizmi,
al-Battani and the *Almagest* among the first (chapter 9). How the later Maragha models reached
Copernicus is still an open question.

    - See the sources for sections 8.1 to 8.11. The quotation on the Maragha school: Wikipedia,
      [Astronomy in the medieval Islamic world](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy_in_the_medieval_Islamic_world) (weaker).

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Give two daily needs that made astronomy important in Islamic societies.

??? note "Answer"
    Any two of: setting the five prayer times; finding the qibla, the direction of Mecca; starting each month of the
    lunar calendar with the sighting of the crescent Moon.

**2.** (beginner) Where does the word "sine" come from?

??? note "Answer"
    From the Sanskrit *jya*, "bowstring", copied into Arabic as *jiba*, read as *jayb*, "fold", and translated into Latin
    as *sinus*, "fold".

**3.** (beginner) What did al-Sufi record in 964 that we now know is another galaxy?

??? note "Answer"
    The Andromeda galaxy, which he described as a "small cloud".

**4.** (intermediate) Why did Ibn al-Haytham object to Ptolemy's equant?

??? note "Answer"
    A real body should move uniformly about the centre of its own circle; Ptolemy made it move uniformly about an
    imaginary point that is not the centre. The model predicted well but could not be a true picture of the heavens.

**5.** (intermediate) What does a Tusi couple do, and why was it useful?

??? note "Answer"
    A circle rolling inside one twice its size makes a point on its rim move back and forth in a straight line. It let
    astronomers produce the varying distances Ptolemy needed, using only uniform circular motions, and so remove the
    equant.

**6.** (intermediate) Name three things done at Ulugh Beg's observatory in Samarkand.

??? note "Answer"
    Any three of: the Fakhri sextant with a radius of 40 m; a new catalogue of 1,018 stars; the sidereal year to within
    58 seconds; al-Kashi's π to 16 decimal places and accurate sine tables.

**7.** (advanced) From a hill 300 m high on Earth ($R$ = 6,371 km), what is the dip of the horizon? Use
$\cos\theta = R/(R+h)$.

??? note "Answer"
    $\cos\theta = 6371 / 6371.3 = 0.999953$, so $\theta \approx 0.0097$ radians $\approx 0.556^\circ \approx 33.4'$.

**8.** (expert) Hope's orbit has a semi-major axis of about 34,890 km. Mars's $GM$ is 42,828 km³/s². Show that the
period is about 55 hours. What would the period be for an orbit with the same $a$ around the Earth
($GM$ = 398,600 km³/s²)?

??? note "Answer"
    $T = 2\pi\sqrt{34{,}890^3 / 42{,}828} = 2\pi\sqrt{9.92 \times 10^8} \approx 2\pi \times 31{,}490 \approx
    198{,}000$ s $\approx 55.0$ hours. Around the Earth, $T = 2\pi\sqrt{34{,}890^3 / 398{,}600} \approx 64{,}900$ s
    $\approx 18.0$ hours. The period scales as $1/\sqrt{GM}$, and $\sqrt{398{,}600/42{,}828} \approx 3.05$.

## Summary

- Prayer times, the qibla and the lunar calendar gave astronomy daily work in Islamic societies.
- Baghdad began with Indian and Persian tables (the *Zij al-Sindhind*), then translated Ptolemy; Thabit and al-Khwarizmi
  were among its scholars, and "sine" came through Arabic from Sanskrit.
- Al-Ma'mun's astronomers measured a degree on the plain of Sinjar around 830; al-Battani measured the year, the tilt and the moving solar
  apogee; Ibn Yunus's eclipses were still useful 800 years later.
- Al-Sufi drew the stars again in 964 and first recorded the Andromeda galaxy; Arabic star names fill our sky.
- Ali ibn Ridwan in Cairo described the star of 1006; Ibn Butlan wrote the only known Arabic account of 1054.
- Al-Biruni measured the Earth from a hilltop at Nandana and weighed the idea of a turning Earth.
- Ibn al-Haytham's *Doubts* attacked the equant; Maragha (1259) brought al-Tusi, al-Urdi and a Chinese astronomer
  together and produced the Tusi couple.
- Ibn al-Shatir's 14th-century models match Copernicus's mathematically, but keep the Earth at the centre.
- Ulugh Beg's Samarkand made the first new star catalogue since Ptolemy and measured the year within a minute; Taqi
  al-Din's Istanbul observatory lasted three years.
- The UAE's Hope probe has studied Mars's weather from orbit since 2021.
