# 21. The space age at Mars

*For three thousand years people watched Mars from the Earth. From 1960 they began to send machines. The first tries
failed one after another, and even today Mars swallows many of the craft sent to it. But the ones that got through
turned a red dot into a real place, with volcanoes, canyons, old river beds and ice. This chapter tells that story as
a world story: the Soviet Union and the United States first, then Europe, Britain, Japan, India, China and the United
Arab Emirates.*

## 21.1 First tries { #first }

The **Soviet Union** tried first. Two probes launched in October 1960, each of about 650 kg, never reached Earth orbit.
Of three more in late 1962, one exploded while leaving Earth orbit and one failed at launch. The third, **Mars 1**,
set off on 1 November 1962, but contact was lost on 21 March 1963; it drifted past Mars at about 100,000 km in June
without sending anything.

The **United States** lost **Mariner 3** at launch in November 1964. Three weeks later **Mariner 4** set off, and in
July 1965 it sent back the first close pictures of Mars: craters and a thin, dry sky, the end of the canals of chapter
17 (chapter 11 tells the flight). Mariners 6 and 7 flew past in 1969.

Launches come in bunches, about every 26 months, because that is how often the Earth and Mars line up for the
cheapest path between them (section 21.10).

## 21.2 1971: into orbit and down to the ground { #orbit }

**Mariner 9** reached Mars on **14 November 1971** and became the first spacecraft to orbit another planet, only just
ahead of the Soviet **Mars 2** and **Mars 3**. It found the whole planet hidden under the largest dust storm ever seen.
Mariner 9 waited. When the dust settled it mapped 85 per cent of the surface in 7,329 pictures. It showed that
**Olympus Mons** is a volcano, the largest known in the Solar System, and the great canyon it found was named
**Valles Marineris** after it.

The Soviet landers were let go hours before their craft reached Mars, straight into the storm.

- On **27 November 1971** the **Mars 2** lander crashed. It was the first human-made object to reach the surface of
  Mars.
- On **2 December 1971** the **Mars 3** lander made the first soft landing on Mars. It began to transmit 90 seconds
  after landing, sent a grey picture with no detail, and fell silent 20 seconds later. Nobody knows why. Both landers
  carried a small rover, PrOP-M, which never got the chance to move.

## 21.3 Is anything alive? { #viking }

In 1976 the two American **Viking** landers came down safely (chapter 11) and tested the soil for life.

- **For.** In the **Labeled Release** test, led by **Gilbert Levin**, soil was given nutrients tagged with radioactive
  carbon-14. If microbes ate them, they would give off tagged gas. On both landers, the first test came back
  **positive**. Levin long held that Viking had found living things, and in 2012 he and Patricia
  Straat argued so again from a new analysis of the data.
- **Against.** The **gas chromatograph–mass spectrometer**, led by Klaus Biemann of MIT, heated the soil and found no
  significant amount of organic molecules, the carbon compounds that life is made of. For most of the Viking
  scientists this settled it: no life was found.
- **A twist.** In 2008 the Phoenix lander found **perchlorate** in the soil, a salt that destroys organic molecules
  when heated. Christopher McKay of NASA estimated that, with that much perchlorate, Viking's soil could have held
  up to 0.1 per cent organic matter and still given a blank result.

Most scientists now say the Viking results are **inconclusive**, and that chemistry alone can explain them.

## 21.4 Wheels on Mars { #rovers }

**Pathfinder.** On **4 July 1997** NASA's **Mars Pathfinder** came down inside airbags at Ares Vallis. It was built
cheaply, under the motto "cheaper, faster and better", and it carried **Sojourner**, a rover of 10.6 kg, the first rover
to work beyond the Earth and the Moon. The lander was renamed the Carl Sagan Memorial Station.

**Spirit and Opportunity.** In January 2004 two rovers landed, three weeks apart, on opposite sides of Mars, each built to last 90 sols.
**Spirit** drove 7.73 km instead of the planned 600 m, got stuck in soft sand in 2009, and last called home on
22 March 2010. **Opportunity** found **hematite**, a clue that there was once water. On 24 March 2015
it passed the length of a marathon, and it worked for **5,111 sols** and 45.16 km, until a planet-wide dust storm
in June 2018 cut off its sunlight. A journalist turned its last data into words that many people shared: "My battery
is low and it's getting dark."

**Curiosity.** On **6 August 2012** the 899 kg rover **Curiosity** was lowered into **Gale crater** on cables from a
hovering **sky crane**. Within a year it had shown that ancient Mars could have been home to microbes.
It landed about 10 km from the foot of Mount Sharp, and it is still at work. **Perseverance** and its helicopter **Ingenuity** followed in 2021
(chapter 11), and **InSight** listened for marsquakes from 2018 (chapter 13).

The map shows where every lander so far has come down. All but Phoenix landed within 50 degrees of the equator, and most on low ground, where there is more air for a
parachute to work with.

## 21.5 Asia arrives { #asia }

**Japan.** Japan's **Nozomi**, launched in 1998, never reached Mars orbit (chapter 6).

**China, first try.** China's first Mars probe, **Yinghuo-1**, took its name from the old Chinese name for Mars
(chapter 4). It rode with Russia's **Fobos-Grunt**, which was meant to bring back soil from Phobos. They launched on
8 November 2011, but Fobos-Grunt never left Earth orbit, and both burned up over the Pacific on 15 January 2012.

**India.** On 5 November 2013 a PSLV rocket launched India's **Mars Orbiter Mission**, *Mangalyaan*. After 298 days
it went into Mars orbit on **24 September 2014** (chapter 5). India became the first Asian nation to reach Mars orbit,
and ISRO the fourth space agency to do it, after the Soviet Union, NASA and ESA, and the second to succeed at the
first attempt. The whole mission cost about 450 crore rupees, the least expensive Mars mission so far; Prime Minister
Narendra Modi said it cost less than the film *Gravity*. The project director was **Subbiah Arunan**, and **Ritu
Karidhal** and **Nandini Harinath** were deputy operations directors for navigation. The orbiter appeared on the
2,000-rupee note. It worked for over seven years, until in April 2022 it passed through a seven-hour eclipse that it was not built to
survive.

**The Emirates.** The UAE's **Hope** went into orbit on 9 February 2021 to study the weather of Mars (chapter 8).

**China, second try.** **Tianwen-1** takes its name from a poem by **Qu Yuan**, written over 2,200 years ago,
"Questions to Heaven". Launched on 23 July 2020, it put an orbiter round Mars, and in May 2021 its lander set down
in **Utopia Planitia** with the rover **Zhurong**, named after a figure of Chinese myth linked with fire and light.
China became the first country to land and drive a rover on Mars at its first attempt. Zhurong was
built for 90 sols and worked for 347. It went to sleep for the winter on 18 May 2022 and, covered in dust, never
woke up.

## 21.6 Europe and Britain { #europe }

**Mars Express**, Europe's first Mars mission, has orbited Mars since 2003 (chapters 9 and 20). It carried a small
British lander, **Beagle 2**, dreamed up by **Colin Pillinger** of the Open University. Its call sign was written by the
band Blur, and its colour test card was painted by Damien Hirst. It was due to land on Christmas Day 2003 and was
never heard from. In January 2015 the HiRISE camera on NASA's Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter found it: it had landed
safely, but two of its four solar panels had not opened, and they blocked its radio antenna.

In 2016 Europe and Russia sent the **Trace Gas Orbiter**, which is still working, and a test lander,
**Schiaparelli**. On 19 October 2016, as it came down, a sensor that measures turning was swamped for about a second.
The computer worked out a height below the ground, let go of the parachute, fired the braking rockets for 3 seconds
instead of 30, and switched on its landing systems while it was still 3.7 km up. It fell to the ground.

Europe's rover **Rosalind Franklin**, named after the British chemist and pioneer of DNA research, was to
fly with Russia. After Russia invaded Ukraine in 2022, ESA's members ended the partnership. The rover is now expected
to launch in 2028, and will be the first to drill up to 2 m into Mars.

## 21.7 Mars is hard { #hard }

By the count in Wikipedia's list, 49 missions were launched towards Mars from 1960 to 2026. Two are still on the way.
Of the other 47, **28 worked** and **19 failed or only partly worked**. Some of the failures were bad luck. Many were
small human mistakes.

- **Phobos 1, 1988.** A test command that switched off the craft's steering thrusters had been left in its computer,
  because removing it meant replacing the whole computer. A single wrong character in a message sent from the ground
  ran it. The craft lost its lock on the Sun, its solar panels turned away, and its batteries ran flat. Its twin,
  **Phobos 2**, reached Mars orbit and sent back data before it too was lost.
- **Mars Climate Orbiter, 1999.** One piece of software, from the builder Lockheed Martin, gave the push of the
  thrusters in **pound-force seconds**. NASA's software that used the numbers expected **newton-seconds**, 4.45 times
  bigger. The orbiter, aimed to pass 226 km above Mars, went in at about 57 km and was lost on 23 September 1999.
  Its partner, Mars Polar Lander, failed too.
- **Schiaparelli, 2016** (section 21.6), and **Beagle 2**, which landed but could not talk.

## 21.8 The methane puzzle { #methane }

Methane in Mars's air might be a sign of life, so a whiff of it is exciting.

- **For.** Curiosity saw a tenfold **spike** in methane around it in late 2013 and early 2014: four readings over
  two months averaged 7.2 parts per billion, against about a tenth of that before and after. In 2018 NASA reported that
  the background level rises and falls with the seasons. Mars Express and telescopes on the Earth had reported methane
  too.
- **Against.** Europe's **Trace Gas Orbiter**, more sensitive than any of them, found **none at all**, and by 2021 had
  set an upper limit below 0.05 parts per billion. Some scientists suspect the rover readings came from the rover
  itself or from a misreading of the raw data.
- **Even if it is there,** methane can come from water reacting with rocks such as olivine, which is common on Mars.
  Microbes are possible, but there is no other evidence for them.

The question is still open.

## 21.9 Bringing Mars home { #samples }

Perseverance has been sealing rock and soil in tubes since 2021, ready to be collected. A joint NASA–ESA mission to
fetch them was approved in 2022, but a cost of about 11 billion dollars was judged too high; NASA paused it in
November 2023, and it was cancelled in 2026.

China plans **Tianwen-3**, two launches on Long March 5 rockets in the window of December 2028 to January 2029, to
bring back about 500 g of Mars. Japan's **MMX**, launched in October 2026, is due to bring back samples of Phobos in
2031 (chapter 6). The first samples brought home on purpose from Mars or its moons may well come in a Japanese or Chinese capsule.

## 21.10 Working it out { #working }

**The trip.** The cheapest path from the Earth to Mars, a **Hohmann transfer**, is half an ellipse touching both
orbits. Its long axis is $1 + 1.524 = 2.524$ AU, so its semi-major axis is 1.262 AU. By Kepler's third law the full
ellipse takes $1.262^{1.5} = 1.418$ years, and half of it takes **0.709 years, or 259 days**. Mangalyaan took 298
days on a similar path.

**The windows.** The Earth gains a full lap on Mars every
$1 / (1/365.26 - 1/686.98) = 780$ days, about 26 months, so the line-up for that path comes round that often. The chart
in section 21.7 shows it: launches in 1960, 1962 and 1964, then 1969, 1971, 1973 and 1975, come in two-year steps.

**The unit mix-up.** One pound-force is 4.448 newtons. A thruster push of 10 pound-force seconds is
$10 \times 4.448 = 44.5$ newton-seconds; read as 10 newton-seconds, it is taken as 4.45 times too small.

**Opportunity.** $45{,}160 \text{ m} / 5111 \text{ sols} = 8.8$ m a sol on average, and it lasted $5111 / 90 = 57$
times longer than planned.

**Mars and the Earth.**

| | Mars | Earth |
|---|---|---|
| Surface gravity | 3.73 m/s² | 9.82 m/s² |
| Weight of Curiosity (899 kg) | $899 \times 3.73 = 3{,}350$ N | $899 \times 9.82 = 8{,}830$ N |
| Escape speed | 5.03 km/s | 11.19 km/s |
| Air density at the surface | about 0.016 kg/m³ | 1.217 kg/m³ |
| Length of a solar day | 24 h 39.6 min | 24 h |

Mars's air is about $1.217 / 0.016 \approx 76$ times thinner. A parachute alone cannot slow a lander enough, which is
why Mars landers have also used rockets, airbags and sky cranes (the physics lessons
[Parachutes in thin air](https://physics.akadian.ca/topics/rockets/level-4/05-parachutes-in-thin-air.html) and
[Seven minutes](https://physics.akadian.ca/topics/rockets/level-4/04-seven-minutes.html)).

## 21.11 How should we judge this story? { #assessment }

**A race, then a crowd.** The first twenty years were a contest between two superpowers, and the Soviet Union, which
launched first and often, had by far the worst record. Since 1998 the club has grown to Europe, Britain, Japan, India, China and the
Emirates. Several of them succeeded on their first or second try, learning from those who went before.

**Cheap and dear.** India reached Mars for about 450 crore rupees, while the plan to bring back Perseverance's samples
was dropped as too costly at about 11 billion dollars. Cost is not everything, since the two missions did very different
things, but it shapes what gets flown.

**Careful with "life".** Viking's tests, the methane and the rock Perseverance found in 2024 (chapter 11) have all
been called possible signs of life. Each time, the honest answer has been "possible, not proven". Extraordinary claims
need more than one kind of evidence.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Which was the first spacecraft to orbit another planet, and what was Mars like when it arrived?

??? note "Answer"
    Mariner 9, in November 1971. Mars was hidden under a planet-wide dust storm, so it had to wait for the dust to
    settle before mapping the surface.

**2.** (beginner) Which country made the first soft landing on Mars, and what happened next?

??? note "Answer"
    The Soviet Union, with Mars 3, on 2 December 1971. The lander sent a grey picture for 20 seconds and then fell
    silent.

**3.** (beginner) Why was the Mars Climate Orbiter lost?

??? note "Answer"
    One piece of software gave thruster pushes in pound-force seconds while the software that used them expected
    newton-seconds. The orbiter's path was wrong, and it went far too deep into Mars's air.

**4.** (intermediate) Give the main argument for and against Viking having found life, and explain how perchlorate
changed the debate.

??? note "Answer"
    For: the Labeled Release test came back positive on both landers. Against: the GCMS found no significant organic
    molecules. Perchlorate, found by Phoenix in 2008, destroys organic molecules when soil is heated, so the GCMS
    might have missed organics that were really there.

**5.** (intermediate) Why do Mars launches come in bunches about two years apart?

??? note "Answer"
    The cheapest path to Mars only works when Mars is about 44° ahead of the Earth at launch. That line-up comes
    round once a synodic period, about 780 days, or 26 months.

**6.** (intermediate) Curiosity and the Trace Gas Orbiter disagree about methane. What did each find?

??? note "Answer"
    Curiosity measured methane near the ground, including a spike averaging 7.2 parts per billion and a seasonal
    cycle. The Trace Gas Orbiter, more sensitive, found none and set a limit below 0.05 parts per billion.

**7.** (advanced) Curiosity has a mass of 899 kg. What is its weight on Mars and on the Earth, and how many times
lighter does it feel on Mars?

??? note "Answer"
    Mars: $899 \times 3.73 = 3{,}350$ N. Earth: $899 \times 9.82 = 8{,}830$ N. Ratio $9.82 / 3.73 = 2.6$ times lighter.

**8.** (expert) Using Kepler's third law, show that the Hohmann transfer to Mars takes about 259 days. Mangalyaan took
298 days. Give one reason a real trip can differ from the ideal.

??? note "Answer"
    Semi-major axis $a = (1 + 1.524)/2 = 1.262$ AU. Period $1.262^{1.5} = 1.418$ years; half is 0.709 years, and
    $0.709 \times 365.26 = 259$ days. Real trips differ because the orbits are not perfect circles in one plane,
    Mars is not exactly where the ideal path needs it, and a mission may trade a longer or shorter trip for less fuel
    or a better arrival.

## Summary

- The Soviet Union tried first, in 1960; Mariner 4 sent the first close pictures in 1965.
- In 1971 Mariner 9 became the first craft to orbit another planet, Mars 2 the first to reach the surface and Mars 3
  the first to land softly.
- Viking's tests for life in 1976 gave mixed results that are still argued over.
- Rovers from Sojourner to Curiosity and Perseverance turned Mars into a place with a past full of water.
- India (2014), the Emirates and China (2021) reached Mars; China landed a rover on its first full try.
- About two in five Mars missions have failed, often through small human mistakes.
- The methane in Mars's air is disputed, and the first samples may come home with China or Japan.
