# 19. Galaxies and the expanding universe

*For most of history the Milky Way was a river, a road or a trail of straw across the sky. In 1900 most astronomers
still thought it was the whole universe, with a few fuzzy patches inside it. Within thirty years that picture was gone.
A woman measuring photographs from Peru found a way to judge the distance to faraway stars, a young man who had trained
as a lawyer used it to show the fuzzy patches are other galaxies, and a Belgian priest and a Russian mathematician
worked out that the whole universe could be growing, as Hubble's measurements then showed. Seen from Mars, those galaxies look exactly the same.*

## 19.1 The Milky Way { #milkyway }

Every people with a dark sky named the pale band across it. In China, Korea and Vietnam it is the **Silver River**,
and in Japan the **River of Heaven**. In West Asia, Central Asia and parts of the Balkans its name means the **path of
straw**, and in several Uralic, Turkic and Baltic languages it is the **Birds' Path**, since migrating birds were seen
to follow it. The Gomeroi people of eastern Australia call it **Dhinawan**, the giant Emu in the Sky.

**Made of stars.** Some Greek thinkers, such as Anaxagoras and Democritus, thought the band was the glow of stars hidden in
the Earth's shadow. Al-Biruni in Persia, around 1000, called it "a collection of countless fragments of the nature of nebulous
stars". In 1610 Galileo's telescope settled it: the Milky Way is a vast number of faint stars (chapter 15).

**A disc.** In 1750 the English astronomer **Thomas Wright** suggested that the Milky Way looks like a band because we
live inside a flat layer of stars. The young philosopher **Immanuel Kant** read about Wright's idea and in 1755 went
further: the Milky Way is one system of stars among many. In 1785 William Herschel (chapter 17) counted stars in
different directions to map its shape. He put the Sun near the middle.

## 19.2 Fuzzy patches { #nebulae }

**Two clouds and a smudge.** In 964 the Persian astronomer al-Sufi described the Andromeda Galaxy as a "small cloud",
the first record of it (chapter 8). The two Magellanic Clouds are bright patches in the far southern sky. Arabic writers of the 800s passed on reports of "bright big stars,
not seen in Iraq" that the people of Tihama, on the Red Sea coast, could see; these may have been stars near Canopus
rather than the Clouds. Italian writers described the Clouds in the early 1500s from Portuguese voyages, and Antonio
Pigafetta wrote about them on Magellan's voyage of 1519 to 1522.

**A comet hunter's list.** The French astronomer **Charles Messier**, whom King Louis XV called the "ferret of comets",
kept a list, with his friend Pierre Méchain, of fuzzy objects that were *not* comets, so as not to waste
time on them. The first
version, of 45 objects, came out in 1774; today the list has 110. Number 31 is Andromeda.

**Spirals.** In 1845 at Birr Castle in Ireland, **William Parsons, Earl of Rosse**, finished a telescope with a mirror
1.83 m across, the largest in the world until the early 1900s. Its regular use was held up for two years by the Great
Irish Famine. With it he saw that some nebulae, like M51, are **spirals**. Were they whirlpools of gas inside the Milky
Way, or other Milky Ways far away? Nobody could tell without knowing how far they were.

## 19.3 How far are the stars? { #parallax }

As the Earth goes round the Sun, a nearby star seems to shift slightly against more distant ones, just as your finger
seems to jump when you look at it with one eye and then the other. This is **parallax**. The shift is tiny, less than
one second of arc for every star, and for centuries nobody could measure it.

Three astronomers did it in the 1830s, in three different places:

- **At the Cape of Good Hope in South Africa.** **Thomas Henderson**, who had trained as a lawyer in Dundee,
  measured **Alpha Centauri** in 1832 to 1833. Manuel Johnson, at the East India Company's observatory on Saint
  Helena, had told him the star moved fast across the sky and so was probably close. Henderson found it was just over
  3 light-years away, but he lacked confidence and did not publish until 1839.
- **At Dorpat, in today's Estonia.** **Friedrich von Struve** measured **Vega** in 1835 to 1836 and published in 1837.
- **At Königsberg, in Prussia.** **Friedrich Bessel** measured **61 Cygni** in 1837 to 1838. His parallax of
  0.314 seconds of arc put the star 10.3 light-years away. He published first, in 1838, and usually gets the credit.

From 2013 to 2025 the European spacecraft **Gaia** measured the parallaxes of about a billion stars, mapping the
Milky Way.

## 19.4 Leavitt's yardstick { #leavitt }

Parallax works only for nearby stars. For anything farther, astronomers needed a star whose true brightness they knew.
Then, from how faint it looks, they could tell how far away it is.

**Henrietta Swan Leavitt** studied at what became Radcliffe College and joined the Harvard computers (chapter 18).
She was gradually going deaf, and was paid 30 cents an hour. Pickering gave her photographs of the two Magellanic
Clouds, taken with a telescope at Harvard's southern station in **Arequipa, Peru** (chapter 17). On them she found
1,777 stars that change in brightness.

Her key idea was simple: all the stars in the Small Magellanic Cloud are at roughly the same distance from us, so if
one looks brighter than another, it really is brighter. In 1908 she noticed that the brighter variable stars took
longer to rise and fall. In **1912**, with 25 stars of the kind called **Cepheids**, she showed the link exactly: the
longer a Cepheid takes to pulse, the brighter it truly is. Time a Cepheid's pulse, and you know how bright it really
is; compare that with how bright it looks, and you know how far away it is.

Leavitt died of cancer in 1921. In 1924 the Swedish mathematician Gösta Mittag-Leffler tried to nominate her for a
Nobel Prize, not knowing she had died; the prize is not given after death. Hubble often said she deserved it.

## 19.5 The Great Debate { #debate }

**Shapley's big galaxy.** **Harlow Shapley** grew up in Missouri and worked as a crime reporter before he turned to
astronomy. At Mount Wilson in California, using Leavitt's yardstick, he measured the distances to globular clusters,
great balls of stars around the Milky Way. In 1918 he used them to map the Milky Way, and found that the Sun is not at
its middle but off to one side. He made the Milky Way some 300,000 light-years across.

**The debate.** On **26 April 1920** in Washington, D.C., Shapley and **Heber Curtis** argued about the spiral nebulae.

- **Shapley**: the Milky Way is so big that the spirals must be small clouds inside it or near its edge. He also
  pointed to Adriaan van Maanen, who claimed to have seen a spiral turning within a few years. If it were a distant
  galaxy, it would have to spin faster than light.
- **Curtis**: the Milky Way is about 30,000 light-years across, and the spirals are separate galaxies. Novae seen in
  Andromeda were about ten magnitudes fainter than novae in the Milky Way, which gave him a distance of about
  500,000 light-years. Spirals were never seen along the band of the Milky Way because dust in our galaxy hides
  them, the same kind of dust seen in spirals edge-on. And spirals moved too fast to be held by our galaxy.

**Who was right?** Both, in part. Curtis was right that the spirals are other galaxies; van Maanen's turning spirals
turned out to be a mistake, since no galaxy can be seen to turn in a human lifetime. Shapley was right that the Sun is far from the centre. The size of the Milky Way lies
between their two numbers: today about 87,400 light-years across, with the Sun about 27,000 light-years from the
centre, taking about 240 million years to go round once.

## 19.6 Hubble and Andromeda { #hubble }

**Edwin Hubble** went to Oxford as a Rhodes Scholar and, as he had promised his dying father, studied law there
instead of science. He came back to astronomy and in 1919 joined Mount Wilson, just as the 2.5 m **Hooker telescope**,
the largest in the world from 1917 to 1949, was ready.

In 1924 he found **Cepheids in Andromeda**. Using Leavitt's rule, they showed that Andromeda lies far outside
the Milky Way: it is a whole galaxy of its own. The news was in *The New York Times* on 23 November 1924, and Hubble
told astronomers at a meeting on 1 January 1925. Others had pointed the same way. In 1922 Ernst Öpik had used the
speeds of Andromeda's stars to place it about 1.5 million light-years away.

Hubble's own distance was too small. In 1952 Walter Baade, at a meeting in Rome, showed that there are two kinds of
Cepheids with different brightness, which doubled the size of the known universe. Today Andromeda is put at about
2.5 million light-years. On a dark night with no Moon, far from city lights, it can be seen with the naked eye.

## 19.7 The universe grows { #expanding }

**Slipher's speeds.** At **Lowell's observatory** in Flagstaff (chapter 17), **Vesto Slipher** began in 1912 to measure
the spectra of spiral nebulae. Andromeda was coming towards us at about 300 km/s, the fastest speed yet measured. But
as he measured more spirals, most had their lines shifted to the **red**: they were rushing away.

**The mathematicians.** In 1922 the Russian mathematician **Alexander Friedmann**, of St Petersburg, showed
that Einstein's theory of gravity allows a universe that expands. Einstein at first did not accept it. Friedmann died
of typhoid in 1925, aged 37, two months after a record balloon flight to 7,400 m. In 1927 **Georges Lemaître**, a
Belgian Catholic priest and physicist who had fought in the First World War, worked out the same idea and saw that
more distant galaxies should be moving away faster. He published in a Belgian journal few people read. As Lemaître
remembered it, Einstein told him, "Your calculations are correct, but your physics is abominable."

**Hubble's law.** In **1929** Hubble, working with **Milton Humason**, put Slipher's speeds together with distances
from Cepheids for 46 galaxies. The farther a galaxy, the faster it moves away: about **500 km/s for every megaparsec**
(3.26 million light-years), on his numbers. Humason had left school at 14, driven mule trains up Mount Wilson while
the observatory was built, and started there as a janitor. The astronomer Carl Wirtz had seen a similar trend
in 1922 and 1924. In 2018 the International Astronomical Union recommended calling it the **Hubble–Lemaître law**.

**A beginning.** In 1931 Lemaître suggested that the universe began from a single "primeval atom". In a radio talk in
1949 Fred Hoyle, who preferred a universe with no beginning, called this "the big bang", and the name stuck. The
afterglow of that beginning was found in 1964 with a radio antenna (chapter 20). Today the universe's age is put at
13.8 billion years.

**Still arguing.** Today's value of the expansion rate depends on the method. The afterglow gives about **67**, while
the Cepheid and supernova ladder that grew from Leavitt's work gives about **73** km/s per megaparsec. This gap, the
"Hubble tension", is one of the big open questions in astronomy.

## 19.9 The same sky from Mars { #onmars }

Mars is never more than about 400 million km from the Earth, less than 23 light-minutes. Andromeda is 2.5 million
light-years away. Seen from Mars, every galaxy and every star beyond the Sun looks just the same as from here, and
the light from Andromeda that reaches a rover tonight left it more than ten times longer ago than modern humans
have existed.

Mars does have one advantage. Its orbit is 1.52 times as wide as the Earth's, so a telescope on Mars would see every
star's parallax 1.52 times bigger (the figure in section 19.3), though it would wait 687 days instead of a year for
each full swing.

## 19.10 Working it out { #working }

**Parallax.** A star at $d$ parsecs shows a parallax of $1/d$ seconds of arc from the Earth's orbit (radius 1 AU).
From an orbit of radius $r$ AU it shows $r/d$. One parsec is 3.26 light-years.

| | Mars | Earth |
|---|---|---|
| Radius of orbit | 1.524 AU | 1 AU |
| Parallax of 61 Cygni (Bessel's 0.314″ from the Earth) | $1.524 \times 0.314 = 0.479$″ | 0.314″ |
| Parallax of a star 10 parsecs away | 0.152″ | 0.100″ |
| Time for one full swing | 687 days | 365 days |
| Light from the Sun takes | 12.7 minutes | 8.3 minutes |
| Light from Andromeda takes | about 2.5 million years | about 2.5 million years |

Bessel's 0.314″ gives $1/0.314 = 3.18$ parsecs, or $3.18 \times 3.26 = 10.4$ light-years. (He gave 10.3.)

**Leavitt's yardstick.** Light spreads out as the square of the distance. Two Cepheids that pulse at the same rate are
equally bright; if one looks 10,000 times fainter, it is $\sqrt{10{,}000} = 100$ times farther away.

**Hubble's law.** $v = H_0 \, d$. With today's $H_0 \approx 70$ km/s per Mpc, a galaxy 100 Mpc away moves away at about
7,000 km/s. That stretches the red hydrogen line of chapter 18 from 656.3 nm by
$656.3 \times 7000 / 299{,}792 = 15.3$ nm, to about 671.6 nm.

**How old?** If galaxies had always moved at today's speeds, they would all have been together a time $1/H_0$ ago.
Since 1 Mpc is $3.086 \times 10^{19}$ km, $1/H_0$ in billions of years is about $977.8 / H_0$.

| $H_0$ (km/s per Mpc) | $1/H_0$ |
|---|---|
| 500 (Hubble, 1929) | 2.0 billion years |
| 73 (distance ladder) | 13.4 billion years |
| 67.4 (afterglow, 2018) | 14.5 billion years |

Hubble's 2 billion years was too young to fit what radioactive elements and the oldest stars were already suggesting,
a puzzle Lemaître tried to solve.

## 19.11 How should we judge this story? { #assessment }

**The yardstick and the hand that held it.** Hubble is the famous name, but his distances stood on Leavitt's rule, and
many of his speeds on Slipher's spectra. Leavitt was paid by the hour and died before anyone could honour her.
Humason had no diploma. The theory came first from Friedmann and Lemaître, and Lemaître's paper was hardly read
outside Belgium.

**A fair debate.** Shapley and Curtis were each right about something and wrong about something. That is common in
science: a debate is a way of sorting good evidence from bad, not a contest with one winner.

**Still open.** The expansion rate is measured to within a few per cent by two methods that do not agree. Either one
of them has a hidden error, or something is missing from our picture of the universe.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) What did Galileo's telescope show about the Milky Way in 1610?

??? note "Answer"
    That it is made of a vast number of faint stars.

**2.** (beginner) Name two of the names that different peoples gave the Milky Way.

??? note "Answer"
    For example the Silver River (China, Korea, Vietnam), the River of Heaven (Japan), the path of straw (West Asia),
    the Birds' Path (Uralic, Turkic and Baltic languages), or Dhinawan, the Emu in the Sky (the Gomeroi of Australia).

**3.** (beginner) Where were the photographs Leavitt studied taken, and of what?

??? note "Answer"
    At Harvard's southern station at Arequipa in Peru, of the Magellanic Clouds.

**4.** (intermediate) Why did it matter that all the Cepheids Leavitt studied were in the same cloud?

??? note "Answer"
    They were all at roughly the same distance, so differences in how bright they looked were real differences in
    brightness. That let her see that the longer a Cepheid's period, the brighter it truly is.

**5.** (intermediate) In the Great Debate, what was Shapley right about and what was Curtis right about?

??? note "Answer"
    Shapley was right that the Sun is far from the centre of the Milky Way. Curtis was right that the spiral nebulae
    are separate galaxies. The true size of the Milky Way is between their numbers.

**6.** (intermediate) Andromeda is coming towards us. Does that go against an expanding universe?

??? note "Answer"
    No. Andromeda is close, and gravity between it and the Milky Way is stronger than the expansion at that distance.
    The expansion shows up for galaxies much farther away, which nearly all move away from us.

**7.** (advanced) A star has a parallax of 0.5″ seen from the Earth's orbit. How far away is it, and what parallax
would it show from Mars's orbit?

??? note "Answer"
    $1/0.5 = 2$ parsecs, or $2 \times 3.26 = 6.5$ light-years. From Mars: $1.524 \times 0.5 = 0.76$″.

**8.** (expert) A galaxy shows the red hydrogen line at 678.2 nm instead of 656.3 nm. Using $H_0 = 70$ km/s per Mpc,
how far away is it, and how long has its light been travelling, roughly?

??? note "Answer"
    $v = 299{,}792 \times (678.2 - 656.3)/656.3 = 299{,}792 \times 0.0334 = 10{,}000$ km/s. Then
    $d = 10{,}000 / 70 = 143$ Mpc, or $143 \times 3.26 = 466$ million light-years, so its light set out roughly
    470 million years ago. (At these distances the simple formulas are still close; much farther out they are not.)

## Summary

- Peoples everywhere named the Milky Way; Galileo showed it is made of stars, and Wright and Kant suggested it is a
  disc and one of many.
- The first star distances were measured in the 1830s at the Cape, Dorpat and Königsberg.
- Leavitt, using plates from Peru, found in 1912 that a Cepheid's period gives its true brightness, and so its
  distance.
- In 1920 Shapley and Curtis debated the size of the Milky Way and the nature of the spirals; each was partly right.
- Hubble's Cepheids showed in 1924 that Andromeda is another galaxy, now put at 2.5 million light-years.
- Slipher's redshifts, Friedmann's and Lemaître's theory and Hubble's law of 1929 showed that the universe is
  expanding; the exact rate is still argued about.
- From Mars the galaxies look the same, but parallaxes are 1.52 times bigger.
