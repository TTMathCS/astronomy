# 2. Mesopotamia

*Between the Tigris and the Euphrates, in what is now Iraq, people began writing on clay more than 5,000 years ago.
In time the sky was written down too: the evening the new Moon first showed, the morning
Venus vanished into the dawn, the night an eclipse darkened the Moon and what it meant for the king. From about 650 BC
observers in Babylon kept a diary of the sky, night after night, for six hundred years. Out of those records came a
cycle that predicted eclipses, a calendar that kept months and years in step, the twelve signs of the zodiac, and
columns of numbers that worked out where the Moon and planets would be. We still count degrees, minutes and seconds
in sixties because of them.*

## 2.1 Clay, wedges and sixties { #clay }

Mesopotamia is Greek for "between the rivers". The Tigris and the Euphrates run from the mountains of Turkey down
through Iraq to the Persian Gulf, and the flat land between them was home to some of the first cities: Uruk, Ur,
Nippur, Babylon, and further north Assur and Nineveh. Over three thousand years the region was ruled by Sumerians,
Akkadians, Babylonians, Assyrians, Persians, Greeks and Parthians, and the astronomy carried on through all of them.

**Writing on clay.** The oldest writing found there, called proto-cuneiform, dates from about 3300 BC, and most of
it is bookkeeping: grain, sheep, workers. A scribe pressed the end of a cut reed into a pad of damp clay, which left
wedge-shaped marks. The script is called **cuneiform**, from the Latin *cuneus*, a wedge. Clay was the great luck of
Mesopotamian history. Paper and bamboo rot; a tablet buried in a ruin lasts for thousands of years, and a fire that
burns a library only bakes its tablets harder.

The astronomy was written in cuneiform to the very end. The last cuneiform tablet that can be dated is an
astronomical almanac from Uruk, written in AD 79 or 80. Most of the known astronomical tablets were bought by the
British Museum in the late nineteenth century, some 2,000 of them, nearly all broken. Specialists estimate that fewer
than one in ten of the original records has survived.

**Counting in sixties.** The Babylonians took over from the Sumerians a number system with base 60. Like ours it was
a **place-value** system: the same sign means a different amount depending on where it stands. Where we have ten
digits, they built every number from 1 to 59 out of just two signs, a wedge for one and a corner mark for ten, and
then used places for sixties, sixty-sixties and so on. There was no sign for zero at first. Context told a reader
whether a wedge meant 1 or 60; later scribes added a sign for an empty place.

Historians write these numbers with commas between the places and a semicolon where we would put a decimal point:

- $1{,}30$ means $1 \times 60 + 30 = 90$
- $1;30$ means $1 + \tfrac{30}{60} = 1.5$
- $0;12$ means $\tfrac{12}{60} = 0.2$
- $0;9{,}30$ means $\tfrac{9}{60} + \tfrac{30}{3600} = 0.158\,33\ldots$

The tablet itself showed none of this punctuation: the scribe knew from the problem where the units were.

**Why sixty is handy.** Sixty divides exactly by 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 10, 12, 15, 20 and 30. A third is $0;20$, a quarter
is $0;15$, a sixth is $0;10$, all exact. In decimals a third never ends. For astronomers, who divide circles and days
into pieces all the time, that was a real gain. Greek astronomers took over the Babylonian way of writing fractions,
and so did Ptolemy, whose great book of the second century AD gives its fractions of a degree in sixties. From the
Greeks it passed to the astronomers of the Islamic world and of Europe. That is why an hour has 60 minutes, a minute
60 seconds, and why a degree is split into 60 minutes of arc and each of those into 60 seconds of arc. The 360
degrees of a full turn go back to the Babylonian zodiac of twelve signs of 30 (section 2.9).

## 2.2 A month that begins with a crescent { #month }

In Babylon, as everywhere in the ancient Near East except Egypt, the day began at sunset, and the month began on
the evening when the thin crescent of the new Moon was first seen low in the west after sunset. The months were named
Nisannu, Aiaru, Simanu, Du'uzu, Abu, Ululu, Tashritu, Arahsamna, Kislimu, Tebetu, Shabatu and Addaru, and the year
began with Nisannu, close to the spring equinox.

Nobody could simply count 29.5 days and declare a new month. The time from one new Moon to the next wanders between
about 29.3 and 29.8 days, and whether the crescent can be seen on a given evening depends on how far it has moved
from the Sun, on the angle of its path to the horizon, and on the weather. So a month had 29 days or 30, and the
only way to know which was to look. If the crescent did not show on the evening after day 29, day 30 was counted and
the new month began the next evening anyway.

**The eleven-day problem.** Chapter 1 met it already: twelve lunar months make about 354 days, eleven short of the
year. A calendar of twelve months only, like the Islamic calendar today, moves through the seasons, with the month
of the barley harvest coming eleven days earlier each year. For a farming state that is a nuisance: festivals, rents
and harvest taxes are tied to the seasons.

The Babylonian answer was a **thirteenth month**. Every two or three years a second Addaru (or, less often, a second
Ululu) was added. For most of Babylonian history this was done by royal order. Letters and documents from the reigns
of Hammurapi in the eighteenth century BC, and of Nabonidus, Cyrus and Cambyses in the sixth, show the king or his
officials announcing an extra month. Scholars advised; the king decided.

**A calendar that is still used.** The Jewish months are called Nisan, Iyar, Sivan, Tammuz, Av, Elul, Tishri,
Heshvan, Kislev, Tevet, Shevat and Adar. These are the Babylonian names, taken over by the Judeans during and after
the exile in Babylon in the sixth century BC; a passage of the Jerusalem Talmud says they "carried the names of the
months back with them from Babylonia". The extra month of the Jewish leap year is still a second Adar.

**Working it out: how fast does the calendar drift?**

| | Days |
|---|---|
| One lunar month (average) | 29.5306 |
| Twelve months | $12 \times 29.5306 = 354.367$ |
| The year (equinox to equinox) | 365.242 |
| Shortfall each year | $365.242 - 354.367 = 10.875$ |

How long until a month has drifted all the way round the seasons? The shortfall has to add up to a whole year:
$365.242 / 10.875 = 33.6$ lunar years. In days that is $33.6 \times 354.367 \approx 11{,}900$, or about 32.6 real
years. The Middle Assyrian calendar did exactly this: it had no extra months until the reign of Tiglath-pileser I,
around 1100 BC, and its months turned through the seasons about every 32½ years.

## 2.3 MUL.APIN, a handbook of the sky { #mulapin }

The oldest known handbook of astronomy fills two clay tablets. Like most Mesopotamian texts it is named after its
first words, MUL.APIN, "the Plough star", the first constellation of its first list. One copy is unusually large,
about the size of a sheet of paper, with the text in two columns.

**What it contains.** The first tablet describes the fixed sky:

- a catalogue of about seventy stars and constellations, sorted into three bands: the "path of Enlil" in the north,
  the "path of Anu" around the middle (roughly the sky's equator) and the "path of Ea" in the south
- the dates in the year when 34 stars and constellations first rise in the dawn
- which stars rise as others set, and the number of days between one rising and the next
- the stars that pass overhead, used to tell the time at night
- the constellations "in the path of the Moon", the band that the Moon, Sun and planets move along. Many of them are
  the ancestors of the zodiac signs: the Bull, the Twins, the Lion, the Scorpion.

The second tablet is about change. It says that the Sun and the planets travel the same path as the Moon. It gives
rough figures for how long each planet is visible and hidden, rules for when to add an extra month, the length of
day and night through the year, the length of a stick's shadow at different hours, a scheme for a water clock, and
a set of omens.

**First rising in the dawn.** Many of its dates use the **heliacal rising** of a star: the first morning it can be
seen low in the east just before sunrise, after weeks lost in the Sun's glare. Each star does this on about the same
date every year, which makes it a natural calendar mark, as the Egyptians found too (chapter 3).

**A star rule for the extra month.** One rule uses the Pleiades. They should first rise in the dawn on the first day
of the second month, Aiaru. If they first rise in the third month instead, the calendar has fallen behind the Sun,
and the year needs an extra month. It is a neat idea: check the Moon calendar against a star, which keeps step with
the Sun. But the leap years that actually appear in Babylonian records do not follow such rules, so they seem to have
been a scholar's scheme rather than a working law.

**An ideal year.** Like most Babylonian sky texts, MUL.APIN uses a tidy model year of twelve months of 30 days, 360
days in all, with the equinoxes on the 15th of the first and seventh months and the solstices on the 15th of the
fourth and tenth. It was a tool for thinking, not the calendar people lived by.

**How old is it?** The oldest dated copy was written in 686 BC; the latest are from around 300 BC. Most specialists
think the text was put together around 1000 BC from older lists. Two astronomers have tried to date the star
observations behind it from the rising dates it gives: Bradley Schaefer found 1370 BC, give or take a century, at the
latitude of Assur, and Teije de Jong found between 1400 and 1100 BC. Older claims of 2300 BC are no longer accepted.
The lists may also be of different ages, since some are written in a much simpler style than others.

**Working it out: why rising dates carry a date.** The Earth's axis wobbles slowly, like a spinning top, through one
full circle in about 26,000 years. This **precession** shifts each star's place against the seasons by
$360° / 26{,}000 \approx 1°$ every 72 years. The Sun moves about 1° a day along its path, so a star's first rising
moves about one day later in the year every 72 years. A list that is ten days off for 300 BC was made about 700 years
earlier. The method is real but rough: the old observers could misjudge a first rising by days, which is why the
answers come with error bars of a century or more.

## 2.4 Omens for the king { #omens }

Why would a state pay people to watch the sky every night? In Mesopotamia the answer was **omens**. The gods were
thought to write warnings in the world: in the organs of a sacrificed sheep, in events on the ground, and in the sky.
Reading the sky well could warn the king of danger in time to do something about it.

**The great omen series.** The main reference work was a collection of 68 or 70 tablets called *Enuma Anu Enlil*,
"When Anu and Enlil", from its first words. It holds some 6,500 to 7,000 omens, each in the same form: *if* this is
seen in the sky, *then* this will happen on Earth. It runs in a fixed order:

| Tablets | Subject |
|---|---|
| 1 to 22 | the Moon: its first crescent, haloes, its meetings with planets; 15 to 22 are lunar eclipses |
| 23 to 39 | the Sun: its colour at rising, clouds; 30 to 39 are solar eclipses |
| 40 to 49 | weather: thunder, rain, earthquakes |
| 50 to 70 | the stars and planets |

A typical omen: if the Moon is seen on the first day of the month, "the land will be happy". If an eclipse happens
in a certain month and watch of the night, with the shadow on a certain side of the Moon, then a particular country
or king is in danger. The series was probably put into its standard form in the Kassite period (about 1595–1157 BC),
with older roots, and was still being copied in 194 BC.

**Scholars, not fortune-tellers.** In the seventh century BC the kings of Assyria received a steady stream of reports
from scholars in Assyrian and Babylonian cities. More than 560 survive. A report quotes the omens that matched what
was seen and adds a short comment for the king. The men who sent them are often called astrologers, but that is
misleading. They did not cast horoscopes, which had not yet been invented, and they watched the land as well as the
sky. Many of them were also exorcists, physicians or diviners, and reporting on the sky was one of several jobs.

The letters show a working network that checked itself. One scholar wrote twice to King Assurbanipal that he had seen
a lunar eclipse through cloud. In another letter, the king is advised to have a report from Kalhu checked against the
observations made at Assur and Arbela. Three reports, from around 680 to 650 BC, describe what may be aurorae. They are
the oldest datable records of the aurora anywhere, and they fit other evidence of strong solar activity around
660 BC.

**Why it matters.** The motive was religious and political, but it forced the watchers to do what science needs:
watch every night, write down what they saw in a fixed form, compare notes between cities, and keep the records. In
time, as section 2.6 shows, those records became data.

The omen series also travelled. David Pingree argued that part of it reached India by the fourth or third century
BC, and parts of its methods can be traced in early Indian texts. Chapter 5 takes up the question.

## 2.5 The Venus tablet of Ammi-saduqa { #venus }

Tablet 63 of *Enuma Anu Enlil* is different from the rest. Instead of general omens it lists, year by year for 21
years, the dates on which Venus vanished in the evening or morning twilight and the dates on which it reappeared, each
paired with an omen. The dates are given in the lunar calendar: month and day.

Its origin is a puzzle in itself. The copies were written in the first millennium BC; the best known, K.160 in the
British Museum, comes from the library of Nineveh and was copied in the seventh century BC. About 20 copies are
known, some only fragments, and the oldest, found at Kish, was copied from a Babylon tablet of the reign of Sargon II
of Assyria (720–704 BC). But one line names the eighth year of a king by its year-name, "the year of the golden
throne", which the scholar Franz Kugler matched in 1912 to Ammi-saduqa, the fourth king after Hammurapi. So the
observations themselves seem to be a thousand years older than the copies: the oldest known long run of planet
sightings.

**A tablet that dates a whole era.** Historians of the ancient Near East have long hoped that the tablet could fix
the dates of the Old Babylonian kings, Hammurapi included. If we know the Moon and Venus well enough, we should be able
to find the year in which Venus behaved exactly as the tablet says. The trouble is that several years fit. Scholars
have argued for starting dates of 1702, 1646 and 1582 BC, among others, and these match the rival "high", "middle" and
"low" chronologies of the period. The middle date is the one most often used, but the argument is not over. The
copies also contain errors: the entries for some years describe things Venus cannot do, and they are usually taken as
mistakes in copying.

**Working it out: why several dates fit.** Venus takes 583.92 days to go round its cycle as seen from Earth (from one
evening appearance to the next). Compare whole numbers of Venus cycles with whole numbers of lunar months:

| Venus cycles | Days | Lunar months | Days | Difference |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 5 | 2,919.6 | 99 | 2,923.5 | 3.9 days |
| 35 | 20,437.2 | 692 | 20,435.2 | 2.0 days |
| 40 | 23,356.8 | 791 | 23,358.7 | 1.9 days |

Five Venus cycles are almost exactly eight years, so Venus repeats its pattern every eight years, landing about four
days earlier in the lunar month each time. After 692 months, which is 56 years, or 791 months, which is 64 years, the
pattern lands on almost the same lunar dates as before. So if one starting year fits the tablet, a year 56 or 64 years
earlier or later fits nearly as well. Look at the candidates: $1702 - 1646 = 56$ and $1646 - 1582 = 64$. To choose
between them, historians have to bring in other kinds of evidence.

## 2.6 The Astronomical Diaries { #diaries }

From the middle of the seventh century BC until the first century BC, observers in Babylon wrote down what they saw
in the sky, every night they could. The Babylonians called this
*nasaru sha gine*, "regular watching". Modern scholars call the tablets the **Astronomical Diaries**.

More than 1,000 tablets and fragments survive, from about 650 to 60 BC; the earliest dated one is from 652 BC and the
latest from 61 BC. That is perhaps a tenth of what was written, or less. Tablets from late in the period show that
the watchers came from families of scholars paid by the assembly of the Esangil, the great temple of Marduk in Babylon.

**What a diary records.** Night by night, the Moon's place among the stars, the planets, eclipses, comets and other
sights, and the weather. Then, at the end of each month, things that are not astronomy at all: the prices in Babylon of barley and other
foods and of wool, the level of the Euphrates, and often news of the day.

This makes the Diaries the most important source for the history of Babylon under the Persian kings and Alexander's
successors. One entry describes the battle of Gaugamela, where Alexander the Great defeated the Persian king in 331
BC, and Alexander's entry into Babylon afterwards. Another describes the preparations for war between the Seleucid
and Ptolemaic kings in 274/3 BC.

**A note on dates.** The online edition calls the Gaugamela diary "−330". Astronomers count years with a year 0, so
that 1 BC is year 0, 2 BC is year −1, and 331 BC is year −330. Historians have no year 0. The two systems differ by
one for every year BC, and it matters when you check an old eclipse against a modern calculation.

**Some highlights.**

- **Halley's comet** appears in texts of 164 BC and 87 BC. They help pin down the comet's orbit in the distant past.
- **Aurorae.** A search of the Diaries found nine records of glows in the sky, five of them likely aurorae.
- **Prices.** The month-end prices make the Diaries a rare record of the economy of an ancient city.

**Why it matters.** The Diaries are the largest body of observations made by anyone in the ancient world. Their
purpose was probably to collect the experience needed to predict the sky, and through it the omens. Because the
watching never stopped, the records covered enough years for the patterns to show: which brings us to eclipses.

## 2.7 The saros: eclipses come back { #saros }

An eclipse of the Moon happens when the full Moon passes through the Earth's shadow; an eclipse of the Sun when the
new Moon passes in front of the Sun. If the Moon's orbit lay flat in the plane of the Earth's orbit, that would
happen every month. It does not, because the Moon's orbit is tilted by about 5°. Most full Moons pass above or below
the shadow. An eclipse can only happen when a full or new Moon falls near one of the two **nodes**, the points where
the Moon's tilted path crosses the Sun's.

**Six months, sometimes five.** The Sun passes a node about every 173 days, so the chances for an eclipse come in
"seasons" about six lunar months apart (now and then five). A watcher who logs lunar eclipses for a few decades will
see this six-month rhythm at once.

**The 18-year cycle.** The real discovery is a longer cycle. After 223 lunar months, eclipses come back in nearly the
same order and of nearly the same kind. The Babylonians knew this period and used it to predict lunar eclipses; from the late fifth century BC, old eclipse
records were collected in tables arranged by it.
Their texts give many predictions, often of the time at which an eclipse would begin. The period is now called the
**saros**, a name it got by mistake: in 1691 Edmond Halley took the word from a Byzantine dictionary, the *Suda*, which
wrongly tied a Babylonian number word, *sar* (3,600), to this cycle. The name stuck.

**Working it out: why 223 months?** Three different months matter for eclipses:

| Kind of month | What repeats | Length (days) | How many | Total (days) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Synodic | the phase: full Moon to full Moon | 29.530589 | 223 | 6,585.32 |
| Draconic | the Moon's return to the same node | 27.212221 | 242 | 6,585.36 |
| Anomalistic | the Moon's return to its nearest point to Earth | 27.554550 | 239 | 6,585.54 |

All three totals agree to within a quarter of a day. So after 223 months the full Moon is back at the same node (so
there is again an eclipse) and the Moon is again at the same distance from Earth (so the eclipse has the same size and
length). Within one saros there are 38 eclipse chances: $33 \times 6 + 5 \times 5 = 223$, thirty-three gaps of six
months and five of five.

The saros is 6,585.32 days, which is 18 years, 11 days and about 8 hours (10 days if five leap days fall inside). The
extra third of a day matters. In 0.32 of a day the Earth turns about $0.32 \times 360° \approx 116°$, so the next
eclipse in a series is seen about a third of the way round the world to the west. For a lunar eclipse, seen from
the whole night side of the Earth, that may still mean Babylon sees it, or not, if it is daytime there. For a solar
eclipse, seen only along a narrow track, it means a different country. That is why solar eclipses were much harder for
the Babylonians: the cycle could flag a possible eclipse, but not whether it would be seen from Babylon.

so a 19-year cycle needs $235 - 228 = 7$ extra months. In the Babylonian scheme they fell in years 1, 4, 7, 9, 12,
15 and 18 of each cycle: a second Ululu in year 18 and a second Addaru in the other six.

**When it began.** Before about 500 BC the extra months came when the king ordered them, in no fixed pattern. From
497 BC, in the reign of the Persian king Darius I, the leap years follow the 19-year pattern with only two
exceptions, and from 380 BC, under Artaxerxes II, the cycle was the fixed rule. It stayed in use until the end of
cuneiform writing. In Athens, the astronomer Meton proposed a 19-year cycle in 432 BC, which is why it is often called
the **Metonic cycle**. Whether Meton learned of it from Babylon or found it himself is not known; chapter 7 returns
to him.

The cycle is still running. The Jewish calendar puts its seven leap years in years 3, 6, 8, 11, 14, 17 and 19 of a
19-year cycle. The gaps between those years (3, 2, 3, 3, 3, 2, 3) are the same as the gaps in the Babylonian list,
just starting at a different point. The date of Easter is also worked out with a 19-year lunar cycle.

**Working it out: how good is it?**

$$19 \times 365.2422 = 6{,}939.60 \text{ days}, \qquad 235 \times 29.530589 = 6{,}939.69 \text{ days}.$$

The difference is 0.09 days, about 2 hours in 19 years. The months run ahead of the seasons by one day only every
$19 / 0.0866 \approx 220$ years. For people with nothing but their eyes, a clay tablet and patience, that is superb.

**And on Mars?** A Moon calendar works on Earth because our Moon's cycle of phases is a convenient twelfth or so of the
year. Mars has two moons, and neither helps:

| | Mars | Earth |
|---|---|---|
| Cycle of phases of the moon | Phobos 7.66 hours, Deimos 30.4 hours | 29.53 days |
| Cycles in one year | Phobos about 2,150, Deimos about 540 | 12.37 |
| Length of the year | 686.97 Earth days | 365.24 days |

A month of 30 hours is no use for a calendar. Anyone who plans a calendar for Mars has to build months out of sols,
with no moon to check them against.

## 2.9 Twelve equal signs { #zodiac }

MUL.APIN's "path of the Moon" was a string of constellations of all sizes, some long and some small, like the road
signs on a winding road. For measuring positions that is awkward. Around 400 BC, Babylonian astronomers replaced it
with something new: twelve **signs** of exactly 30 degrees each, named after the constellations they started from:
the Farm Worker (our Aries), the Bull of Heaven, the Great Twins, the Crayfish, the Lion, the Furrow (Virgo), the
Scales, the Scorpion, Pabilsag (Sagittarius), the Goat-Fish (Capricorn), the Great One (Aquarius) and the Tail of
the Swallow (Pisces). A position could
now be given as a number: "Jupiter at 18 degrees of the Lion."

This is the **zodiac**, and it was the first coordinate system for the sky. Careful study of where the Moon and
planets stand in the dated texts shows that it was brought in between 409 and 398 BC, under Persian rule, and
probably within a few years of 401 BC. The twelve equal signs copy the twelve ideal months of 30 days, and the 360
degrees of the circle come with them.

The Babylonian signs were fixed to the stars. Western astrology later started the signs from the spring equinox
instead. Because of precession (section 2.3) the two kinds of zodiac slowly drift apart. Hindu astrology still
uses a zodiac fixed to the stars, and today the two differ by about 24 degrees: the Sun enters Aries around 21 March
in the Western zodiac but around 14 April in the Hindu one.

**The first horoscopes.** Once positions could be stated in signs and degrees, a new kind of text appeared: a list of
where the Moon, the Sun and the planets were on the day a child was born. The oldest known Babylonian horoscope is for
a birth in 410 BC. Only a few dozen survive. They are short and mostly state the positions, with brief predictions.
From Babylon the idea spread to the Greek world, Egypt and India, and grew into the astrology of later centuries.
Astronomy and astrology were still one subject; separating them took another two thousand years.

## 2.10 Tables that predict the sky { #tables }

In the last four centuries BC, under the Persian and then the Seleucid kings, the scholars of Babylon and Uruk went
beyond watching and cycles. They built **mathematical astronomy**: methods that compute, from a few starting numbers,
where the Moon and planets will be on any date.

The results are tablets called **ephemerides**: tables in columns, one line per month or per event. One column gives
the date, others the position in the zodiac, the speed, the length of daylight, the size of an eclipse, and so on.
Each column is built from the one before by simple rules: add a fixed amount each line; or let the amount rise
steadily to a maximum and fall to a minimum, a **zigzag**; or use one speed in one part of the zodiac and another speed
in another, a **step function**. Modern scholars sort the methods into two families, **System A**, built mostly on step
functions, and **System B**, built mostly on zigzags. Separate **procedure texts** explain the rules.

These tables use no picture of the heavens, no circles or spheres. They are arithmetic in base 60 (section 2.11
has the one known use of geometry), with periods and starting values that were probably tuned on centuries of
Diaries. That is their power: they predict well without saying
why.

**Names.** Most of these scholars are unknown. Two names survive in Greek writers. The geographer Strabo lists
Kidenas and Naburianos among the famous Chaldean astronomers, and Babylonian tablets name a Kidinnu and a
Naburimannu. Colophons of two System B Moon tables call them the *tersitu* of Kidinnu, a word whose meaning is
uncertain. An ancient note credits Kidenas with the discovery that 251 lunar months equal 269 returns of the Moon to
its closest point. A damaged Babylonian chronicle says that "Kidinnu" was killed by the sword in the summer of 330 BC,
a few months after Alexander took Babylon; whether this is the same man is not known.

**Working it out: how good were the numbers?**

*The month.* The length of the month used in System B is $29;31{,}50{,}8{,}20$ days. In decimals:

$$29 + \frac{31}{60} + \frac{50}{60^2} + \frac{8}{60^3} + \frac{20}{60^4} = 29.530\,594 \text{ days}.$$

The modern average is 29.530589 days. The difference is 0.000005 days, under half a second a month.

*Kidinnu's relation.* $251 \times 29.530589 = 7{,}412.18$ days, and $269 \times 27.554550 = 7{,}412.17$ days. They
agree to a hundredth of a day. With this relation one can follow the Moon's changing speed (fast near Earth, slow far
away) month by month, which is what an eclipse time prediction needs.

**What came next.** Greek astronomers used Babylonian period relations and old Babylonian eclipse records: Ptolemy, in
the second century AD, discusses the Chaldeans' Moon periods and how Hipparchus improved them. Strabo also names
Seleucus of Seleucia among the Chaldean astronomers, and Plutarch reports that he argued the Earth goes round the Sun,
one of the very few ancient astronomers known to have backed Aristarchus's idea (chapter 7).

## 2.11 Jupiter and the area of a trapezoid { #jupiter }

In 2016 the historian Mathieu Ossendrijver published a reading of five small tablets in the British Museum, written
in Babylon between about 350 and 50 BC. They had been in the museum since the late nineteenth century. One of them,
BM 40054, had been filed as a maths exercise. Ossendrijver saw that it did the same calculation as four other tablets,
two of which name Jupiter, and that together they compute how far Jupiter moves.

**The calculation.** After Jupiter first appears in the dawn, it moves eastward against the stars, slowing down, until
it stops and turns back (its **station**). The tablet takes Jupiter's daily movement as:

- $0;12$ degrees a day on the day it first appears
- falling evenly to $0;9{,}30$ degrees a day after 60 days
- then falling evenly to $0;1{,}30$ degrees a day after another 60 days.

To get the distance in the first 60 days it adds the first and last speeds, halves the sum and multiplies by 60:

$$\frac{0;12 + 0;9{,}30}{2} \times 60 = 0;10{,}45 \times 60 = 10;45 \text{ degrees}.$$

The second stretch is $\dfrac{0;9{,}30 + 0;1{,}30}{2} \times 60 = 5;30$ degrees, and the total to the station is
$10;45 + 5;30 = 16;15$ degrees.

**What is new here.** That arithmetic alone would not be surprising. What Ossendrijver showed is that four of the
tablets describe it as a figure: a trapezoid whose width is the time and whose sides are the speeds, with the
distance as its area. Then they go further. To find when Jupiter has covered half the distance, they cut the trapezoid
into two smaller trapezoids of equal area. The cut falls a little before day 30, because Jupiter moves faster at the
start.

This is the idea that distance is the area under a speed-time graph, which every physics student learns. In Europe it
first appears in the fourteenth century, with the "Oxford Calculators" of Merton College and a scholar working with
them in Paris. The
Babylonian tablets are about 1,400 years older. Some newspapers called it "calculus"; it is not, since there is no
idea of a limit or a rate of change, but it is a real use of geometry in a field that had been thought to be purely
arithmetic.

**Working it out: where is the halfway cut?** The speed falls from $a = 0.2°$ to $b = 0.158\,33°$ a day in 60 days.
A line that cuts a trapezoid with parallel sides $a$ and $b$ into two pieces of equal area has length

$$m = \sqrt{\frac{a^2 + b^2}{2}}.$$

(Each piece's area is its width times the average of its two sides, and the widths are in proportion to how much the
speed drops, so the condition $a^2 - m^2 = m^2 - b^2$ falls out.) Here $m = \sqrt{(0.04 + 0.025\,07)/2} = 0.180\,37°$
a day. The speed drops $0.041\,67°$ in 60 days, so it reaches $m$ after

$$t = \frac{0.2 - 0.180\,37}{0.041\,67} \times 60 = 28.26 \text{ days},$$

about $28;15$ days in base 60: a little before day 30.

## 2.12 Babylon's clock and the slowing Earth { #rotation }

The Babylonian records are not only history. Geophysicists use them today to measure something no modern instrument
can: how the Earth's spin has changed over thousands of years.

**The idea.** The Moon's tides act as a brake on the Earth, so the day gets very slowly longer. Today's clocks and
orbit calculations tell us exactly where the Sun and Moon were at any moment in the past, measured in a steady time
scale. But the Earth's turning is not steady, so that moment did not happen at the clock time on the ground that a
steady Earth would give. The difference is called **ΔT** ("delta T"). An eclipse timed by an observer in Babylon,
together with a modern calculation of when it really happened, gives ΔT for that date.

Babylonian astronomers timed the beginning and end of eclipses, and often the middle, from the eighth century BC
until perhaps the first century AD. Their lunar eclipse records run from before 700 BC; the solar ones from about
350 BC. Chinese, Greek and medieval Arab records continue the series; medieval Europe contributed
almost no timed eclipses.

**The result.** In 2016 Richard Stephenson, Leslie Morrison and Catherine Hohenkerk combined these with later records
and found that the length of the day has grown by about 1.8 thousandths of a second per century on average. Tidal
braking alone should give 2.3; something else, thought to be the slow rebound of the land since the ice sheets melted and
the coupling between the Earth's core and mantle, makes up the difference. Their fit for ΔT over 2,700 years is

$$\Delta T = -320 + 32.5 \left( \frac{\text{year} - 1825}{100} \right)^2 \text{ seconds}.$$

**Working it out: an eclipse of 700 BC.** In astronomers' numbering, 700 BC is year −699.

$$\frac{-699 - 1825}{100} = -25.24, \qquad 32.5 \times 25.24^2 = 20{,}705, \qquad \Delta T \approx 20{,}385 \text{ s}.$$

That is 5.66 hours. If we assumed the Earth had always turned at today's rate, we would put the eclipse at the wrong
clock time by almost six hours. In that time the Earth turns $5.66 \times 15° = 85°$, so the track of a solar
eclipse would be drawn a quarter of the way round the world from where it really fell. Yet the day itself was only
about $1.78 \times 25.24 \approx 45$ thousandths of a second shorter then. A tiny difference each day, added over some
920,000 days, becomes six hours; and the only way to see it is to have a record made on the day.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Write $0;45$ and $1;20$ as decimals. Write $2.75$ in base 60.

??? note "Answer"
    $0;45 = 45/60 = 0.75$. $1;20 = 1 + 20/60 = 1.333\ldots$. And $2.75 = 2 + 45/60$, so it is $2;45$.

**2.** (beginner) Why did the Babylonian calendar sometimes need a thirteenth month, and who decided when, before
about 500 BC?

??? note "Answer"
    Twelve lunar months are about 354 days, eleven short of the year, so without a fix the months drift through the
    seasons. An extra month every two or three years pulls them back. Before about 500 BC the king ordered it, on his
    scholars' advice.

**3.** (beginner) Besides the sky, what did the Astronomical Diaries record?

??? note "Answer"
    The weather, the prices of barley and other foods and of wool in Babylon, the level of the Euphrates, and news of
    events such as the battle of Gaugamela.

**4.** (intermediate) Why can the Venus tablet fit several different dates for King Ammi-saduqa?

??? note "Answer"
    The pattern of Venus's appearances, counted in lunar months, nearly repeats after 56 years (35 Venus cycles and
    692 months) and after 64 years (40 Venus cycles and 791 months). A starting year that fits the tablet is matched
    almost as well by years 56 or 64 years away. That is why the candidate dates differ by those amounts.

**5.** (intermediate) Show that 19 years are close to 235 months, and explain why the cycle needs 7 extra months.

??? note "Answer"
    $19 \times 365.2422 = 6{,}939.60$ days and $235 \times 29.5306 = 6{,}939.69$ days, only about two hours apart.
    Nineteen ordinary years of 12 months are $228$ months, so $235 - 228 = 7$ months must be added.

**6.** (advanced) A solar eclipse is seen in a certain place. Using the saros, after how long will the "same" eclipse
come back to roughly the same longitude?

??? note "Answer"
    Each saros is 6,585.32 days, so each eclipse in the series falls $0.32 \times 360° \approx 116°$ further west.
    After three saroses the shift is $0.96$ of a turn, about $347°$, so the eclipse is back within about $13°$ of the
    same longitude. Three saroses are $19{,}756$ days, about 54 years and a month. (The Greeks called this period
    the *exeligmos*. The latitude also shifts, so it is the same series but not the same place.)

**7.** (advanced) Check the tablet's second stretch. Jupiter slows from $0;9{,}30$ to $0;1{,}30$ degrees a day over 60
days. How far does it move, and how far in the full 120 days?

??? note "Answer"
    The average speed is $(0;9{,}30 + 0;1{,}30)/2 = 0;11/2 = 0;5{,}30$ degrees a day, and $0;5{,}30 \times 60 = 5;30$
    degrees. The total is $10;45 + 5;30 = 16;15$ degrees, that is $16.25°$.

**8.** (expert) Use the formula of section 2.12 to find ΔT for 500 BC. By how many degrees of longitude would a
solar eclipse track be misplaced if we ignored it? Why can tidal braking alone not explain the observed ΔT?

    500 BC is year −499. $(-499 - 1825)/100 = -23.24$, and $32.5 \times 23.24^2 = 17{,}553$, so

## Summary

- Mesopotamian scholars wrote on clay in cuneiform, from about 3300 BC until the last astronomical tablet in AD 79/80,
  and counted in base 60, which is why we have 60 minutes, 60 seconds and 360 degrees.
- The month began with the first crescent, and twelve months fell 11 days short of the year. For most of their
  history the king added a thirteenth month when needed; the Jewish calendar still uses the Babylonian month names.
- MUL.APIN, compiled about 1000 BC, is the oldest known handbook of the sky: star lists, first risings, the path of the
  Moon, planet rules and calendar rules.
- *Enuma Anu Enlil*, a series of about 7,000 sky omens, gave the reason to watch. Scholars reported to the kings of
  Assyria and checked each other's sightings. The Venus tablet records 21 years of Venus, probably from the reign of
  Ammi-saduqa; its date is still argued over because the pattern repeats after 56 or 64 years.
- The Astronomical Diaries, 652 to 61 BC, are the largest body of ancient observations: Moon, planets, weather, prices,
  the river and the news.
- The saros of 223 months (18 years and 11 days) let Babylon predict lunar eclipses. The 19-year cycle of 235 months,
  the fixed rule from 380 BC, kept months and years in step; it still runs in the Jewish calendar.
- The zodiac of twelve signs of 30° was introduced around 400 BC, and the first horoscopes followed.
- In the last centuries BC, Babylonian tables computed the Moon and planets by arithmetic, with a month length correct to
  under half a second. Some tablets found Jupiter's motion as the area of a trapezoid, 1,400 years before Europe.
- Babylonian eclipse timings still measure how the Earth's spin has slowed: ΔT was nearly 6 hours in 700 BC.
