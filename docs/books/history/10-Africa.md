# 10. Africa

*Africa's astronomy is older and wider than Egypt's. Earlier chapters met the Ishango bone, Nabta Playa, the Egyptian
calendar and the observatories of Cairo. This chapter goes south and west: stone pillars by Lake Turkana and a
calendar of the Moon and seven stars still used by the Borana; Ethiopia's own calendar and its ancient book of the

weighed from both sides. It ends with Africa's great telescopes today, and with the rocks from Mars that keep turning
up in the Sahara.*

## 10.1 A continent of calendars { #continent }

Africa is a big place, with thousands of peoples and languages, and its skies were read in many ways. Three kinds of
calendar are found there: **lunar** (counting Moons), **solar** (following the Sun and the seasons) and **stellar**
(watching when particular stars appear). Most African calendars combine them. Among the many with names are the Akan,
Berber, Ethiopian, Igbo, Yoruba, Shona, Somali, Swahili, Xhosa, Borana, Luba and Ankole calendars.

Earlier chapters of this book have already visited Africa: the notched bones of Border Cave and Ishango and the stone
circle of Nabta Playa (chapter 1), the whole of Egyptian astronomy (chapter 3), and Ibn Yunus, Ibn al-Haytham and
Ali ibn Ridwan in Cairo (chapter 8). This chapter covers the rest, as far as the records allow. Much African
astronomy lived in speech, not in writing, so a great deal of it is known only through what people still remember or
what visitors wrote down.

## 10.2 The stone pillars of Lake Turkana { #namoratunga }

On the west side of Lake Turkana in northern Kenya stands the **Nasura** (or **Kalokol**) **pillar site**, also known as
**Namoratunga II**. *Namoratunga* means "people of stone" in the Turkana language. It has 19 basalt pillars set inside a
ring of stones.

In 1978 the archaeologists Mark Lynch and L. H. Robbins published it in *Science* as "the first archeoastronomical
evidence in sub-Saharan Africa". They argued that lines through the pillars point to the rising places of seven stars
and star groups (Triangulum, the Pleiades, Aldebaran, Bellatrix, the middle of Orion, Saiph and Sirius), the same ones
used by the Borana calendar (section 10.3), and dated the site to about 300 BC.

Later work has not been kind to the idea:

- Other archaeologists re-analysed the alignments, and the astronomer and archaeologist **Clive
  Ruggles** questioned the link between the pillars and the Borana calendar.
- New excavations found a radiocarbon date of about **2400 BC**, two thousand years older than first thought, which
  makes a link with the Borana calendar much harder to argue.
- Several similar pillar sites around the lake, such as Lothagam North, turned out to be **cemeteries** built by the
  region's first herders.

So the pillars are real and important, but whether they were ever a calendar is, at best, unproven. In 2023 Kenya put
the site on its tentative list for UNESCO World Heritage.

## 10.3 The Borana calendar: the Moon and seven stars { #borana }

The **Borana Oromo** live in southern Ethiopia and northern Kenya. Their calendar is **lunar-stellar**: a year of 12
months of about 29.5 days, 354 days in all, in which months are found by watching the Moon beside particular stars.

- The year begins in the month **Bittottessa**, when the Moon is seen with **Triangulum**.
- Each of the next five months begins when the Moon is seen with the next star group in turn: the **Pleiades**,
  **Aldebaran**, **Bellatrix**, the middle of **Orion** with **Saiph**, and **Sirius**.
- The remaining six months are named after the shape of the Moon: full, gibbous, half and three sizes of crescent.
- There are no weeks. Instead each day of the month has one of **27 names**. A month is longer than 27 days, so the
  first two or three names are used twice, at the beginning and at the end of the month.

| Month | Star group | Borana name of the stars |
|---|---|---|
| Bittottessa | Triangulum | Lami |
| Camsa | Pleiades | Busan |
| Bufa | Aldebaran | Bakkalcha |
| Waxabajjii | Bellatrix | Algajima |
| Obora Gudda | middle of Orion, Saiph | Arb Gaddu |
| Obora Dikka | Sirius | Basa |

Because each month is checked against the sky, small errors are corrected as they happen, by a day or two here and
there. The astronomer Laurance Doyle re-examined how it works in 1986.

## 10.4 Ethiopia's calendar and its book of the heavens { #ethiopia }

**Thirteen months.** The Ethiopian calendar has **12 months of 30 days**, followed by a short thirteenth month,
**Pagume**, of 5 days, or 6 in a leap year. A leap year comes **every four years without exception**, as in the
Julian calendar (chapter 9, section 9.10). It is a close relative of the Coptic calendar of Egypt, and through it of the
ancient Egyptian civil year of 12 × 30 + 5 days (chapter 3, section 3.2).

The new year, **1 Meskerem**, falls on **11 September** (12 September in the year before a Gregorian leap year). Ethiopian
years are also numbered differently: from 11 September to 31 December an Ethiopian year number is **7 less** than the
Gregorian one, and from 1 January to 10 September it is **8 less**. That is because Ethiopia counts from a date for the
birth of Christ worked out by Annianus of Alexandria, not from Dionysius's (chapter 9, section 9.1). Ethiopia celebrated its millennium, the year 2000, on
11 September 2007.

**The Sea of Ideas.** The Ethiopian Orthodox Church works out Easter (*Fasika*) and its other feasts with the **Bahire
Hasab**, "Sea of Ideas", a computus built on the same 19-year lunar cycle used in Alexandria.

**The Book of Enoch.** The **Book of Enoch**, a Jewish religious book written in the last centuries BC, is part of the
Bible of the Ethiopian Orthodox Church. One part of it, chapters 72 to 82, is the **Astronomical Book**, which describes
the motions of the Sun and Moon and a year of **364 days** in four seasons of 91 days. Fragments of the early parts were
found among the Dead Sea Scrolls, but the **whole** book survives only in **Ge'ez**, the old language of Ethiopia. Europe
learned of it when the Scottish traveller James Bruce brought three Ge'ez copies home in 1773. So an ancient Jewish
book about the heavens was kept alive by Ethiopian scribes.

## 10.5 The manuscripts of Timbuktu { #timbuktu }

From the 14th to the 16th century, in Mali's golden age, **Timbuktu** was a great centre of Islamic learning, with
three famous mosque-schools (Sankore, Sidi Yahya and Djinguereber) and scribes who copied books from all over the
Islamic world. Hundreds of thousands of manuscripts survive.

Some of them are about the sky. A study of fourteen translated manuscripts found that their writers:

- used the Julian calendar alongside the Islamic one;
- held the usual Earth-centred view of the heavens, with diagrams of the planets and calculations;
- could find the direction of Mecca accurately;
- and recorded what they saw. The chronicler **Mahmud Kati** wrote of a meteor shower in August 1583, probably the
  Perseids: "after half the night had passed stars flew around as if fire had been kindled in the whole sky, east,
  west, north and south... It continued until after dawn."

One manuscript in the collections is a later copy of a work by Ibn Yunus of Cairo (chapter 8, section 8.3): astronomy
from Cairo reached Timbuktu.

Further east, **Muhammad al-Kashnawi** from Katsina, in today's northern Nigeria, was known for his mastery of magic
squares, arithmetic, calendars and astronomy, and died in Cairo in 1741.

In 2012–2013, when armed groups occupied Timbuktu, about **4,200 manuscripts** were burned or stolen. But Abdel Kader Haidara
and others smuggled about **350,000** to safety in Bamako, the capital; most were still there in 2022, and more than 150,000 have been photographed so that they can be read anywhere.

## 10.6 Stars and farming in southern Africa { #south }

In southern Africa the stars were a farming calendar.

- The **Pleiades**, called **isiLimela**, were the "digging stars".
  Their appearance warned that it would soon be time to start hoeing the fields.
- Among the **Venda**, the bright stars of the Southern Cross and its pointers were seen as giraffes, called *Thutlwa*,
  "rising above the trees". In October they skim the treetops on the evening horizon, a reminder to finish planting.
- **Canopus** was known to some peoples as the "ants' egg star", because it was prominent when ants' eggs, a food,
  were plentiful.
- In stories told in southern Africa, the **Milky Way** was made by a girl who threw the embers and ashes of a fire into
  the sky, making a road in the dark. To the Xhosa it looked like the bristles standing up on the back of an angry dog.

Among the Khoe-speaking herders the Moon had special importance in religion. Today African astronomers and historians
are gathering what people still remember (see Further reading).

## 10.7 The Dogon and Sirius: a claim tested { #dogon }

**The claim.** The **Dogon** live on the cliffs of Bandiagara in Mali. The French anthropologist **Marcel Griaule**
worked among them from the 1930s, with his colleague **Germaine Dieterlen**. They reported that the Dogon knew that
**Sirius** has a small, heavy companion star that circles it every **fifty years**, and that the Dogon *Sigui* ceremony,
held every sixty years, was linked to it.

That would be remarkable. The companion, **Sirius B**, is a white dwarf: far too faint to see beside Sirius without a
good telescope. Europe learned of it only in 1844, when Friedrich Bessel worked out from Sirius's wobbling path that an
unseen companion must be pulling it, and in 1862, when Alvan Graham Clark first saw it.

In 1976 Robert Temple's book *The Sirius Mystery* went further, claiming that the knowledge came from visitors from
another planet. That idea has no evidence behind it.

**The case against.** The anthropologist **Walter van Beek**, who studied the Dogon after Griaule, reported in 1991
that he found no knowledge of Sirius as a double star:

> Though they do speak about *sigu tolo* [what Griaule said was Sirius] they disagree completely with each other as to
> which star is meant; for some it is an invisible star that should rise to announce the *sigu*, for another it is
> Venus that, through a different position, appears as *sigu tolo*.

Van Beek suggested that Griaule's forceful, leading questions had drawn out answers that matched what he expected. The
astronomer Carl Sagan pointed out that the Dogon claim was first reported in the 1940s, long after Sirius B was famous,
and that the Dogon had had plenty of contact with Europeans who could have mentioned it; he also noted that the Dogon
stories, as reported, knew of no ringed planet beyond Saturn: just what a European of the time could have told them.

**The other side.** Griaule's daughter, Geneviève Calame-Griaule, and the anthropologist Luc de Heusch defended his
work, and called van Beek's criticism speculation that ignored the secret, esoteric side of Dogon tradition, which an
outsider might not be told.

**Where this leaves us.** Griaule's records of Dogon religion are a rich source, but the Sirius B story rests on his
reports alone, later fieldwork could not find it, and contact with Europeans explains it easily. The Dogon do not need
it: their real knowledge of their land, their crops and their sky is impressive enough without a mystery.

## 10.8 Africa's great telescopes { #telescopes }

The southern sky, which Europe hardly knew, was first mapped properly from Africa.

- From 1751 to 1752 the French astronomer **Nicolas-Louis de Lacaille** observed at the Cape of Good Hope almost every
  night for over a year and measured nearly **10,000 southern stars**. He also had to invent **14 new constellations**,
  still used today, among them Telescopium, Microscopium, Octans and Antlia.
- In 1820 Britain founded the **Royal Observatory at the Cape of Good Hope**, the first scientific institution in
  Africa. In 1972 it became part of the **South African Astronomical Observatory**, whose main telescopes now stand on a
  hilltop near **Sutherland**, in the dry Karoo.
- There, the **Southern African Large Telescope (SALT)**, with a mirror of 91 hexagonal segments measuring 11.1 by
  9.8 m, saw first light on 1 September 2005. It is the largest optical telescope in the southern hemisphere.
- In the Karoo too stands **MeerKAT**, 64 radio dishes each 13.5 m across. At its opening on 13 July 2018 it showed the
  most detailed radio image yet of the centre of our Milky Way, around its giant black hole. It is the forerunner of the
  **Square Kilometre Array**, which South Africa and Australia are building together (chapter 20).

## 10.9 Pieces of Mars in the Sahara { #mars }

Now and then a big impact on Mars blasts rocks off into space, and a few of them, after wandering around the Sun, fall
on Earth. Only a few hundred are known, and many of them were found in the Sahara.

- **Tissint.** At about 2 a.m. on 18 July 2011 people in the Drâa valley of Morocco saw a bright fireball. The stones they
  later found, 12 to 15 kg in all, were from Mars: only the fifth Mars meteorite ever **seen** to fall, and the first
  since 1962. It was knocked off Mars between about 0.7 and 1.1 million years ago.
- **Black Beauty** (NWA 7034), found by nomads in the Western Sahara in 2011, is a 320 g Martian rock with parts
  **4.4 billion years** old, and holds more water than any other Mars meteorite. **Teghaza 001**, found in Mali in 2022,
  is more than 4.1 billion years old.
- **NWA 16788**, found in the Agadez region of **Niger** on 16 July 2023, weighs 24.67 kg, the largest piece of Mars
  on Earth. It was sold at Sotheby's in New York on 16 July 2025 for **US$5.3 million**. Niger's government has questioned
  whether it left the country legally, raising the question of who such stones belong to.

## 10.10 Working it out { #working }

**An Ethiopian date.** The Ethiopian year 2019 began on 1 Meskerem = 11 September 2026. What is 5 November 2026?
Counting from 11 September: 20 days to the end of September (days 1 to 20), 31 in October (days 21 to 51), and 5 in
November (days 52 to 56). Day 56 is the 26th day of the second month, so 5 November 2026 is **26 Tikimt 2019**.

**Why 11 September will move.** The Ethiopian leap rule is the Julian one, one leap day every four years with no
exceptions, so it gains a day on the seasons every 128 years, just as the Julian calendar did (chapter 9, section 9.12).
In 2100 the Gregorian calendar skips a leap day and the Ethiopian does not, so from 2100 the Ethiopian New Year
moves to 12 September.

**The Borana year.** Twelve months of 29.530589 days make $12 \times 29.530589 = 354.37$ days, about 10.9 days short of
the year of the seasons. A calendar that only counted would slip a month every three years. The Borana calendar does
not slip, because each month is fixed by seeing the Moon beside a star, and the stars return to the same place in the
sky at the same season every year.

**Weighing Sirius B with Kepler's third law.** For two stars orbiting each other, with masses in Suns, the period $P$
in years and the average distance $a$ in AU:

$$a^3 = (M_A + M_B)\,P^2.$$

With $M_A = 2.06$, $M_B = 1.02$ and $P = 50.1$ years: $a^3 = 3.08 \times 2510 = 7730$, so $a = 19.8$ AU, roughly the
distance from the Sun to Uranus. Astronomers run this the other way: they measure $a$ and $P$ and get the masses. That
is how Sirius B was found to weigh as much as the Sun.

**A star the size of a planet.** Sirius B has the Sun's mass squeezed into a ball smaller than the Earth:

| | Mars | Sirius B | Earth |
|---|---|---|---|
| Radius | 3,389.5 km | 5,634 km | 6,371 km |
| Mass | 0.107 Earths | about 339,000 Earths | 1 |
| Average density | 3.93 g/cm³ | about 2.7 million g/cm³ | 5.51 g/cm³ |
| Surface gravity | 3.71 m/s² | about 4.3 million m/s² | 9.80 m/s² |

A teaspoon (5 cm³) of Sirius B would weigh about 13 tonnes.

**Why Mars rocks reach Earth.** To leave a planet a rock needs at least the escape speed:

| | Mars | Earth |
|---|---|---|
| Escape speed | 5.03 km/s | 11.19 km/s |

An impact on Mars has to throw rocks less than half as fast as one on Earth would, through a much thinner atmosphere.
That is one reason rocks travel from Mars to Earth far more easily than from Earth to Mars.

## 10.11 How should we judge African astronomy? { #assessment }

**Hard to see, not absent.** African astronomy has often been left out of histories because so little of it was
written down, and because what was written was often by outsiders. The record we do have shows calendars tied to
farming and herding (the Borana, the southern African digging stars), a written tradition of computus and astronomy in
Ethiopia, and Islamic astronomy copied and used across the Sahara.

**Claims need testing in both directions.** Africa has suffered from two opposite errors. One ignores African
knowledge altogether. The other credits it with things it probably did not have, like the Dogon and Sirius B, or the
Namoratunga pillars as an observatory, sometimes in ways that make Africans seem to need outside help to explain their
own culture. The right approach is the same as anywhere: check the evidence, and say how strong it is.

**A future in the south.** Africa's dark, dry skies and its southern view of the Milky Way have made it one of the
world's great places for astronomy, from Lacaille to SALT, MeerKAT and the Square Kilometre Array, with a fast-growing
community of African astronomers.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) What are the three kinds of calendar found in Africa?

??? note "Answer"
    Lunar (counting Moons), solar (following the Sun and seasons) and stellar (watching particular stars). Most African
    calendars combine them.

**2.** (beginner) How many months has the Ethiopian calendar, and how long are they?

??? note "Answer"
    Thirteen: twelve of 30 days, and Pagume of 5 days, or 6 in a leap year.

**3.** (beginner) What did the Pleiades (isiLimela) tell farmers in southern Africa?

??? note "Answer"
    That it was time to begin hoeing the fields: they were the "digging stars".

**4.** (intermediate) Give two reasons to doubt that the Namoratunga pillars were a star calendar.

??? note "Answer"
    Any two of: re-analysis of the alignments did not support them; a radiocarbon date of about 2400 BC makes the site
    far older than the Borana calendar; similar pillar sites around the lake were cemeteries; Clive Ruggles questioned
    the link to the Borana calendar.

**5.** (intermediate) Why is the Book of Enoch important for the history of astronomy, and why is Ethiopia part of the
story?

??? note "Answer"
    Its Astronomical Book (chapters 72–82) describes the motions of the Sun and Moon and a 364-day year. The whole book
    survives only in Ge'ez, because the Ethiopian Church kept it as scripture.

**6.** (intermediate) Give one argument for, and two against, the claim that the Dogon knew about Sirius B.

??? note "Answer"
    For: Griaule and Dieterlen reported it, and their defenders say secret knowledge may not be told to outsiders.
    Against (any two): van Beek's fieldwork found no such knowledge, and informants disagreed about which star was
    meant; the claim was first reported in the 1940s, after decades of contact with Europeans; Griaule's questioning
    was leading; the stories know of no ringed planet beyond Saturn, matching what Europeans knew at the time.

**7.** (advanced) What is 15 September 2026 in the Ethiopian calendar? And 15 January 2027?

??? note "Answer"
    15 September 2026 is the 5th day after 1 Meskerem 2019 (11 September), so **5 Meskerem 2019**. For 15 January
    2027: Tir 2019 starts on 9 January, so 15 January is **7 Tir 2019**.

**8.** (expert) Sirius A and B have masses 2.06 and 1.02 Suns and the orbit has $a = 19.8$ AU. Use Kepler's third law
to find the period. Then: a rock thrown off Mars at 5.5 km/s escapes; would it escape from Earth?

??? note "Answer"
    $P^2 = a^3 / (M_A + M_B) = 7762 / 3.08 = 2520$, so $P \approx 50.2$ years. A rock at 5.5 km/s is above Mars's
    escape speed of 5.03 km/s, but well below the Earth's 11.19 km/s, so from Earth it would fall back.

## Summary

- Africa's calendars are lunar, solar and stellar, and most combine them; much of its astronomy lived in speech.
- The Namoratunga pillars by Lake Turkana were once called an observatory; later dates and studies make that unlikely.
- The Borana calendar fixes its months by the Moon beside seven star groups, with 27 day names and no weeks.
- Ethiopia's calendar has 12 months of 30 days and Pagume, starts on 11 September and keeps the Julian leap rule; the
  whole Book of Enoch, with its Astronomical Book, survives only in Ge'ez.
- Timbuktu's manuscripts include astronomy, a meteor shower of 1583 and copies of Ibn Yunus; some 350,000 were saved in
  2012–2013.
- In southern Africa the Pleiades were the digging stars and the Southern Cross giraffes marked the end of planting.
- The Dogon Sirius B claim rests on Griaule's reports alone; later fieldwork found no trace of it.
- Lacaille mapped the southern sky from the Cape; SALT and MeerKAT now stand in the Karoo.
- Mars meteorites such as Tissint, Black Beauty and NWA 16788 have been found in Morocco, the Sahara and Niger.

clouds of the Milky Way, and peoples from the Andes to the Arctic built their own calendars of Sun, Moon and stars.
