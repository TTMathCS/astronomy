# 12. Oceania and Australia

*The Pacific is the largest ocean on Earth, and its peoples crossed it in canoes, steering by the stars, long before
any European ship arrived. This chapter follows the navigators who read the rising and setting points of stars like a
compass, the canoe Hōkūleʻa that tested their methods in 1976, and the Raʻiātean navigator Tupaia, who sailed with
Cook after the transit of Venus. It looks at Matariki and the Pacific year, at the oldest living sky knowledge in the
world, held by Aboriginal Australians, and at Tagai of the Torres Strait. It ends with the great telescopes of the
southern skies, the arguments over Mauna Kea, and what the Pacific has given to the study of Mars.*

## 12.1 A sea of islands { #ocean }

The ancestors of the Polynesians spoke Austronesian languages. By the most widely accepted theory they spread out
from Taiwan between 3000 and 1000 BC. Between about 1600 and 500 BC the people of the **Lapita** culture, known by
their pottery, sailed into the islands of Melanesia and then on to Fiji, Tonga and Samoa. Their
descendants settled the **Polynesian Triangle**, with Hawaiʻi, Rapa Nui (Easter Island) and Aotearoa (New Zealand) at
its corners. A common timeline has Hawaiʻi reached by about AD 900, Rapa Nui by 1000 and New Zealand by 1200, though
the dates are still debated.

Australia was settled far earlier: Aboriginal Australians had reached what is now New South Wales by 40,000 BC
(chapter 1, section 1.9), and their sky knowledge is among the oldest living traditions on Earth. Between Australia and New Guinea
live the Torres Strait Islanders, a people of the sea with a sky of their own.

## 12.2 Steering by the stars { #wayfinding }

A star always rises at the same point on the horizon and sets at the same point, night after night and year after
year; only the time changes with the seasons. So each star marks a fixed direction, as it rises and as it sets. A
Pacific navigator steered towards a star low on the horizon, and when it climbed too high, switched to the next star
rising at the same point. For each route a navigator memorised a sequence of stars.

Put all these rising and setting points round the horizon and you have a **star compass**. Some star compasses name as
many as 150 stars, though most have a few dozen. In the Caroline Islands of Micronesia, boys learned it in the men's
house, with pebbles, shells or pieces of coral laid on the sand in a circle to stand for the stars.

Stars also told the navigator how far north or south the canoe was. A star that passes **straight overhead** at an
island will pass overhead anywhere on the same latitude. Navigators knew which stars passed over which islands, so
they could sail until the right star passed overhead and then turn east or west along that latitude to the island.
For Hawaiian navigators the guiding overhead star was **Arcturus**, called *Hōkūleʻa*, "star of gladness".

When clouds hid the stars, navigators steered by the long ocean **swells**, which keep the same direction for days,
and by the wind. Near land they watched for land birds, for certain kinds of cloud, and for the light of shallow water
reflected on the underside of clouds.

## 12.3 Hōkūleʻa: testing the voyages { #hokulea }

**The case for accident.** In 1956 the New Zealand historian **Andrew Sharp** published *Ancient Voyagers in the
Pacific*. He argued that Polynesian canoes and navigation were too poor for planned voyages of discovery, and that the
islands had been found by luck: by canoes blown off course or drifting. He could point to real cases. On one of
Cook's voyages, Cook met Tahitians who had been lost in a gale and blown 1,000 miles (1,600 km) to the island of Atiu.

**The test.** The anthropologist **Ben Finney** decided to find out by building a canoe and sailing it. In the 1960s
he built *Nalehia*, a 12 m copy of a Hawaiian double canoe, and tested it in Hawaiian waters. In 1973 he co-founded
the **Polynesian Voyaging Society**, which launched the double canoe ***Hōkūleʻa*** on 8 March 1975. The old methods had been lost in
Hawaiʻi, so the society asked **Mau Piailug** of Satawal in the Caroline Islands, a master
navigator, to guide it.

From **1 May to 4 June 1976**, 34 days, Hōkūleʻa sailed from Honolua Bay on Maui to Papeete in Tahiti, about
4,350 km, with no compass, charts or instruments. In 1980 Mau's student **Nainoa Thompson** made the same voyage as
navigator, the first Native Hawaiian in modern times to cross thousands of kilometres of ocean without instruments.
Between 2014 and 2017 Hōkūleʻa sailed round the world.

**What it shows.** The voyages proved that canoes like these, steered by the stars and the swells, can find a small
island across thousands of kilometres of open sea, so Sharp was wrong to say it could not be done. But the revival
mostly used the Micronesian methods that Mau taught, so it shows what was possible, not exactly how the first
Polynesian voyagers did it.

## 12.4 Tupaia, Cook and the transit of Venus { #tupaia }

On **3 June 1769** the planet Venus crossed the face of the Sun. James Cook had been sent to Tahiti by the Royal Society
of London to watch it, and on a sandy point on Matavai Bay, which Cook named **Point Venus**, he, the astronomer
**Charles Green** and the naturalist Daniel Solander timed the transit with their own telescopes. Timing a transit from
places far apart can give the distance to the Sun, an idea of James Gregory in 1663 that Edmond Halley worked out in
1716 (chapter 16 explains how). In practice a blurred "black drop" joined Venus to the edge of the Sun and made exact
timing impossible, and the Royal Society was disappointed. Even so, from the 1769 results the astronomer Thomas
Hornsby worked out a distance to the Sun of 150.8 million km, less than 1% from today's value.

At Tahiti the expedition met **Tupaia**, a navigator and *arioi* priest from the island of **Raʻiātea**, born around
1725. He became a friend of the botanist Joseph Banks during the transit, and joined Cook's ship *Endeavour* as guide
and translator. He drew a **chart of the islands** within about 3,200 km of Raʻiātea, showing 130 islands and naming
74 of them: Pacific knowledge of the ocean set down in a European form. Tupaia died of illness at Batavia (now
Jakarta) on 20 December 1770, on the voyage home.

## 12.5 Matariki and the island year { #matariki }

**Matariki.** To the Māori of Aotearoa, **Matariki** is the Pleiades. It sets in the west in the early evening in May,
and reappears in the east just before sunrise in late June or early July. That first rising begins the first month of
the year, *Pipiri*. Each month of the Māori calendar was marked the same way, by a star first seen before dawn at the
new Moon; the tenth month, *Poutūterangi*, by Altair. Older sources name seven stars in Matariki; a manuscript passed
down to the astronomer Rangi Mātāmua names nine. Some iwi (tribes) use **Puanga**, the bright star Rigel, instead.
Matariki became a public holiday in New Zealand on **24 June 2022**; in 2026 it fell on 10 July.

On **Rapa Nui** too, Matariki began the new year, and its disappearance in mid-April ended the fishing season.

**Makahiki.** In Hawaiʻi the new-year season, **Makahiki**, honoured the god Lono. On Hawaiʻi Island it began with the
first crescent Moon after **Makaliʻi**, the Pleiades, rose just after sunset, usually around 17 November. During
Makahiki war was forbidden.

**The Moon calendar.** The Māori lunar calendar, the **maramataka** ("the turning of the Moon"), gives each of the 30
nights of the month its own name, from the new Moon (*whiro*) through the full Moon (*atua*) to the last of the
waning Moon (*mutu*). It tells when to fish, catch eels and plant. Different iwi kept different months and names.

The Pleiades mark a turning point of the year in many places: the planting season in the Andes (chapter 11, section
11.6), the new year in Aotearoa, Rapa Nui and Hawaiʻi, and the start of winter in central Australia (section 12.6).

## 12.6 The oldest living sky: Aboriginal Australia { #aboriginal }

Chapter 1 met the Emu in the Sky, made of the dark clouds of the Milky Way, and the stone ring of Wurdi Youang. Here are
more pieces of Aboriginal astronomy, with the reminder that some knowledge is sacred and not shared with outsiders.

**Calendars.** Many groups in northern Australia divide the year into **six seasons**, and many mark the seasons by
which stars are seen. For the Pitjantjatjara, the Pleiades rising at dawn in May marks the start of winter. The rising
or setting of a star can say when to move camp or which food to look for.

**The Moon, the tides and Venus.** The Yolŋu of Arnhem Land tell that **Ngalindi**, the Moon-man, grows fat (the full
Moon), is cut down by his wives' axes (the waning Moon), dies (the new Moon) and after three days rises again. The Yolŋu
also linked the Moon with the **tides**. They see Orion as a canoe, **Julpan**, carrying three brothers: the three stars
of Orion's belt. One of them ate a sawfish that their law forbade, so the Sun-woman Walu sent a waterspout that lifted
the canoe into the sky; Betelgeuse and Rigel are its bow and stern. Venus, **Barnumbirr**, is honoured in a Morning
Star ceremony: as she rises before dawn she draws a rope of light from the island of Baralku, along which, with the
help of a decorated Morning Star Pole, people can speak with loved ones who have died.

**Stars that change.** Aboriginal people of the Great Victoria Desert in South Australia tell of Betelgeuse as the club
of the hunter Nyeeruna (Orion), which fills with fire-magic, fades and returns. Betelgeuse really does change in
brightness; between October 2019 and February 2020 it faded to about a third of its usual brightness. In 2010 the
astronomers Duane Hamacher and David Frew showed that the Boorong people of north-western Victoria saw the great
outburst of the star **Eta Carinae** in the 1840s and added it to their stories as Collowgulloric War, the wife of War
(Canopus, the crow).

**Stories of craters.** The Djaru people call the Wolfe Creek meteorite crater in Western Australia **Kandimalal**. An
Elder told how the crescent Moon and the evening star once passed very close; the evening star grew so hot that it fell
to the ground with a great explosion and a cloud of dust. The crater is less than 120,000 years old. The Western
Arrernte call the ring of hills at Gosses Bluff **Tnorala**: star women were dancing in the Milky Way, and a baby fell
from its wooden carrier into the Earth and pushed up the hills; the carrier is the constellation Corona Australis. But
Tnorala was made by an impact about 142 million years ago, long before people, so this story explains the crater; it
cannot remember the impact.

**What was lost.** Much was written down by outsiders, such as William Stanbridge, who came to Australia in 1841 and
befriended the Boorong. Some was lost: the Nganguraku people lost most of their culture, language included, after
Christian missionaries banned it before 1913.

## 12.7 Tagai: the sky of the Torres Strait { #tagai }

The Torres Strait Islanders see themselves as people of the sea, and their law and their sky are joined in the stories
of **Tagai**. In one, Tagai is a man standing in a canoe. In his left hand he holds a fishing spear, which is the
**Southern Cross**; in his right hand a *sorbi*, a red fruit. Before a voyage his crew of twelve ate and drank all the
food meant for the journey, so Tagai tied them together in two groups of six and threw them into the sea. They became
two star groups: the **Pleiades** and **Orion**. Islanders say they follow the instructions of Tagai, a system of order
in which everything has its place.

## 12.8 Telescopes of the south and the Pacific { #telescopes }

**Parkes.** The 64 m radio dish at **Parkes** in New South Wales was finished in 1961. In 1969 it was one of the
antennas that received the live television pictures of the Apollo 11 Moon landing. Its observations gave the first link
between a **quasar** and a visible object, and **fast radio bursts** were discovered in 2007 in Parkes data from 2001.
In November 2020 it was given a Wiradjuri name, **Murriyang**, the home in the stars of the creator spirit Biyaami.

**Siding Spring** in New South Wales is home to the 3.9 m Anglo-Australian Telescope. At **Mount John** in New Zealand,
the land around the observatory became the Aoraki Mackenzie International Dark Sky Reserve in 2012.

**Mauna Kea.** At 4,207 m on the island of Hawaiʻi, Mauna Kea has dark skies, dry and steady air, and thirteen
telescopes. It is also sacred in Native Hawaiian religion. When the **Thirty Meter Telescope** (TMT) was to be built
there, protest began at its groundbreaking on 7 October 2014. In 2015 people blocking the road were arrested and the
Hawaiʻi Supreme Court cancelled the building permits; in 2019 protectors (*kiaʻi*) blocked the road again and their camp grew from
hundreds to thousands, while others rallied in support of the telescope. Supporters pointed to the quality of the
site; opponents to a sacred mountain that already carried thirteen telescopes. In 2024 the TMT project apologised
for its part in dividing the community, care of the summit is passing to a new authority that includes both astronomers
and Native Hawaiians, and in June 2025 the US National Science Foundation chose to support the Giant Magellan
Telescope instead.

**The Square Kilometre Array.** In the Murchison region of Western Australia, on the land of the **Wajarri** people,
the low-frequency half of the SKA is being built, with the other half in South Africa (chapter 10). The site is named
**Inyarrimanha Ilgari Bundara**, "sharing sky and stars" in the Wajarri language. It made its first picture in March
2025 with 1,000 of its planned 131,000 antennas.

## 12.9 Mars from the Pacific { #mars }

**The oldest fossils, and a tool for Mars.** In the Pilbara of Western Australia, rocks about **3.5 billion years old**
hold stromatolites, layered mounds that are among the earliest widely accepted signs of life on Earth. Not everyone
agrees: some geologists have argued that structures this old could have formed without life. In 2006 the geologist
**Abigail Allwood** and her team published evidence in *Nature* that they were made by living things. She went on to
develop **PIXL** at NASA's Jet Propulsion Laboratory, an X-ray instrument on the Perseverance rover that maps the
elements in Martian rocks, part of the search for signs of past life there (chapter 11, section 11.10).

**Living on "Mars" in Hawaiʻi.** On the slopes of Mauna Loa, at about 2,500 m, the **HI-SEAS** habitat sits on bare,
Mars-like lava. Between 2013 and 2018 NASA paid for crews to live there in isolation, to study how people get on,
handle stress and solve problems together on long missions. The longest, HI-SEAS IV, kept six people inside for a full year, from 29 August 2015 to 29 August 2016.

**A Mars mission with New Zealand roots.** NASA's twin **ESCAPADE** spacecraft, named Blue and Gold, are built on a
spacecraft design from **Rocket Lab**, a company founded in New Zealand by Peter Beck in 2006. They launched on
13 November 2025, were to swing past the Earth in November 2026, and are planned to reach Mars orbit in September 2027.
Together they will study how the solar wind helped strip away most of Mars's air.

**Transits from Mars.** Cook went to Tahiti to watch Venus cross the Sun. From Mars, the **Earth** can be seen crossing
the Sun. Nobody has ever watched one; the last was on 11 May 1984, and the next will be on **10 November 2084**.

## 12.10 Working it out { #working }

**Where a star rises.** The rule of chapter 1, section 1.11 works for any star, not only the Sun. With azimuth $A$
measured from north through east, latitude $\varphi$ and the star's declination $\delta$:

$$\cos A = \frac{\sin \delta}{\cos \varphi}.$$

Arcturus ($\delta = +19.18°$) seen from Hawaiʻi ($\varphi = 19.6°$):
$\cos A = 0.3286 / 0.9421 = 0.3488$, so $A = 69.6°$, about 20° north of east. It sets at $360° - 69.6° = 290.4°$,
20° north of west. Because $\delta$ hardly changes over a lifetime, the bearing is the same every night.

**The overhead star.** A star passes straight overhead where the latitude equals its declination. Arcturus at
$+19.18°$ passes 0.4° south of the overhead point on the island of Hawaiʻi; Sirius at $-16.72°$ passes about 1° north
of overhead at Tahiti ($17.67°$ S).

How far is 1° of latitude? It is $\frac{1}{360}$ of the way round the planet: $2\pi R / 360$.

**A navigator on Mars.** Mars is smaller, so everything on its surface is closer. The distance to the horizon for an eye
at height $h$ is about $d = \sqrt{2Rh}$ (ignoring the bending of light by the air):

| | Mars | Earth |
|---|---|---|
| Radius | 3,389.5 km | 6,371.0 km |
| One degree of latitude | 59.2 km | 111.2 km |
| Horizon for an eye 2 m up | 3.7 km | 5.0 km |
| Top of a 4,207 m peak, seen from sea level, up to | 169 km | 232 km |
| Bright star near the north pole of the sky | Deneb, about 9° away | Polaris, under 1° away |

A Martian navigator would find the overhead-star method works just as well, with each degree worth only 59 km, but
would have no good pole star: the nearest first-magnitude star to the north pole of the Martian sky is Deneb, about 9°
away.

**The 1769 result.** Hornsby's distance to the Sun was 150,838,800 km; today's value is 149,597,871 km. The error is

$$\frac{150{,}838{,}800 - 149{,}597{,}871}{149{,}597{,}871} = 0.83\%.$$

## 12.11 How should we judge the astronomy of Oceania and Australia? { #assessment }

**Astronomy put to work.** Pacific navigators used the whole sky as a working instrument, holding in memory the rising
points of dozens of stars, the overhead stars of many islands and the patterns of the swells. It took them across the
largest ocean on Earth long before European ships crossed it with compasses and charts.

**Memory with limits.** Oral traditions can carry real observations for generations, as the Boorong record of Eta
Carinae shows. But stories also explain things nobody saw, like the crater of Tnorala, made 142 million years before
people. Each claim needs checking, just as written records do.

**Nearly lost, then brought back.** Mau Piailug's initiation as a master navigator was the last held on Satawal for
fifty years. The knowledge survived because a few people kept it and chose to teach it, and today Hōkūleʻa sails,
Matariki is a national holiday, and telescopes carry Aboriginal names.

**Who shares the sky.** Mauna Kea and the SKA show that modern astronomy works on land that belongs to someone, often
land that is sacred. The naming of Murriyang and Inyarrimanha Ilgari Bundara shows another way, with astronomers and
Indigenous peoples sharing the sky.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Besides the stars, what did Pacific navigators use to find their way?

??? note "Answer"
    The ocean swells and the wind; near land, land birds, certain kinds of cloud and the light of shallow water
    reflected on clouds.

**2.** (beginner) What is Matariki, and when does it begin the Māori year?

??? note "Answer"
    The Pleiades. Its first rising before dawn, in late June or early July, begins the month Pipiri and the new year.

**3.** (beginner) What do the Torres Strait Islanders see in the Southern Cross?

??? note "Answer"
    The fishing spear in the left hand of Tagai, a man standing in a canoe.

**4.** (intermediate) What did Andrew Sharp argue in 1956, and what did the voyage of Hōkūleʻa show? What is the limit
of that test?

??? note "Answer"
    Sharp argued that the islands were found by accident, by canoes that drifted or were blown off course. Hōkūleʻa
    sailed from Hawaiʻi to Tahiti in 34 days with no instruments, so planned voyages were possible. But it used the
    Micronesian methods taught by Mau Piailug, so it cannot show exactly how the first Polynesians navigated.

**5.** (intermediate) Give the main argument on each side of the dispute over the Thirty Meter Telescope.

??? note "Answer"
    For: Mauna Kea is one of the best sites on Earth for astronomy, high, dark, dry and steady. Against: it is sacred
    in Native Hawaiian religion and already carries thirteen telescopes.

**6.** (intermediate) Why can the Western Arrernte story of Tnorala not be a memory of the impact that made it?

??? note "Answer"
    The impact happened about 142 million years ago, long before there were any people. The story explains the crater
    rather than remembering its making.

**7.** (advanced) At Tahiti ($17.67°$ S), in which direction does Sirius ($\delta = -16.72°$) rise, and how far from the
overhead point does it pass, in degrees and in kilometres?

??? note "Answer"
    $\cos A = \sin(-16.72°) / \cos 17.67° = -0.2877 / 0.9528 = -0.3019$, so $A = 107.6°$, about 18° south of east. It
    passes $17.67° - 16.72° = 0.95°$ north of overhead, which is $0.95 \times 111.2 \approx 106$ km.

**8.** (expert) On Mars, a navigator sees a star whose declination she knows pass 2° south of the overhead point. How
far is she from the latitude where it passes straight overhead? How far would it be on Earth? Why is it harder on Mars
to find north by the stars?

??? note "Answer"
    On Mars $2 \times 59.2 = 118$ km; on Earth $2 \times 111.2 = 222$ km. The north pole of the Martian sky has no
    bright star near it: Deneb is about 9° away, while on Earth Polaris is less than 1° from the pole.

## Summary

- The Pacific was settled from Taiwan through Melanesia; Polynesians reached Hawaiʻi, Rapa Nui and New Zealand by about
  AD 900 to 1200.
- Navigators steered by the fixed rising and setting points of stars, a star compass, and found latitude from stars
  passing overhead, such as Arcturus over Hawaiʻi.
- In 1976 Hōkūleʻa, guided by Mau Piailug, sailed from Hawaiʻi to Tahiti in 34 days without instruments, answering
  Sharp's theory of accidental settlement.
- Tupaia of Raʻiātea met Cook at the 1769 transit of Venus and drew a chart of 130 Pacific islands.
- Matariki (the Pleiades) begins the Māori year; Makahiki began with the Pleiades in Hawaiʻi; the maramataka names
  each night of the Moon.
- Aboriginal traditions describe the Moon and tides, Venus, changing stars like Betelgeuse and Eta Carinae, and craters;
  some stories explain rather than remember.
- Tagai of the Torres Strait holds the Southern Cross as his spear.
- Parkes, Siding Spring, Mauna Kea and the SKA make the region a centre of astronomy, with hard questions about land.
- The Pilbara fossils led to PIXL on Perseverance; HI-SEAS rehearsed Mars on Mauna Loa; ESCAPADE flies on a
  Rocket Lab design.
