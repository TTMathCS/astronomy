# 22. Astronomy today

*This book began with people who watched the sky by eye. It ends in a time of mirrors nearly 40 m wide, telescopes
1.5 million km from the Earth, more than six thousand known planets round other stars, and signals that are not light
at all. It also ends with a warning: a third of the world can no longer see the Milky Way. And yet people still read
the sky by eye, and some of the newest discoveries were made by volunteers looking at pictures at home.*

## 22.1 Mirrors on mountains { #giants }

A telescope's main mirror is a bucket for light: the wider it is, the fainter the things it can see. The chart shows
how the record has grown.

- **1789.** William Herschel, helped by his sister Caroline, finished his "Forty-Foot" at Slough in England. Its mirror
  was about 1.2 to 1.26 m across, and it stayed the largest telescope in the world for 50 years (chapter 17).
- **1845.** William Parsons, Earl of Rosse, finished the "Leviathan" at Birr Castle in Ireland, with a mirror 1.83 m
  across. With it he drew the spiral shape of the Whirlpool Galaxy.
- **1917 and 1948.** The 2.5 m **Hooker** telescope on Mount Wilson, near Los Angeles, was the largest from 1917 to
  1949; with it Edwin Hubble showed in 1923 that the Andromeda nebula is a galaxy outside our own (chapter 19). Then came the 5.1 m **Hale** telescope on Mount Palomar,
  twice as wide as any other.
- **1993.** The **Keck** telescopes, 4,145 m up near
  the top of Mauna Kea in Hawaiʻi, each built a 10 m mirror from 36 pieces fitted together. Keck I was finished in
  1993 and Keck II in 1996.
- **2005 and 2007.** South Africa's **SALT**, 11.1 by 9.8 m, saw first light in 2005 (chapter 10). The **Gran
  Telescopio Canarias**, built by Spain with Mexico and the University of Florida, saw first light in 2007; at
  10.4 m it is the world's largest single optical telescope.

Two giants are now being built in the Atacama Desert of **Chile**. The European Southern Observatory's
**Extremely Large Telescope** (ELT) on Cerro Armazones will have a mirror 39.3 m across, made of 798 six-sided
pieces each about 1.45 m wide. First light is set for March 2029. The **Giant Magellan Telescope** (GMT) at Las
Campanas will join seven huge mirrors into one 25.4 m wide; it is being built by institutions from Australia,
Brazil, Chile, Israel, South Korea, Taiwan and the United States, and should start work in the early 2030s. A third,
the Thirty Meter Telescope, is stuck over the dispute on Mauna Kea (chapter 12).

## 22.2 Telescopes in space { #space }

The air blurs starlight and glows faintly itself. Above it, a smaller mirror can see sharper and fainter things.

**Hubble.** NASA and ESA's **Hubble Space Telescope** was launched in 1990 with a 2.4 m mirror. The first pictures
were blurred: the mirror had been ground to the wrong shape. Astronauts on the Space Shuttle fitted corrections in
1993.

**Webb.** The **James Webb Space Telescope**, built by NASA with ESA and the Canadian Space Agency, has a mirror
6.5 m across made of 18 gold-coated pieces. It was launched on **25 December 2021** on an Ariane 5 rocket
from Kourou in French Guiana, and in January 2022 it reached its post near the Lagrange point L2, about 1.5 million km
from the Earth. It sees mostly in the infrared. It came in years late and cost about 10 billion US dollars.

**Around the world.** Europe's **Euclid**, launched on 1 July 2023, is measuring how the expansion of the universe speeds
up, to learn about dark energy and dark matter. India's **AstroSat**, launched on a PSLV rocket on 28 September 2015, was planned for five years and is
still working after eleven. India's **Aditya-L1**, its first mission to study the Sun, reached the point L1 between
the Earth and the Sun on 6 January 2024. China's **Xuntian**, a 2 m space telescope that will see a patch of sky 300
to 350 times larger than Hubble's, is due to launch in 2027. It will fly near China's Tiangong space station and
dock there from time to time for servicing.

## 22.3 Planets round other stars { #planets }

The first real evidence of planets round other stars came only at the end of the 20th century.

- **1988, Canada.** Bruce Campbell, G. A. H. Walker and Stephenson Yang, of the University of Victoria and the
  University of British Columbia, found that the star Gamma Cephei wobbled as if a planet pulled it. They were careful
  not to claim too much, and other astronomers doubted it for years. It was confirmed in 2003.
- **1992, Puerto Rico.** Aleksander Wolszczan, a Polish astronomer, and Dale Frail used the great radio dish at
  **Arecibo** to study a pulsar (chapter 20), and found two planets about the mass of the Earth going round it.
  They were the first planets beyond the Sun to be confirmed, but round a dead star.
- **1995, France.** On 6 October 1995 **Michel Mayor** and his student **Didier Queloz** of Geneva announced a planet
  round **51 Pegasi**, a star like the Sun. It was the first planet found round an ordinary star. They found it with a new spectrograph at the Haute-Provence Observatory,
  from the star's tiny back-and-forth Doppler shift. The planet is a giant, but it circles its star in about four
  days, the first of the "hot Jupiters". They shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2019.

**Thousands.** NASA's **Kepler** telescope stared at 530,506 stars from 2009 to 2018, waiting for a planet to cross in
front of one and dim it slightly, and found 2,662 planets. An Earth-sized planet crossing a star like the Sun dims
it by only about 80 parts in a million. In 2016 and 2017 a team led by the Belgian astronomer
**Michaël Gillon** of the University of Liège, using small telescopes at La Silla in Chile and Oukaïmeden in
**Morocco**, with others in South Africa, Europe and space, found seven rocky planets round the small star
**TRAPPIST-1**. By 5 November 2026, 6,464 planets round other stars had been confirmed.

The chart shows why small planets are so hard to find this way. A planet like Jupiter dims a star like the Sun by
about 1 per cent; the Earth by less than a hundredth of that, and Mars by less again.

## 22.4 Is anyone out there? { #life }

Webb has been used to study the air of planets round other stars, reading which gases are there from their spectra
(chapter 18). That has started a new argument about life.

**K2-18b** is a planet about 124 light-years away, about 2.6 times as wide as the Earth, round a small red star in
Leo. It gets about as much light from its star as the Earth does from the Sun. It may have
deep water under an air of hydrogen. In 2025 a team reported signs of **dimethyl sulfide** in its air, a gas that could be a
sign of life.

- **For.** If the gas is there, living things are one way it could be made.
- **Against.** Other scientists doubt that the data really show dimethyl sulfide rather than something else, and even
  if it is there, chemistry without life might make it.

The claim has not been widely accepted. It is the same pattern as Viking's tests and the methane on Mars (chapter 21):
an exciting first signal, then years of checking.

??? note "Sources"
    - K2-18b: 124 light-years, an M dwarf in Leo, 2.6 times the Earth's radius, about as much light as the Earth gets, found with Kepler and observed with Webb, the hycean idea (water
      under hydrogen), the controversial report of dimethyl sulfide in 2025, not widely accepted, abiotic processes and
      doubts about the data: Wikipedia, [K2-18b](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/K2-18b) (weaker).

## 22.5 New messengers { #messengers }

For all of history, astronomy meant catching light, and from chapter 20 radio waves too. Now there are other
messengers.

**Gravitational waves.** In 1974 Russell Hulse and Joseph Taylor found two neutron stars orbiting each other, one
of them a pulsar (chapter 20). Over the years they showed that the pair were spiralling together, losing energy just
as Einstein's theory said they should by sending out waves in space itself. They won the Nobel Prize in 1993. On **14 September 2015** the two **LIGO**
detectors in the United States, 3,002 km apart at Hanford and at Livingston in Louisiana, caught such waves directly
for the first time: two black holes merging about 1.4 billion light-years away, with a signal that matched Einstein's
theory. The news was given on 11 February
2016.

On **17 August 2017** LIGO and Virgo, near Pisa in Italy, caught waves from two **neutron stars** colliding in the
galaxy NGC 4993. Under two seconds later, space telescopes saw a burst of gamma rays, and 11 hours later astronomers found the
glow of the blast. The blast is thought to have made heavy elements weighing thousands of Earths, among them
about ten Earths' worth of gold and platinum.

**Neutrinos.** **IceCube** is a cubic kilometre of Antarctic ice at the South Pole, with thousands of sensors buried in it. In 2018 its
team traced a neutrino that arrived in September 2017 back to a blazar called TXS 0506+056, 3.7 billion
light-years away, and in 2023 it saw neutrinos coming from our own Milky Way.

## 22.6 Watching the whole sky { #surveys }

**Rubin.** On a 2,682 m peak of Cerro Pachón in Chile stands the **Vera C. Rubin Observatory**, named after the
American astronomer who studied how fast galaxies turn, work that pointed to dark matter. Its camera has 3.2
billion pixels, the largest ever built. Its first pictures came out on 23 June 2025, and in July 2026 it began a
ten-year survey: one picture every 40 seconds, covering the whole southern sky every few nights, a time-lapse film of
the universe.

**LAMOST.** The Chinese Academy of Sciences' **LAMOST** telescope has 4,000 optical fibres, each moved to catch one
star, so it can take tens of thousands of spectra a night. It set out to survey 10 million stars of the Milky Way.

**Pushing an asteroid.** Rubin is expected to find more than five million asteroids, about 100,000 of them ones that
come near the Earth. On **26 September
2022** NASA's **DART** spacecraft flew into **Dimorphos**, a 160 m moonlet of the asteroid Didymos, at 23,000 km/h,
10.9 million km from the Earth. Neither rock is a danger to us. The crash shortened Dimorphos's orbit by 32 minutes;
the mission would have counted as a success with 73 seconds.

## 22.7 Reading the sky by eye { #eye }

Not all astronomy today needs a giant mirror.

- **Old knowledge, still used.** Farmers in the Andes still look at the Pleiades in June to judge the coming rains, a
  method that satellite data tested and supported in 2000 (chapter 11). Since 2022 **Matariki**, the rising of the
  Pleiades, has been a public holiday in New Zealand, and in 1976 the canoe Hōkūleʻa showed that Pacific navigators
  could cross the ocean by the stars alone (chapter 12).
- **Volunteers.** **Galaxy Zoo** asked anyone with a computer to sort pictures of galaxies by shape. In its first year more than 150,000 people made more than
  50 million classifications. Volunteers pointed out odd things that turned out to be new kinds of object, such as
  **Hanny's Voorwerp** and the **Green Pea** galaxies.
- **Names.** Since 2016 the International Astronomical Union's Working Group on Star Names has chosen official names
  for stars, approving them in batches; in 2017 it agreed rules for Chinese star names.

## 22.8 Astronomy from Mars { #mars }

The rovers of chapter 21 are also astronomers.

- **Eclipses.** Mars never sees a total eclipse of the Sun: its larger moon, **Phobos**, about 20 by 25 km, is not
  big enough or close enough to cover it. Opportunity caught Phobos crossing the Sun in March 2004,
  Curiosity watched a ring-shaped eclipse on 20 August 2013, and in 2022 Perseverance made the sharpest film yet.
- **The Earth crossing the Sun.** From Mars, the Earth sometimes passes across the face of the Sun, just as Venus
  does for us. It happened on 8 May 1905 and 11 May 1984, with no one there to see it. The next is on
  **10 November 2084**. Perhaps someone will be standing on Mars to watch.
- **The Earth from Mars.** On 3 October 2007 the HiRISE camera, orbiting Mars, turned round and photographed the
  Earth and the Moon 142 million km away (the physics lesson
  [Seeing Earth from Mars](https://physics.akadian.ca/topics/waves/level-7/06-seeing-earth-from-mars.html)).

??? note "Sources"
    - Phobos about 20 by 25 km, the transit filmed by Opportunity in March 2004, Curiosity's annular eclipse on
      20 August 2013, Perseverance's Mastcam-Z film of 2022: Wikipedia,
      [Solar eclipses on Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_eclipses_on_Mars) (weaker). The same page explains why Phobos cannot make a total eclipse.
    - No one has seen a transit of Earth from Mars; the dates 8 May 1905, 11 May 1984 and 10 November 2084:

## 22.9 Losing the dark { #dark }

**Light.** In June 2016 scientists estimated that a third of the people in the world could no longer see the Milky
Way from where they live, including 80 per cent of Americans and 60 per cent of Europeans. Light pollution grew by
at least 49 per cent worldwide between 1992 and 2017. Since the 1980s a dark-sky movement, now led by **DarkSky International**,
has worked to cut the light that goes up into the sky, and dark-sky reserves such as Aoraki Mackenzie in New Zealand
protect what is left (chapter 12).

**Satellites.** In October 2026 SpaceX's **Starlink**, which sells internet access by satellite, had about 11,150
satellites in low orbit.

- **Against.** Astronomers say the satellites add light to the sky and crowd the orbits. The International
  Astronomical Union, the US National Radio Astronomy Observatory and the Square Kilometre Array Observatory have all
  said they are worried.
- **The other side.** Starlink exists to sell internet access, and SpaceX has tried to make the satellites dimmer: a dark coating was dropped because the satellites got too hot, but later designs reflect less, and one
  newer type is about a fifth as bright as the first.

??? note "Sources"
    - The June 2016 estimate of one third, 80 per cent of Americans and 60 per cent of Europeans, the growth of at
      least 49 per cent from 1992 to 2017, the dark-sky movement of the 1980s, the International Dark-Sky Association,
      now DarkSky International, reducing the light going up into the sky: Wikipedia,
      [Light pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_pollution) (weaker). The Aoraki Mackenzie reserve:
      chapter 12.
    - Starlink: a SpaceX satellite internet constellation, about 11,150 satellites as of October 2026, criticism by

## 22.10 Who does astronomy now { #world }

Look back at this chapter. Many of the biggest telescopes on the ground are in **Chile**, **Hawaiʻi** and the **Canary Islands**, built by Europe, the
United States, Mexico and partners from Australia to Brazil and South Korea. **South Africa** has SALT and, with Australia,
the Square Kilometre Array (chapter 20). **China** has FAST, LAMOST and, soon, Xuntian. **India** has AstroSat and
Aditya-L1. A Belgian team found TRAPPIST-1's planets with a telescope in **Morocco**. The pulsar planets came from a
dish in **Puerto Rico**, and the first hot Jupiter from **France** and **Switzerland**.

This book began with many traditions, some linked and some far apart: Babylon and China, India and the Maya, the
Pacific navigators and the Islamic observatories. Today astronomy is one shared science, and the old traditions have
not disappeared.

## 22.11 Working it out { #working }

**Light-gathering.** A wider mirror catches more light. The light it collects goes with its area, so with the square
of its width. Compared with
Hubble's 2.4 m mirror:

$$\text{Webb: } \left(\frac{6.5}{2.4}\right)^2 = 7.3, \qquad \text{ELT: } \left(\frac{39.3}{2.4}\right)^2 = 268.$$

From Herschel's mirror to the ELT is $(39.3/1.2)^2 \approx 1{,}070$ times more light, or $(39.3/1.26)^2 \approx 970$
times if his mirror was 1.26 m.

**The dip.** When a planet of radius $r$ crosses a star of radius $R$, it blocks a fraction $(r/R)^2$ of the light.
For the Sun, $R = 695{,}700$ km:

$$\text{Jupiter: } \left(\frac{69{,}911}{695{,}700}\right)^2 = 0.0101 = 1.01\%.$$

**The lucky line-up.** A far-off astronomer sees the planet cross only if they lie almost in the plane of its orbit.
The chance is about $R/a$, where $a$ is the planet's distance from its star.

**Mars and the Earth, seen from another star.**

| | Mars | Earth |
|---|---|---|
| Radius | 3,389.5 km | 6,371 km |
| Dip when it crosses the Sun | $(3{,}389.5 / 695{,}700)^2 = 0.0024\%$ | $(6{,}371 / 695{,}700)^2 = 0.0084\%$ |
| Distance from the Sun, $a$ | 227.96 million km | 149.60 million km |
| Chance of the line-up, $R/a$ | $695{,}700 / 227{,}960{,}000 = 0.31\%$, about 1 in 330 | $695{,}700 / 149{,}600{,}000 = 0.47\%$, about 1 in 215 |
| Time between crossings | 687 days | 365.26 days |

Kepler's figure of about 80 parts in a million for an Earth-sized planet matches our 0.0084 per cent, which is 84
parts in a million. Mars would be harder to find than the Earth in every way: its dip is
$0.0084 / 0.0024 \approx 3.5$ times smaller, fewer observers are lined up to see it, and it crosses less often.

**Rubin's night.** One picture every 40 seconds for a 10-hour night is $36{,}000 / 40 = 900$ pictures, or
$900 \times 3.2 = 2{,}880$ billion pixels.

**DART's push.** 23,000 km/h is $23{,}000 / 3{,}600 = 6.4$ km/s. The change of 32 minutes, 1,920 seconds, was
$1{,}920 / 73 = 26$ times what the mission needed.

    - Mirror sizes: section 22.1 and 22.2. A wider mirror catches more light: the physics lesson
      [Telescopes](https://physics.akadian.ca/topics/waves/level-7/04-telescopes.html).
    - Radii, distances from the Sun and orbit periods: NASA,
      [Sun fact sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/sunfact.html),
      [Jupiter fact sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/jupiterfact.html),
      [Mars fact sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/marsfact.html) and

## 22.12 How should we judge this story? { #assessment }

**Big science, shared.** No country builds a 39 m telescope or a space telescope like Webb alone. That spreads the
cost and the skill, but it also means long delays and huge bills: Webb cost about 10 billion dollars, and the
Thirty Meter Telescope has waited years over the right to use a sacred mountain (chapter 12). Who decides where
telescopes go, and who shares in what they find, is now part of astronomy.

**Careful with "life".** K2-18b follows Viking and the Mars methane (chapter 21): a first signal, a burst of
excitement, then doubt. Good science keeps both sides in view until the evidence is strong.

**The sky as heritage.** For most of this book, anyone could look up and see the Milky Way. Now most people in rich
countries cannot. The same technology that lets us see the edge of the universe is taking away the view from our own
gardens.

    - See the sources for sections 22.1 to 22.10.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) How are the main mirrors of Keck and the ELT made, and how wide are they?

??? note "Answer"
    Both are made of many six-sided pieces fitted together: Keck's 10 m mirrors from 36 pieces each, and the ELT's
    39.3 m mirror from 798.

**2.** (beginner) Why put a telescope in space?

??? note "Answer"
    The air blurs starlight and adds its own faint glow. Above the air a smaller mirror can see sharper and fainter
    things.

**3.** (beginner) What was special about the planet found round 51 Pegasi in 1995?

??? note "Answer"
    It was the first planet found round a star like the Sun. It is a giant planet but orbits its star in about four
    days, the first "hot Jupiter".

**4.** (intermediate) Give one argument for and one against the claim of dimethyl sulfide on K2-18b showing life.

??? note "Answer"
    For: the gas could be a sign of life. Against: the data may not really show it, and chemistry without life might
    make it.

**5.** (intermediate) What did the collision of 17 August 2017 show that light alone could not?

??? note "Answer"
    It was seen both as gravitational waves and as light: a gamma-ray burst under two seconds later and a glow
    11 hours later. It showed that such collisions make heavy elements, including gold and platinum.

**6.** (intermediate) Give two ways people still study the sky by eye today.

??? note "Answer"
    For example: Andean farmers reading the Pleiades for rain, Matariki in New Zealand, and volunteers sorting
    galaxies for Galaxy Zoo.

**7.** (advanced) How many times more light does Webb's 6.5 m mirror gather than Hubble's 2.4 m one?

??? note "Answer"
    $(6.5 / 2.4)^2 = 2.71^2 = 7.3$ times.

**8.** (expert) An astronomer on a far planet watches the Sun. How much does it dim when Mars crosses it, and when
the Earth does? Why would Mars be harder to discover?

??? note "Answer"
    Mars: $(3{,}389.5 / 695{,}700)^2 = 0.0024\%$. Earth: $(6{,}371 / 695{,}700)^2 = 0.0084\%$. Mars's dip is about
    3.5 times smaller; its orbit is wider, so fewer observers line up to see it cross (about 0.31 per cent against
    0.47 per cent); and it crosses once every 687 days instead of every 365.

## Summary

- Telescope mirrors grew from about 1.2 m in 1789 to 10 m in the 1990s; the 39.3 m ELT is due in Chile in 2029.
- Hubble, Webb, Euclid, AstroSat, Aditya-L1 and soon Xuntian work above the air.
- Planets round other stars were found from 1988 to 1995 in Canada, Puerto Rico and France; more than 6,000 are known.
- Signs of life on other planets, like those on Mars, are still argued over.
- Gravitational waves and neutrinos are new messengers from space.
- People still read the sky by eye, and volunteers help find new objects.
- Light and satellites are taking away the dark sky.
