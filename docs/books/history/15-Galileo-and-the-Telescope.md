# 15. Galileo, and the telescope goes round the world

*In 1608 a spectacle maker in the Netherlands asked for a patent on a tube with two lenses that made far things look
near. Within two years Galileo Galilei had turned one to the sky and seen mountains on the Moon, moons round Jupiter
and Venus going through phases like the Moon. Within five years a telescope had reached Japan, and within a few
decades telescopes were in use in China, Korea, Siam, India and Brazil. This chapter follows the telescope's first
discoveries, the trial that made Galileo famous, and the first views of Mars as something more than a red dot.*

## 15.1 A Dutch spyglass { #invention }

The first record of a telescope is a patent application. On **2 October 1608** Hans Lipperhey, a spectacle maker in
Middelburg, asked the States General of the Netherlands for a patent on an instrument "for seeing things far away as if
they were nearby". A few weeks later Jacob Metius of Alkmaar asked for one too. Neither got a patent, because the idea
was already too easy to copy. Years later the son of another Middelburg spectacle maker, Zacharias Janssen, claimed
that his father had made one first, but the evidence for that is weak.

News of the "Dutch spyglass" spread fast. A report of it went round Europe in October 1608, and in Venice the friar
Paolo Sarpi had it by November. The first person known to draw the sky through one was not Galileo but the English
mathematician **Thomas Harriot**, who drew the Moon on **5 August 1609** with a telescope that magnified six times. He
saw only a "strange spottednesse".

## 15.2 Galileo turns it to the sky { #galileo }

Galileo Galilei was born in Pisa on **15 February 1564**, the son of a musician. From 1592 he taught mathematics at the
University of Padua, in the Republic of Venice. When he heard of the Dutch instrument in 1609 he made his own, at first
magnifying about three times. On **25 August 1609** he showed one of about eight or nine times to the lawmakers of
Venice. Later he made telescopes of up to about 30 times.

**The Moon.** On **30 November 1609** he turned his telescope on the Moon. Harriot had seen it first, but Galileo worked
out what the light and shade meant: the uneven edge between the lit and dark parts came from **mountains and
craters** and their shadows. The Moon was a rough world, not a perfect sphere.

**The Milky Way.** It broke up into a great many faint stars, too close together to see one by one without a telescope.
Al-Tusi at Maragha had written the same thing three and a half centuries before (chapter 8, section 8.8).

## 15.3 Four moons of Jupiter, and a rival { #jupiter }

On **7 January 1610** Galileo saw three small "fixed stars" in a straight line through Jupiter. Night after night they
changed places, on one night one was missing, hidden behind Jupiter, and soon he counted four. They were not stars but **moons going round Jupiter**. Here, plainly, were bodies that did
not go round the Earth, which was a strong blow to the old picture.

He rushed out a short book, *Sidereus Nuncius*, "the starry messenger", printed in Venice on **13 March 1610**, with
more than seventy drawings. It was the first scientific book based on observations through a telescope. He named the
moons the "Medicean stars" after Cosimo II de' Medici, Grand Duke of Tuscany, his old pupil, and the book won him a post
at the Grand Duke's court.

**A rival.** In Germany, **Simon Marius** saw the same moons and noted them on 29 December 1609 in the old calendar he
used, which was 8 January 1610 in Galileo's, one day after Galileo. When Marius published in 1614, Galileo accused him
of copying. In 1903 a Dutch committee looked at the evidence again and decided Marius had found them on his own. The
names we use, **Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto**, are the ones Marius chose, on a suggestion from Kepler.

**Earlier still?** Chinese records say that in 365 BC **Gan De** saw a small reddish star beside Jupiter, which some
think was Ganymede seen with the naked eye. Others doubt it, because Ganymede is far too faint for anyone to see its
colour without a telescope.

## 15.4 Venus, Saturn and the Sun { #venus }

**The phases of Venus.** Late in 1610 Galileo saw that Venus goes through phases like the Moon: a small full disc when
it is far beyond the Sun, a half, and a large thin crescent when it is nearest us. He first sent Kepler a scrambled Latin
sentence, an anagram, and a few months later the real one: "the mother of love imitates the shape of Cynthia", that
is, Venus copies the Moon.

In Ptolemy's system Venus always stays between the Earth and the Sun, so we could only ever see a crescent. A full set
of phases means Venus goes round the Sun. That **ruled out Ptolemy**. But it did **not** prove Copernicus: in Tycho's
system Venus also goes round the Sun (chapter 14, section 14.4), and so many astronomers now moved to Tycho's system or
to others like it. In 1639 the Jesuit Giovanni Zupi saw the phases of Mercury as well.

**Saturn's ears.** In 1610 Saturn looked to Galileo like three bodies, a large one with two small ones at its sides. Later
the side bodies vanished, because the ring was edge on to the Earth, and in 1616 they were back. The puzzle was solved in 1659 by Christiaan
Huygens in the Netherlands: Saturn has "a thin, flat ring, nowhere touching" the planet.

**Spots on the Sun.** Harriot saw sunspots in December 1610. Johannes Fabricius in Frisia saw them on 27 February 1611
and was the first to publish, that year. The Jesuit Christoph Scheiner saw them in 1611 too, and Galileo wrote his
*Letters on Sunspots* in reply, starting a bitter quarrel over who was first. None of them was: Chinese astronomers had
been recording sunspots with the naked eye since 28 BC (chapter 4, section 4.7).

## 15.5 Mars through the early telescopes { #mars }

In September 1610 Galileo began to watch Mars. His telescope was far too weak to show anything on its surface, so he
looked for a phase like Venus's. He was not sure he saw one, but by December he did notice that Mars had grown smaller in his
telescope.

Mars can never show much of a phase. The angle at Mars between the Sun and the Earth is never more than about 41° with
Mars at its average distance, and 46° when Mars is nearest the Sun, so Mars always looks at least 85% lit, at most
slightly gibbous (section 15.9). The Polish astronomer
**Johannes Hevelius** in Gdańsk managed to see that slight phase in 1645.

**The first drawings.** Francesco Fontana in Naples drew Mars through a telescope in 1636. Some historians say he saw no
real markings; others credit him with spotting signs of its turning. The first drawing that clearly shows a real
feature was made by **Christiaan Huygens** on **28 November 1659**: a dark patch now called **Syrtis Major**. Watching it
move, he worked out that Mars turns in about 24 hours, like the Earth, and guessed that Mars was about 60% of the
Earth's size (the true figure is 53%).

**Polar caps.** In 1666 **Giovanni Domenico Cassini** saw the white cap at Mars's south pole, and timed the turning of
Mars as **24 hours 40 minutes**, less than three minutes from today's value. In 1672 Huygens saw a white cap at the north
pole too. Mars was turning into a world with days and seasons, like ours.

**Better telescopes.** These views came from a new design. In 1611 Kepler had shown that a telescope with a convex eyepiece,
instead of Galileo's concave one, would be far more useful, and by the 1650s Huygens was grinding lenses with his brother to make
long telescopes of Kepler's type. With one of his own design he found Saturn's largest moon, Titan, in 1655.

## 15.6 The trial of Galileo { #trial }

**1615 and 1616.** Galileo's discoveries made him a public champion of Copernicus, and that drew the Church in. In 1615
Cardinal **Robert Bellarmine** wrote that the moving Earth could not be defended without "a true physical demonstration"
of it. In February 1616 the Inquisition's experts called
the claim that the Sun stands still at the centre "foolish and absurd in philosophy, and formally heretical". On
**5 March 1616** the Church banned books that taught the Copernican system, suspended Copernicus's own book "until
corrected". Bellarmine had already ordered Galileo to abandon the idea completely.

**1632 and 1633.** In 1623 Galileo's friend and admirer became **Pope Urban VIII**, who let him write about Copernicus as
long as he treated it as a hypothesis. Galileo's *Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems* (1632) was a lively
conversation in which the defender of the old view, Simplicio, kept losing, and was handed the pope's own
favourite argument. The pope felt mocked. Galileo was called to Rome, and on **22 June 1633** the Inquisition found him
"vehemently suspect of heresy". He had to "abjure, curse and detest" the moving Earth, and was kept under house arrest
for the rest of his life, from 1634 at his villa at Arcetri near Florence, close to the convent where his daughter
Maria Celeste was a nun. There he wrote *Two New Sciences* (1638), his great book on motion. He went blind in 1638 and
died on **8 January 1642**, aged 77.

The story that he muttered "*Eppur si muove*", "and yet it moves", after his sentence is probably a legend. It
first appeared in an English book in 1757.

**Both sides.** It is easy to tell this as science against religion, and partly it was. But the evidence in 1633 did
not yet settle the question. No yearly parallax of the stars could be seen, and Tycho's system, with the Earth still,
fitted every telescope observation as well as Copernicus's did. Galileo's own "proof" of the moving Earth was his theory
of the tides, which was wrong. Against that, the Church had made a question of astronomy into a matter of faith, and
punished a man for an idea that turned out to be right. The general ban on books teaching a moving Earth was dropped in
1758, Copernicus's book and the *Dialogue* left the list of banned books in 1835, and on **31 October 1992** Pope John
Paul II said that the judges had erred.

## 15.7 The telescope goes round the world { #world }

The telescope travelled with traders, diplomats and missionaries, often within a few years of its invention.

- **Japan, 1613.** The English captain John Saris brought the retired shogun **Tokugawa Ieyasu** a large silver-gilt
  telescope as a royal gift. It is said to have been the first telescope to leave Europe. Japan later made
  its own: **Iwahashi Zenbei** in Osaka built his first in 1793 and went on to make many, and in 1831 the gunsmith
  **Kunitomo Ikkansai** built a reflecting telescope that magnified 60 times, and drew the Moon with it. Japan's Western astronomy came through Dutch books (chapter 6, section 6.8).
- **China, 1615 to 1626.** The Portuguese Jesuit **Manuel Dias** described the telescope in Chinese in 1615, and an
  Italian cardinal sent one with the Jesuits who sailed in 1618. In 1626 **Johann Adam Schall von Bell** published a
  book in Chinese explaining how it works. The Jesuits went on to work with Chinese scholars on a new calendar
  (chapter 4, section 4.11).
- **Korea, 1631.** **Jeong Du-won**, on a diplomatic mission from Seoul to Beijing, met the Jesuit João Rodrigues at
  Dengzhou in Shandong, who gave him his own telescope. Jeong praised it as a tool of war.
- **Siam, 1685.** **King Narai** watched a lunar eclipse with French Jesuit astronomers at Lopburi.
- **India, 1689.** The French Jesuit **Jean Richaud**, watching a comet from Pondicherry, saw that the bright southern
  star Alpha Centauri is two stars. Yet in the 1720s and 1730s Jai Singh II built his great stone observatories for the
  naked eye (chapter 5, section 5.8).
- **Brazil, 1668.** In Salvador, Bahia, the Jesuit **Valentin Stansel** found a bright comet on 5 March 1668 and sent
  his observations to Europe for publication.

## 15.8 Telescopes at Mars { #onmars }

In Galileo's telescope Mars was a tiny disc. Today the largest telescope
ever sent into deep space is at Mars. **HiRISE**, on NASA's Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter, is a reflecting telescope with
a mirror **0.5 m** across. It has been in orbit since March 2006 and can see things on the ground smaller than a metre.

Mars has telescopes looking back too. From Mars, the Earth is an inner planet, so it shows the full set of phases that
Galileo saw on Venus.

- In March 2004 the rover **Spirit** photographed the Earth as a morning star, an hour before sunrise.
- On 3 October 2007 HiRISE photographed the Earth and the Moon from 142 million km. The angle at the Earth between
  the Sun and Mars was 98°, so less than half of each disc was lit: a fat crescent Earth with a crescent Moon beside it.
- On 31 January 2014 the rover **Curiosity** photographed the Earth and the Moon as two bright evening stars, 80
  minutes after sunset, from 160 million km.

## 15.9 Working it out { #working }

**How big did Mars look?** At an average opposition Mars is 17.8″ across. Galileo's best telescope magnified about 30
times, so Mars looked $17.8'' \times 30 = 534'' \approx 8.9'$ wide. The full Moon, to the naked eye, is about 31.6′
wide. To make Mars look as big as the Moon you would need about $1896 / 17.8 \approx 107$ times.

**How much of a planet is lit?** If the angle at the planet between the Sun and the viewer is $\alpha$, the fraction of
the disc that looks lit is

$$k = \frac{1 + \cos \alpha}{2}.$$

For an outer planet the largest $\alpha$ comes when the line from the planet just touches the viewer's orbit, so
$\sin \alpha = r_\text{viewer} / r_\text{planet}$. For Mars seen from the Earth, $\sin \alpha = 1 / 1.524$, so
$\alpha = 41°$ and $k = 0.88$. When Mars is nearest the Sun, at 1.381, $\alpha = 46°$ and $k = 0.85$. For the Earth seen from Mars in 2007, $\alpha = 98°$ and $k = 0.43$.

**Weighing planets with their moons.** Kepler's third law works for moons too, but the constant depends on the planet:
for any moon, $a^3 / P^2$ is proportional to the mass of the planet it goes round. With $a$ in thousands of km and $P$ in
days:

| Moon | $a$ | $P$ | $a^3/P^2$ |
|---|---|---|---|
| Phobos (Mars) | 9.378 | 0.31891 | 8,110 |
| Deimos (Mars) | 23.459 | 1.26244 | 8,100 |
| The Moon (Earth) | 384.4 | 27.3217 | 76,090 |
| Io (Jupiter) | 421.8 | 1.769138 | 23,977,000 |
| Callisto (Jupiter) | 1,882.7 | 16.689017 | 23,960,000 |

So Jupiter is about $23{,}960{,}000 / 8{,}105 \approx 2{,}960$ times as heavy as Mars; NASA gives 2,958. The Earth comes
out $76{,}090 / 8{,}105 = 9.39$ times as heavy as Mars; NASA gives 9.31, and most of the gap is the Moon's own mass.

**Mars beside Earth.**

| | Mars | Earth |
|---|---|---|
| Planets that show every phase, crescent to full | Mercury, Venus and the Earth | Mercury and Venus |
| Next planet out, and its largest angle $\alpha$ (average distances) | Jupiter, 17° | Mars, 41° |
| So that planet always looks at least | 98% lit | 88% lit |
| $a^3/P^2$ for its moons | about 8,100 | 76,090 |
| Mass, in units of $10^{24}$ kg | 0.642 | 5.97 |

## 15.10 How should we judge Galileo? { #assessment }

**Not first, but first to understand.** Harriot drew the Moon first, Marius found Jupiter's moons on his own, and
Fabricius published on sunspots first. What Galileo did better was to work out what he was seeing, publish it quickly
and clearly, and use it to argue about how the world is built.

**Right and wrong.** He was right about the Moon's mountains, the stars of the Milky Way, Jupiter's moons and the phases
of Venus. He was wrong about the tides. In a long dispute from 1619 to 1623 with the Jesuit Orazio Grassi over the
comets of 1618, Grassi followed Tycho's method and rightly put the comet beyond the Moon, while Galileo's own guesses
about comets turned out to be wrong. His telescope evidence ruled out Ptolemy, but it could not tell Copernicus from
Tycho. That needed physics: Kepler's ellipses and, in chapter 16, Newton's gravity.

**A world instrument.** The telescope spread faster than any earlier astronomical idea. Within twenty-five years it had
reached Japan, China and Korea. But for a long time it travelled with the Jesuits, who taught Tycho's system, so in
much of Asia the telescope arrived before the moving Earth did.

      Wikipedia,

    - The Jesuits and Tycho's system in China: as in chapter 4, section 4.11, and chapter 14, section 14.4.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Who first asked for a patent on a telescope, and when?

??? note "Answer"
    Hans Lipperhey, a spectacle maker in Middelburg in the Netherlands, on 2 October 1608.

**2.** (beginner) Name four things Galileo saw with his telescope in 1609 and 1610.

??? note "Answer"
    Any four of: mountains and craters on the Moon, the many stars of the Milky Way, four moons going round Jupiter,
    the phases of Venus, Saturn's odd shape, and (soon after) sunspots.

**3.** (beginner) Who drew the first clear marking on Mars, and what was it?

??? note "Answer"
    Christiaan Huygens, on 28 November 1659. It was the dark patch now called Syrtis Major.

**4.** (intermediate) Why did the phases of Venus rule out Ptolemy's system but not Tycho's?

??? note "Answer"
    In Ptolemy's system Venus always stays between the Earth and the Sun, so it could only look like a crescent. A
    full set of phases means Venus goes round the Sun. Tycho's system also has Venus going round the Sun, so it gives
    the same phases.

**5.** (intermediate) Why can Mars never look like a crescent from the Earth?

??? note "Answer"
    Mars is outside the Earth's orbit. The angle at Mars between the Sun and the Earth can never be more than about
    41° (46° when Mars is nearest the Sun), so we always see most of its sunlit side: at least 85% of the disc is lit.

**6.** (intermediate) Give one argument on each side of Galileo's trial, using what was known in 1633.

??? note "Answer"
    For the judges' caution: no stellar parallax had been seen, Tycho's system with a still Earth fitted all the
    telescope evidence, and Galileo's tide argument was wrong. Against the judges: they turned a question of
    astronomy into a matter of faith and punished a man for an idea that turned out to be right.

**7.** (advanced) Galileo's telescope magnified about 30 times. At its closest, Mars is 25.6″ across. How wide did it
look, and what fraction of the naked-eye Moon's 31.6′ is that?

??? note "Answer"
    $25.6'' \times 30 = 768'' = 12.8'$, which is $12.8 / 31.6 \approx 0.41$ of the Moon's width, a bit over two
    fifths.

**8.** (expert) Phobos goes round Mars at 9,378 km in 0.31891 days. An imaginary moon goes round the Earth at the same
distance. Using the Earth-to-Mars mass ratio of 9.31, how long would its orbit take? Then explain why, seen from Mars,
the Earth can look like a crescent but Jupiter cannot.

??? note "Answer"
    For the same $a$, $P^2$ is inversely proportional to the mass, so
    $P = 0.31891 / \sqrt{9.31} = 0.31891 / 3.051 = 0.1045$ days, about 2 hours 30 minutes. (Such a moon would be inside
    the Earth, whose radius is 6,371 km, so this is only a sum.) The Earth is inside Mars's orbit, so the angle at the
    Earth between the Sun and Mars can reach 180°, and the Earth shows every phase. Jupiter is outside, and the angle
    at Jupiter between the Sun and Mars is never more than about 17°, so Jupiter always looks at least 98% lit.

## Summary

- The telescope appeared in the Netherlands in 1608. Harriot drew the Moon through one in August 1609.
- Galileo saw mountains on the Moon, stars in the Milky Way, four moons of Jupiter (found independently by Marius), the
  phases of Venus, Saturn's odd shape and sunspots, and published *Sidereus Nuncius* in 1610.
- The phases of Venus ruled out Ptolemy but fitted both Copernicus and Tycho.
- On Mars, Galileo saw only a shrinking disc. Huygens saw Syrtis Major and a day of about 24 hours in 1659, Cassini the
  south polar cap and a day of 24 h 40 m in 1666.
- Galileo was condemned in 1633 and spent his last years under house arrest. The evidence then did not yet prove the
  moving Earth, but the Church judged a scientific question as one of faith; it admitted the error in 1992.
- The telescope reached Japan by 1613, China by 1618, Korea by 1631, and was in use in Siam, India and Brazil by the
  1680s.
- From Mars, the Earth shows phases like Venus, and a telescope at Mars now sees things smaller than a metre.
