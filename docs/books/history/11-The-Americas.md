# 11. The Americas

*The astronomy of the Americas grew up on its own, out of touch with Asia, Europe and Africa, so it shows what people
could work out from the same sky by a different road. This chapter starts with the Maya, who wrote numbers with a
zero, kept calendars that turned like gears and filled their books with tables for Venus, eclipses and Mars. It goes on
to the Mexica of central Mexico, the Inca of the Andes and the farmers who still read the weather in the Pleiades; then
north to Cahokia, Chaco Canyon, the Plains and the Arctic, and east to the Tupinambá of Brazil. It ends with the Pawnee
Morning Star, which may have been Mars, and with the spacecraft that first showed Mars close up.*

## 11.1 Many peoples, few books { #peoples }

Hundreds of peoples lived in the Americas before Europeans arrived, from the Arctic to Tierra del Fuego, and most of
them watched the sky to time farming, hunting and festivals. Only in Mesoamerica (southern Mexico and Central America)
did people write whole books. The Maya writing system and their place-value numbers were invented there, with no help
from outside.

Very few of those books survive. The Maya wrote on folding strips of paper made from the bark of a wild fig tree. Of
the thousands of books they made, most were lost after the Spanish conquest, and some were burned on purpose: on
12 July 1562, at Maní in Yucatán, the Franciscan **Diego de Landa** burned Maya
books (27, by his own count; some historians think many more) and about 5,000 images. He wrote that the books held
"nothing in which were not to be seen as superstition and lies of the devil". Only **four** Maya books are known today.

So most of what we know of astronomy in the Americas comes from other kinds of evidence: buildings and their
alignments (chapter 1 met the thirteen towers of Chankillo in Peru, section 1.8), carved dates on stone, accounts
written by Spanish chroniclers, and knowledge still held by Indigenous peoples today.

## 11.2 Counting in twenties, with a zero { #count }

**Numbers.** The Maya wrote numbers with three signs: a **dot** for 1, a **bar** for 5 and a **shell** for 0. With
these they could write any digit from 0 to 19, and bigger numbers were stacked in a column, in powers of twenty: a
**base-20** place-value system with a real zero. The oldest dates using it are found outside the Maya homeland, so the
zero and the Long Count were probably invented by an earlier people, possibly the Olmec.

**Two counts that mesh.** Every day had a place in two cycles at once:

- the **Tzolk'in**, a count of **260 days**, made by pairing the numbers 1 to 13 with 20 day names;
- the **Haab'**, a year of **365 days**: 18 months of 20 days and a short period of 5 days, the **Wayeb'**, thought
  to be a dangerous time.

A day such as "4 Ajaw 8 Kumk'u" names its place in both. The same pair of names comes back only after **18,980 days**,
which is 52 Haab' years. This cycle is called the **Calendar Round** (section 11.11 shows why the number is 18,980).

**The Long Count.** For history the Maya needed something longer, so they counted days from a starting date in the
mythical past. The units were:

| Unit | Days |
|---|---|
| k'in | 1 |
| winal = 20 k'in | 20 |
| tun = 18 winal | 360 |
| k'atun = 20 tun | 7,200 |
| b'ak'tun = 20 k'atun | 144,000 |

A date was written as five numbers, such as 9.12.2.0.16. The count starts at the creation date 13.0.0.0.0 4 Ajaw 8
Kumk'u, which by the most widely used correlation is **11 August 3114 BC** (in the Gregorian calendar run backwards).
One of the oldest Long Count dates, on Stela 2 at Chiapa de Corzo in Mexico, is probably from **36 BC**.

**The Moon.** Maya inscriptions record the age of the Moon and whether a month had 29 or 30 days. Since they did not
use fractions, they used a rule: **149 months in 4,400 days**. That makes a month of 29.5302 days; the true average is
29.530589 days.

**A Maya astronomer's notes.** In 2012 archaeologists published the walls of a small room at **Xultun** in Guatemala,
painted in the Late Classic period, centuries before any surviving Maya book. Its walls are covered with calendar
sums: a lunar table and very long counts of time, like the rough work of astronomers. One of these texts is signed by its
author, **Sak Tahn Waax**, "White-chested Fox", the first Classic Maya astronomer known by name. His text takes the
2,920-day cycle of Venus (section 11.3) and splits it in new ways, using the 260-day count, the year and the 780-day
cycle of Mars.

**The end of the world that wasn't.** The Long Count reached 13.0.0.0.0 again on **21 December 2012**. Some people
claimed the Maya had predicted the end of the world for that day. Maya scholars said no Classic Maya text forecast
any disaster; the count simply rolls on, as a car's odometer does.

## 11.3 The Dresden Codex: Venus, eclipses and Mars { #dresden }

The finest of the four surviving Maya books is the **Dresden Codex**, 78 pages long, from the region of Chichén Itzá
in Yucatán. Its date is uncertain; estimates run from the 11th to the 14th century. Johann Christian Götze, director
of the royal library in Dresden, bought it in Vienna in 1739. In 1880 another Dresden librarian, **Ernst
Förstemann**, published a full copy and worked out that its numbers were Maya calendar dates and Long Counts. The book
survived the bombing of Dresden in February 1945, but was badly soaked in a flooded basement.

**Venus.** Like the Babylonians (chapter 2, section 2.5), the Maya followed Venus as a morning star and an evening
star. Six pages of the Dresden Codex (24 and 46 to 50) are a **Venus table**. It lists the days on which Venus should first appear and
disappear as morning and evening star, for 65 Venus cycles, about 104 years. The first appearance as morning star was
thought to be a time of danger, and the table gave warning of it. The Maya used a Venus cycle of **584 days**; the true
average is 583.92 days, so errors built up. The codex seems to give ways to correct them, and the table was used at
least four times, with different starting dates, from the 10th to the 14th century.

Five Venus cycles make 2,920 days, which is **eight years of 365 days**. So every eight years Venus is back in the
same place at the same season. The picture shows the pattern this makes in the sky.

**Eclipses.** Another part of the book is an **eclipse table** of 405 lunar months, about 33 years. It warns of all solar
eclipses and most lunar ones, but cannot say which will be seen from the Maya lands; that depends on where the Moon's
shadow falls. With its corrections it could be used from the 8th century to the 18th.

**Mars.** The Dresden Codex has three Mars tables. Pages 43b to 45b follow Mars through its **780-day** cycle, from
one time it is opposite the Sun to the next, with most attention on the weeks when Mars moves backwards against the
stars and shines brightest. The table is set at the backward loop of **AD 818**. The true average is 779.94 days.
Note that 780 = 3 × 260, so the Mars cycle fits exactly three Tzolk'in counts. Chinese astronomers watched the same
loops of Mars (chapter 4, section 4.8).

**Another Venus book.** The **Maya Codex of Mexico** (once called the Grolier Codex) is a Venus almanac of which ten
pages survive. Its authenticity was disputed for decades, but in 2018 Mexican scientists dated it to between **1021
and 1154**, which makes it the oldest surviving book of the Americas.

**Buildings.** At Chichén Itzá, a round tower called the **Caracol** has windows that line up with the farthest north
and south points Venus reaches, and it is often called an observatory.

## 11.4 The Mexica: binding the years { #mexica }

The **Mexica**, often called the Aztecs, ruled central Mexico from their city of Tenochtitlan until the Spanish
conquest. They kept the same two counts as the Maya, 260 days and 365 days, and so the same 52-year cycle.

At the end of every 52 years came the **New Fire ceremony**, the "binding of the years". In the last five days of the
old cycle people threw away hearth stones, pots and other household goods, and every fire was put out. At sunset on the
last day, priests walked from the city to a hill called Huixachtlan on the shore of Lake Texcoco. There they watched
the **Pleiades**, which they called *Tianquiztli*, "the marketplace". If the Pleiades crossed the top of the sky at
midnight, the world was safe for another 52 years. A new fire was lit on the chest of a captive, whose heart was cut
out and thrown into the flames, and the fire was carried to the great temple and from there to every home.

**The Sun Stone.** The great carved disc found in Mexico City on 17 December 1790, during repairs to the cathedral, is
often called the "Aztec calendar". It is 3.6 m across and weighs about 24.6 tonnes, with a face in the middle that is often said to be
the Sun god. But it was probably not used to count days: archaeologists at Mexico's National Museum of Anthropology
think it was more likely a ceremonial basin or altar used for sacrifices.

## 11.5 The Inca: pillars, lines and the dark river { #inca }

The **Inca** built the largest state in the Americas, stretching along the Andes from today's Ecuador to Chile, with
its capital at **Cusco** in Peru. They kept a solar calendar and a lunar calendar side by side. One of their greatest
feasts, **Inti Raymi**, the festival of the Sun, was held in the month of the June solstice, which in the southern hemisphere
is midwinter.

**Pillars on the horizon.** Several Spanish chroniclers of the 16th century described stone **pillars** on the hills
round Cusco, which are thought to have marked the Sun's place on the horizon at the solstices. None survive: looting after the conquest and
the growth of the modern city destroyed them. In 1998 the archaeologist Brian Bauer and the astronomer David Dearborn
reported the first remains of such pillars ever found, on the **Island of the Sun** in Lake Titicaca. At the June solstice the Inca king
and the high priests stood in a plaza beside a sacred rock and watched the Sun set between two pillars. Ordinary
pilgrims stood on a second platform outside the wall; from there the Sun set over the nobles, who called themselves
the children of the Sun. Chankillo, with its thirteen towers (chapter 1, section 1.8), shows that people in Peru had
marked the Sun on the horizon more than 1,500 years earlier.

**Lines from the Sun temple.** From the **Coricancha**, the temple of the Sun in Cusco, 41 or 42 lines called *ceques*
ran out across the land, with about **328 shrines** (*huacas*) along them. The anthropologist Tom Zuidema
suggested that the shrines stood for the days of a calendar: 328 days is about twelve circuits of the Moon round the
sky, and some lines may have been sight lines to the horizon. This remains a theory. The lines also mark out the lands
of the different family groups of Cusco, and many follow springs and canals.

**Animals in the dark.** The Inca and their descendants see the Milky Way as a river, the **Mayu**, and they named
not only groups of stars but also the **dark clouds** of dust along it. The largest is **Yacana**, the llama, a
mother with her young, whose eyes are the bright stars Alpha and Beta Centauri. There is also a toad near the
Southern Cross, a serpent, and a fox near the tail of Scorpius. The sky river was thought to gather water from the
Earth and give it back as rain. Aboriginal Australians made a constellation from the dark clouds too, the Emu in the
Sky (chapter 1, section 1.9).

## 11.6 Reading the weather in the Pleiades { #pleiades }

In the high Andes of Peru and Bolivia, Quechua and Aymara farmers have watched the Pleiades for more than 400 years to
decide when to plant potatoes. They watch the sky in the week before the feast of San Juan (24 June), and on the
feast night they climb the mountains to look at the cluster before dawn. If the Pleiades look bright and big, they expect good rains and plant on time. If they look dim, they
expect a dry season and plant later.

In 2000 the anthropologist Benjamin Orlove and the climate scientists John Chiang and Mark Cane put this to the test,
in a paper in *Nature*. Using satellite records they found that the Pleiades look dim in June in years when thin, high
cirrus cloud, too thin to notice, covers the sky. Those clouds are a sign of a coming **El Niño**, the warming of the
Pacific that months later brings less rain to the Andes in the growing season. **The farmers' forecast works.**

## 11.7 North America: a woodhenge, a sun dagger and a guest star { #north }

**Cahokia.** In Illinois, near the Mississippi, stood **Cahokia**, the largest ancient site north of the great cities
of Mexico. Between AD 1050 and 1100 its centre grew from fewer than 3,000 people to between 10,000 and 15,000. In the early 1960s,
while digging ahead of a new highway, the archaeologist **Warren Wittry** found arcs of large post holes west of
Monks Mound, the city's great earthen pyramid. He suggested they were whole circles of posts used as a **solar
calendar**. The best known, Woodhenge III, had 48 posts in a ring 120 m across. It was rebuilt in
the 1980s, and from its centre the post for the equinox sunrise lines up with Monks Mound; crowds now gather there
at the solstices and equinoxes. How exactly the circles were used is still Wittry's idea, not something the builders wrote
down.

**The Sun Dagger.** In **Chaco Canyon**, New Mexico, the Ancestral Puebloans flourished around AD 1000.
In 1977 the artist **Anna Sofaer**, recording rock art on Fajada Butte, saw a "dagger" of sunlight cut through a spiral
carved behind three leaning stone slabs. At about 11:15 in the morning of the summer solstice the dagger cuts
through the larger spiral; other patterns of light mark the winter solstice and the equinoxes. Sofaer and her colleagues also claimed
the site marks the 18.6-year cycle of the Moon. Most experts accept the solar markings. Fewer accept the Moon: the
astronomer Michael Zeilik found no sign that later Pueblo peoples cared about that cycle, and he thought the site
was more likely a sun shrine, since watching the sunrise against the horizon gives a better calendar. The butte was
closed to visitors in the 1980s, and in 1989 one slab was found to have shifted, loosened by erosion from visitors'
feet.

**A guest star?** Under a rock overhang near Peñasco Blanco in Chaco Canyon is a painting of a hand, a crescent Moon
and a star. Some think it shows the supernova of **1054**, which Chinese astronomers recorded as a guest star (chapter
4, section 4.7): on the morning of 5 July 1054 the crescent Moon was close to it. It is a lovely idea that cannot be
proved. Paintings like this are hard to date closely, a Moon beside Venus or Jupiter would look much the same, and
the idea does not explain why the even brighter supernova of 1006, also seen there, was not painted instead.

## 11.8 Stars of the Plains, the Arctic and Brazil { #plains }

**The Pawnee.** The Skidi Pawnee lived in villages of earth lodges by the rivers of the Great Plains. In their beliefs
the **Morning Star** and the **Evening Star** were the parents of the first Pawnee woman.
Section 11.10 returns to the Morning Star.

**The Inuit.** Far in the north, around latitude 69° N, the Sun does not rise at all from about the end of November
to the middle of January. In those dark weeks the stars told the Inuit the time of day: when to wake, and when the
hunters should set out. When the pair of stars **Aagjuuk** (Altair and Tarazed, in Aquila) first appear, around the
middle of December, they announce the winter solstice and the coming return of light. The three
stars of Orion's belt are **Ullaktut**, "the runners", chasing their prey; the bright star Rigel is **Kingulliq**, a
hunter who fell behind because he dropped his glove. The seven stars of the Big Dipper are **Tukturjuit**, the caribou.

**The Tupinambá.** In 1614 the French friar **Claude d'Abbeville**, who had spent four months with the Tupinambá in
Maranhão, near the mouth of the Amazon, published an account of their life. He wrote that they "attribute the high and
low tides to the moon", and knew that the highest tides come at full Moon and new Moon. That was before Galileo, who
in 1632 wrongly blamed the tides on the Earth's own motions (chapter 15). D'Abbeville also recorded Tupi names for
star groups: *curuça*, the Southern Cross; *seichu*, the Pleiades; *tuibaé*, the old man; and *nhandutin*, the rhea,
the big flightless bird of South America. The Brazilian astronomer **Germano Bruno Afonso**, who read the book in
the late 1970s, later found that the Guarani, more than 3,000 km away, have the same constellations nearly 400 years
on.

## 11.9 Claims put to the test { #claims }

The ancient Americas attract big claims. Some hold up, some do not, and some cannot be decided.

**The Nazca lines.** In the desert of southern Peru, between about 500 BC and AD 500, the Nazca people made huge
lines and figures by clearing away the reddish-brown surface pebbles. The historian **Paul Kosok** and the mathematician
**Maria Reiche** suggested they were an astronomical
calendar, pointing to where the Sun and stars rose and set. Later the archaeoastronomers **Gerald Hawkins** and
**Anthony Aveni** tested this and found the evidence too weak. In 1985 the archaeologist **Johan Reinhard** published
evidence that the Nazca religion centred on mountains and water, and argued that the lines were part of rituals for
water.

**The serpent of Chichén Itzá.** Near the equinoxes, the afternoon Sun throws a row of triangle-shaped shadows down
the stairs of the great pyramid of Chichén Itzá, so that a feathered serpent seems to crawl down it. Thousands come to
watch every March. But the effect can be seen for several weeks around the equinox, and nobody knows whether the
builders planned it.

**What holds up.** The Dresden Codex tables, the Calendar Round and the Long Count are written down and can be
checked, and they work. The Sun Dagger's solstice markings are widely accepted. The
Andean Pleiades forecast has been confirmed with satellites (section 11.6). The 2012 doomsday claim (section 11.2)
does not hold up.

## 11.10 Mars in the Americas { #mars }

**The red Morning Star.** The Skidi Pawnee held a **Morning Star ceremony**. A captive girl was kept through the
winter; in spring, when the Morning Star rose ringed with red, the priest knew it was time, and she was sacrificed to
the star. The last known sacrifice was on **22 April 1838**, of a 14-year-old Oglala Lakota girl named Haxti. Pawnee
leaders such as Petalesharo worked to end the practice.

Which planet was the Morning Star? The earliest accounts said **Venus**. Most later ethnographers favoured **Mars**,
because the star was described as red; some suggested **Jupiter**. Calculations show that Venus was in the morning sky
at the ceremonies of 1827 and 1838, so the question is still open.

**Mars close up.** The first spacecraft to show Mars close up came from the Americas. **Mariner 4**, launched by NASA
on 28 November 1964, flew past Mars on 15 July 1965 at a distance of 9,846 km. It sent back 21 pictures and part of a
22nd, covering about 1% of the planet. They showed craters like the Moon's and a very thin atmosphere, and ended most
hopes of finding intelligent life there (chapter 17 tells the story of the "canals"). On 20 July 1976 **Viking 1**
landed and sent the first clear picture from the surface.

Canada joined in too. The **Phoenix** lander, the first to land in Mars's polar region, on 25 May 2008, carried a weather
station from the Canadian Space Agency. Its laser saw **snow falling from Martian clouds**, which nobody had known
happened. And Chile's **Atacama Desert**, the driest non-polar desert on Earth, is used by NASA to test instruments
for Mars.

On 18 February 2021 the rover **Perseverance** landed in **Jezero Crater**, an ancient river delta, to look for signs
of past life and collect samples. Its helicopter **Ingenuity** made the first powered flight on another planet in
April 2021. In July 2024 Perseverance found a rock, **Cheyava Falls**, with a "possible biosignature": features that
living things could have made, though other causes have not been ruled out.

## 11.11 Working it out { #working }

**The Calendar Round.** When do the 260-day and 365-day counts come back to the same pair of days? We need the least
common multiple. Split each number into primes:

$$260 = 2^2 \times 5 \times 13, \qquad 365 = 5 \times 73.$$

They share only the 5, so

$$\mathrm{lcm}(260, 365) = 2^2 \times 5 \times 13 \times 73 = 18{,}980 \text{ days} = 73 \times 260 = 52 \times 365.$$

**Venus, the year and the Tzolk'in.** Five Venus cycles of 584 days make 2,920 days, exactly 8 Haab' years, but not a
whole number of Tzolk'in counts: 2,920 / 260 = 11.23. Thirteen times longer, everything fits:

$$13 \times 2920 = 37{,}960 = 65 \times 584 = 104 \times 365 = 146 \times 260.$$

That is why the Venus table of the Dresden Codex runs for 65 cycles and 104 years: then Venus, the year and the
Tzolk'in all come back together. It is also two Calendar Rounds.

But the true Venus cycle is 583.92 days, so 65 real cycles take $65 \times 583.92 = 37{,}954.8$ days, about 5 days less
than the table. That is why the table needed corrections when it was reused.

**The Maya month.** The rule "149 months in 4,400 days" gives

$$\frac{4400}{149} = 29.53020 \text{ days},$$

against the true 29.530589. In 4,400 days the real Moon runs $149 \times 29.530589 = 4400.058$ days, so the rule is
only 0.058 days, less than an hour and a half, out.

**Mars.** The Maya Mars cycle of 780 days is 0.06 days longer than the true 779.94, so it drifts by a day in about 17
cycles, or 36 years.

**The Long Count.** Thirteen b'ak'tuns is $13 \times 144{,}000 = 1{,}872{,}000$ days. Dividing by the length of the
year of the seasons, 365.2422 days, gives 5,125.4 years: from 11 August 3114 BC to 21 December 2012.

**Synodic periods from Mars.** The time between two meetings of planets, the **synodic period** $S$, comes from the
two orbital periods $P_1$ and $P_2$:

$$\frac{1}{S} = \left| \frac{1}{P_1} - \frac{1}{P_2} \right|.$$

A Maya astronomer living on Mars would have needed different tables:

| Cycle of | Seen from Mars | Seen from Earth |
|---|---|---|
| Venus | 333.9 days | 583.9 days |
| Earth and Mars (each seen from the other) | 779.9 days | 779.9 days |
| Jupiter | 816.4 days | 398.9 days |

From Mars, Venus comes round faster, because Mars moves slowly and Venus laps it quickly. Jupiter comes round more
slowly, because Mars is closer to Jupiter's speed and takes longer to overtake it.

## 11.12 How should we judge the astronomy of the Americas? { #assessment }

**A second road to the same sky.** With no telescopes, no Greek geometry and no contact with the Old World, the Maya
reached a place-value system with a zero, a month correct to within a minute, and tables that warned of eclipses and
followed Venus and Mars for centuries. Like the Babylonians (chapter 2), they did it with arithmetic of cycles rather
than with models of the heavens.

**What was lost.** Only four Maya books survive. The Inca kept their records, calendars among them, on knotted cords
called *khipu*, and left no books. Much of what we know was written down by Spaniards, sometimes by the same people
who destroyed the rest: Landa's own account of the Maya later helped scholars learn to read Maya writing. Any judgement of the astronomy of the
Americas rests on a small part of what there was.

**What is still alive.** Inuit star lore, the dark constellations of the Andes and the Pleiades forecast are not only
history. They are held by living people, and the last of these has passed a modern scientific test.

**Claims in both directions.** As in Africa (chapter 10), the history has suffered from people who ignored Indigenous
knowledge and from people who invented things for it, such as the 2012 doomsday. The cure is the same: look at the
evidence and say how strong it is.

    - See the sources for sections 11.1 to 11.11.
    - Inca records, including calendars, kept on knotted cords: Wikipedia, [Quipu](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Quipu)
      (weaker). Landa's account and the reading of Maya writing: Wikipedia,
      [Diego de Landa](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diego_de_Landa) (weaker).

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Which three signs did the Maya use to write numbers, and what base did they count in?

??? note "Answer"
    A dot for 1, a bar for 5 and a shell for 0. They counted in base 20.

**2.** (beginner) How long is the Calendar Round, in days and in years?

??? note "Answer"
    18,980 days, which is 52 years of 365 days (and 73 Tzolk'in counts).

**3.** (beginner) What do Andean farmers look for in the Pleiades in June, and what do they do about it?

??? note "Answer"
    Whether the Pleiades look bright or dim. If they are dim, they expect a dry growing season and plant their potatoes
    later.

**4.** (intermediate) Why do only four Maya books survive?

??? note "Answer"
    Of thousands of Maya books, most were lost after the Spanish conquest, and some were burned on purpose: Diego de
    Landa burned 27 by his own count at Maní in 1562.

**5.** (intermediate) Give one reason for and two against reading the Peñasco Blanco painting as the supernova of 1054.

??? note "Answer"
    For: on the morning of 5 July 1054 the crescent Moon stood close to the supernova, as in the painting. Against (any
    two): such paintings are hard to date closely; a Moon beside Venus or Jupiter would look much the same; the idea
    does not explain why the brighter supernova of 1006 was not painted.

**6.** (intermediate) Was the Pawnee Morning Star Mars? Give the evidence on each side.

??? note "Answer"
    For Mars: it was described as red, and most ethnographers favoured Mars. For Venus: the earliest accounts said
    Venus, and calculations show Venus was in the morning sky at the ceremonies of 1827 and 1838. The question is still
    open.

**7.** (advanced) The Maya took 584 days for Venus. By how many days were 65 of their cycles off from 65 real ones? And
by how much does their Mars cycle of 780 days drift in 10 cycles?

??? note "Answer"
    $65 \times 584 = 37{,}960$ and $65 \times 583.92 = 37{,}954.8$, so about **5.2 days**. For Mars,
    $10 \times (780 - 779.94) = 0.6$ days.

**8.** (expert) A Mars base keeps a 260-sol ritual count beside a calendar year of 669 sols. How many sols until the two
come back into step? How long is that in Earth years? (One sol is 1.02749 days.)

??? note "Answer"
    $669 = 3 \times 223$ and $260 = 2^2 \times 5 \times 13$ share no factor, so the lcm is
    $260 \times 669 = 173{,}940$ sols: 260 Mars calendar years. That is $173{,}940 \times 1.02749 = 178{,}722$ days, or
    about **489 Earth years**, more than nine times the Maya Calendar Round.

## Summary

- The Americas developed astronomy on their own; few books survive, after the burnings of the Spanish conquest.
- The Maya counted in base 20 with a zero and kept the 260-day Tzolk'in, the 365-day Haab', the 52-year Calendar
  Round and the Long Count, which rolled over on 21 December 2012 with no disaster.
- The Dresden Codex has tables for Venus (584 days, 65 cycles in 104 years), eclipses (405 months) and Mars (780 days).
- The Mexica watched the Pleiades cross the sky at midnight to know the world would last another 52 years.
- The Inca marked the solstice Sun with pillars, ran ceque lines from the Sun temple, and named dark shapes in the
  Milky Way.
- Andean farmers' Pleiades forecast of the rains was confirmed by satellite data in 2000.
- Cahokia's woodhenge and Chaco's Sun Dagger probably marked the Sun; claims about the Moon, the 1054 supernova and the
  Nazca lines are weaker.
- The Pawnee Morning Star may have been Mars or Venus. Mariner 4, Viking 1, Phoenix and Perseverance brought Mars
  close.

Australians keep some of the oldest living star knowledge, and the Māori new year begins with the rising of Matariki.
