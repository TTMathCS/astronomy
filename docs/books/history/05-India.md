# 5. India

*Indian astronomy began as a way of timing sacrifices: priests needed to know the phase of the Moon and the season to
the day. Over two thousand years it grew into one of the great mathematical traditions of the world. It took in ideas
from Babylon and from the Greeks, and it gave to Baghdad and to China. Aryabhata had the Earth turning on its axis in
AD 499; Brahmagupta wrote rules for zero; astronomers in Kerala found endless series for the sine two centuries before
Europe; and a king in Jaipur built a sundial 27 metres high. This chapter also shows the arguments, because few
subjects have been pulled about by national pride, in both directions, as much as the history of Indian astronomy.*

## 5.1 The Moon's stations and the Vedic year { #vedic }

The oldest Indian texts are the Vedas, hymns and ritual books in Sanskrit, and they are very hard to date. Their sky is mostly a sky for ritual. The year
of the ritual books has 360 days, twelve months of thirty, and sacrifices are timed by the Moon.

**The nakshatras.** As in China (chapter 4, section 4.4), the path of the Moon was split into stations, one for each
night of its circuit of the sky. India calls them **nakshatras**. The *Atharvaveda* lists 28; later astronomers dropped
one, the star Abhijit, and used 27 equal parts of the zodiac, each $360^\circ / 27 = 13^\circ 20'$ wide. The Moon goes
round the sky in 27.3 days, so it spends about a day in each. In the old lists the first nakshatra is **Krittika**, the
Pleiades; in later lists the count starts from the point opposite the star Spica, called Chitra.

Whether the Chinese mansions and the Indian nakshatras share one origin, and if so where, is an old question with no
agreed answer.

**Dating by the stars.** Because the sky slowly shifts with precession (chapter 2, section 2.9), a text that says where
the Sun stood at an equinox or a solstice seems to carry its own date. The *Shatapatha Brahmana* says the Pleiades "do
not swerve from the east", that is, they rise due east. That was exactly true around 2950 BC, and some writers use it to
date the text that early. But the Pleiades still rose within about 8° to 13° of east in the 8th to 6th centuries BC,
the usual date given for the text, and a priest's "due east" need not be exact. The same problem runs through the whole
subject: an astronomical description gives a date only if the description was meant to be precise and was made at the
time, and both are hard to prove.

## 5.2 The Vedanga Jyotisha { #vedanga }

The first Indian text that is really about astronomy is the **Vedanga Jyotisha**, "the limb of the Veda on the
lights", a short book of verses traditionally said to be by **Lagadha**. It exists in two versions, one attached to
the *Rigveda* and one to the *Yajurveda*.

**A five-year calendar.** The Vedanga Jyotisha sets up a cycle of five years, a **yuga**, that begins when the Sun and
the new Moon meet at the star Dhanishtha (in our constellation Delphinus) on the day of the winter solstice. In five
years it counts:

- 1,830 days, so each year is 366 days;
- 62 months, two of them extra (leap) months, added after every 30 months;
- 1,860 **tithis**, lunar days, each a thirtieth of a month. Because there are 30 more tithis than days in a yuga, one
  tithi is dropped every 61 days.

This is a calendar of averages. It ignores the changing speeds of the Sun and Moon, and section 5.11 shows how quickly it
would slip.

**When was it written?** This is a real argument among scholars, and it matters because the date is used to date the
Vedas themselves.

- **Early.** The text puts the winter solstice at Dhanishtha. Because of precession, that was true around 1400 to
  1200 BC. T. K. S. Sastry and R. Kochhar take the text to have been written when it describes, between about 1370 and
  1150 BC. David Pingree computed the described solstice to about 1180 BC, but said it was not clear what that had to do
  with the date of the text.
- **Late.** Michael Witzel points out that the Sanskrit is late, that the solstice point need not be meant exactly, and
  that the contents resemble Babylonian works. He puts the text in the last centuries BC. Santanu Chakraverti dates it
  after 700 BC, and suggests that an older observation was carried into a later book.

A fair summary is that the observation behind it may be old, but the text as we have it is probably from the second
half of the first millennium BC.

## 5.3 A day 3:2 long: did Babylon reach India? { #babylon }

The Vedanga Jyotisha says the longest day of the year lasts 18 **muhurtas** and the shortest 12, out of 30 muhurtas in
a whole day and night. That is a ratio of 3 to 2, or 14.4 hours of daylight at midsummer and 9.6 at midwinter.

Babylon used the same ratio. The tablets of MUL.APIN and later Babylonian schemes give the longest and shortest days in
a ratio of either 2:1 or 3:2 (chapter 2, section 2.3).

How long the longest day is depends on latitude: the further from the equator, the longer the summer days. So the ratio
is a clue to where it was measured. The curve below shows the real ratio at different latitudes, worked out in section
5.11.

**Pingree's case.** David Pingree argued that the ratio fits a latitude of about 35° N, far north of the old Indian
heartland on the Ganges, and that India took it, with other ideas, from Mesopotamia.

**The reply.** Yukio Ohashi and others answer that a latitude of about 34° N is northern India too: the valley of
Kashmir and the region of Kabul and Gandhara lie there. On this view the Vedanga Jyotisha records a real observation in
the north-west, and the agreement with Babylon is no proof of borrowing.

**What the curve shows.** Neither place fits perfectly. At Babylon itself the ratio is about 1.44, not 1.5; on the
Ganges plain it is about 1.3. The 3:2 ratio is right at about 35° N. A round ratio like 3:2 may be a simple scheme rather than a
measurement made anywhere. The question is open; what is
certain is that both traditions used the same simple number.

## 5.4 Greek stars in Sanskrit { #yavana }

Greek astronomy reached India from the 4th century BC, after Alexander's campaigns, and kept coming through the early
centuries AD. A Greek sundial of the 3rd or 2nd century BC has been found at Ai-Khanoum, a Greek city in what is now
Afghanistan. Sanskrit called the Greeks **Yavanas**.

**The Yavanajataka.** The "Nativity according to the Greeks" is one of the earliest Sanskrit books of horoscopes. In a
famous study of 1978 David Pingree argued that it was a verse version made in AD 269/270 by Sphujidhvaja, of a Greek
prose book written in Alexandria by Yavaneshvara in AD 149/150. Pingree also argued that it held the earliest Indian
use of a decimal place-value system with zero.

That picture has been challenged. Bill Mak, working from newly found manuscripts, argues that Pingree's readings of the
key passages rested on faulty corrections to the text; that the book is full of Indian material (medicine, Hindu gods,
military astrology) absent from Greek sources; and that no Greek original has ever been found. The date is now
disputed, with proposals from after AD 22 to the early 7th century. What nobody disputes is that Greek horoscopy
reached India in the early centuries AD, through this book and others.

**Five systems in one book.** In the 6th century the astronomer **Varahamihira** (about 505 to 587) of Ujjain wrote the
*Pancha-siddhantika*, "The Five Treatises", which summarised five rival systems of astronomy. Two of them are openly
Western: the *Romaka Siddhanta*, "the doctrine of the Romans", which uses the 19-year cycle of Meton in the form
Hipparchus gave it, and the *Paulisa Siddhanta*. The book is our best record of Indian astronomy before Aryabhata's
school took over.

## 5.5 Aryabhata and the turning Earth { #aryabhata }

**Aryabhata** wrote his *Aryabhatiya* in AD 499, when, he tells us, he was 23 years old, so he was born in 476. He
worked at Kusumapura, part of Pataliputra, near modern Patna. His short book, in verse, became the base of a whole
school of Indian astronomy.

**The Earth turns.** Nearly everyone then, in India as in Greece, thought the sky turned around a still Earth once a
day. Aryabhata said the Earth turns on its axis instead:

> Just as a man in a boat moving forward sees the stationary objects on the shore as moving backward, just so are the
> stationary stars seen by the people on earth as moving exactly towards the west.

He gave the number of turns of the Earth in a great cycle of years, which comes to a **sidereal day** (one turn
relative to the stars) of 23 hours 56 minutes 4.1 seconds. The modern value is 23 h 56 min 4.091 s. His sidereal year
was 365 days 6 hours 12 minutes 30 seconds, about three minutes too long.

The idea had followers: five centuries later the Persian scholar al-Biruni reported that Aryabhata's school held that
the Earth turns. Other astronomers argued hard against rival schools; Brahmagupta filled a whole chapter of his book
with such criticism (section 5.6).

**Eclipses.** Popular belief blamed eclipses on Rahu and Ketu, demons that swallow the Sun and Moon. Aryabhata explained
them by shadows: the Moon passing into the Earth's shadow, or the Moon's shadow falling on the Earth. Rahu and Ketu
survived as names for the two points where the Moon's path crosses the Sun's, which is where eclipses happen.

**The sine table.** Greek astronomers used tables of chords. Aryabhata's book holds the oldest surviving table of
**half-chords**, the ancestor of our sine. It gives 24 values, one for every 3°45′ of a quarter circle, for a circle of
radius 3,438. That odd radius is the number of minutes of arc in a radian: a circle of circumference $360 \times 60 =
21{,}600$ minutes has radius $21{,}600 / 2\pi \approx 3{,}438$. Aryabhata's names for the sine (*jya*) and cosine (*kojya*)
shaped the later history of trigonometry.

**Pi.** Aryabhata gave the circumference of a circle 20,000 across as 62,832, which makes $\pi = 3.1416$.

## 5.6 Brahmagupta, Bhaskara and the observatory at Ujjain { #brahmagupta }

**Brahmagupta** (598 to about 668) came from Bhillamala, now Bhinmal in Rajasthan. In 628, aged 30, he finished the
*Brahmasphutasiddhanta*, "the correctly established doctrine of Brahma", and in 665 a practical handbook, the
*Khandakhadyaka*.

- His book is the first to give rules for calculating with **zero** and with negative numbers.
- He argued about astronomy fiercely; one chapter of his book is entirely criticism of rival schools.
- He described the Earth's pull on things. In the Persian scholar al-Biruni's report of his views: "all heavy things fall
  down to the earth by a law of nature, for it is the nature of the earth to attract and to keep things, as it is the
  nature of water to flow". This is a description, not a law of gravity with numbers, but it is an early statement that
  the Earth attracts.
- His books went to Baghdad in the 770s and were translated into Arabic as the *Sindhind* (section 5.10).

**Ujjain.** The city of Ujjain was the reference meridian of Indian astronomy, its "Greenwich", from which longitudes
were counted, and the main centre of mathematical astronomy. Varahamihira worked there, and so, five centuries later,
did **Bhaskara II** (1114 to 1185), head of its observatory. In 1150, aged 36, he wrote the *Siddhanta Shiromani*,
"Crest-jewel of the treatises". In it he separated a planet's mean motion from its true motion at each moment, and
noticed that when a planet's apparent motion reaches its greatest value, its rate of change there is zero: the idea
behind the turning points of the planets.

**Mars in the Indian books.** The Sanskrit name of Mars is **Mangala**, also *Lohita*, "the red one"; Tuesday is
*Mangalavara*, Mars's day. The Indian books gave Mars's period round the stars to within half an hour (section 5.11).

## 5.7 The Kerala school { #kerala }

From the 14th to the 16th century a chain of teachers and pupils in Kerala, in the far south-west of India, did some of
the most original mathematics of the age. They were astronomers, and they needed better ways to compute sines and the
positions of the Moon and planets.

**Madhava** of Sangamagrama (about 1340 to 1425) found **infinite series**: ways of writing the sine, the cosine and the
arctangent as sums of endlessly many terms that get smaller and smaller. One of them gives π:

$$\frac{\pi}{4} = 1 - \frac{1}{3} + \frac{1}{5} - \frac{1}{7} + \cdots$$

In Europe the first such series came from James Gregory in 1667, and this one is now often called the
Madhava–Gregory–Leibniz series. We know of Madhava's series mainly through later Kerala books that credit them to him.

**Nilakantha Somayaji** (1444 to 1544) finished his *Tantrasangraha* in 1500–1501. In it, and in his commentary on
Aryabhata, he built a model in which Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn go round the Sun, and the Sun goes round
the Earth. It is much like the system Tycho Brahe would propose in Denmark eighty years later (chapter 14).

**Jyeshthadeva** wrote the *Yuktibhasha* around 1530, in Malayalam, the local language, not Sanskrit. It gathers the
school's results and, unusually for Indian texts, gives proofs and derivations.

**Did it reach Europe?** Some historians have suggested that the Kerala series reached Europe through merchants and
Jesuit missionaries. Kerala traded with China, Arabia and Europe, so the route
was possible. But there is no direct manuscript evidence that it happened. The mathematician David Bressoud wrote that
there is "no evidence that the Indian work on series was known beyond India, or even outside of Kerala, until the
nineteenth century". Both claims should be stated with care: the Kerala results are real and came first; the transfer
to Europe is unproven.

## 5.8 Jai Singh's stone observatories { #jantar }

In the early 18th century **Jai Singh II** (1688–1743), the Maharaja of Amber, found that the
astronomical tables of his day did not match the sky. He decided to make new ones, and to do it he built observatories
of masonry, in which the instruments are buildings. He built five, at **Delhi**, **Mathura**, **Varanasi**, **Ujjain**
and **Jaipur**. Delhi's was finished in about 1724. The Jaipur one, finished in 1734, is the best preserved; it has nineteen instruments, and it is a UNESCO
World Heritage Site. Mathura's has disappeared.

**The Samrat Yantra.** The "king of instruments" at Jaipur is a sundial 27 m high. Its great sloping edge points at the
celestial pole, so it rises at 27°, the latitude of Jaipur. On each side a curved scale lies parallel to the equator,
and the edge's shadow moves across it as the Sun crosses the sky, about a millimetre a second, or a hand's breadth a
minute. It is said to give local time to about two seconds.

**The tables.** Jai Singh's tables, the *Zij-i Muhammad Shahi*, named after the Mughal emperor, were used in India for a
century, though they made little mark outside it. They drew heavily on existing tables, including those of Ulugh Beg of
Samarkand (chapter 8) and of the French astronomer Philippe de La Hire. Jai Singh's observatories were built at a time
when European astronomers had been using telescopes for over a century, and they are among the last great
observatories built for the naked eye.

## 5.9 Two zodiacs and a national calendar { #calendars }

**A lunisolar calendar on a star year.** The traditional Hindu calendars use lunar months, like the Chinese and
Babylonian ones, but tie the year to the stars, not to the seasons. Twelve lunar months make about 354 days; the
**sidereal year**, one trip of the Sun round the stars, is about 365. Every 32 or 33 months an extra month, the
**adhika masa**, is added. Each solar month begins with a **sankranti**, when the Sun enters a new sign of the zodiac;
a lunar month in which no sankranti happens becomes the extra month. (Compare the Chinese rule in chapter 4, section
4.5.)

**Two zodiacs.** Western astrology and the Western calendar count the zodiac from the spring equinox: the **tropical**
zodiac, tied to the seasons. Indian astronomy counts it from the stars: the **sidereal** zodiac. Because of precession
the two slide apart by about 1° every 72 years. The gap between them is called the **ayanamsa**. The most widely used
value in India, Lahiri's, makes it about 24.2° in 2026.

You can see this in a festival. **Makar Sankranti** marks the Sun entering the sidereal sign Makara (Capricorn). Its
other name, *Uttarayana*, means the Sun's turn northward, which really happens at the winter solstice. Today it falls on 14 January,
more than three weeks after the real solstice on 21 or 22 December, because the two zodiacs have slipped about 24°
apart and the Sun moves about 1° a day.

**The Indian national calendar.** After independence the government asked a committee led by the astrophysicist
**Meghnad Saha** to reform the calendar. Its national calendar, in use from 22 March 1957 (1 Chaitra 1879 of the Saka
era), has solar months fixed to the Gregorian dates; Chaitra begins on 22 March, or 21 March in a leap year. The
traditional calendars carry on beside it for festivals.

## 5.10 Out of India and back again { #exchange }

Indian astronomy did not stay at home.

**To Baghdad.** In the early 770s a Sanskrit treatise was brought from India to the court of the caliph al-Mansur in
Baghdad. Translated into Arabic by **al-Fazari**, it became the *Zij al-Sindhind*, the "Great tables of the Sindhind"
(from *siddhanta*), and al-Khwarizmi worked on it. Aryabhata's work was translated into Arabic around 820 and cited
by al-Khwarizmi. Through Arabic, Indian methods later reached Spain and Latin Europe. Chapter 8 follows this story.

**To China.** At the Tang court the astronomer **Gautama Siddha**, born in Chang'an to a family that had come from
India generations before, led the compilation of a great astrological treatise and translated an Indian calendar,
the *Navagraha*, into Chinese. In 718 he introduced Indian numerals with a sign for zero. Chinese mathematicians did
not take them up, because their counting rods already worked by place value.

**Into India.** Indian astronomy absorbed Babylonian ideas (perhaps), Greek horoscopy and geometry (certainly), and
later Persian and European tables in Jai Singh's time.

**India in modern astronomy.** The Kodaikanal Solar Observatory, founded in the hills of Tamil Nadu in 1899, is where
John Evershed found in January 1909 the flow of gas in sunspots now called the Evershed effect. In 1920
Meghnad Saha's ionisation equation linked the spectra of stars to their temperatures, one of the foundations of
astrophysics (chapter 18). And on 24 September 2014 India's **Mars Orbiter Mission**, nicknamed *Mangalyaan*, "Mars
craft", went into orbit around Mars after a 298-day journey, making India the first Asian nation to reach Mars orbit,
and on its first attempt (chapter 21).

## 5.11 Working it out { #working }

**How fast did the Vedanga Jyotisha slip?** Its year was $1{,}830 / 5 = 366$ days, against a real year of the seasons of
365.2422 days, too long by 0.758 days a year, or 3.8 days in each five-year yuga. Its month was $1{,}830 / 62 = 29.516$
days, about 21 minutes short of the real 29.5306. After a single yuga, real time had run 1,826.2 days for the Sun but
1,830.9 days for the 62 months, so the Sun and Moon no longer met where the calendar said, and the calendar needed
correcting as the centuries went by.

**Where is the longest day 3:2?** On the longest day the Sun is at its greatest declination $\delta = \varepsilon$, the
Earth's tilt (about 23.8° around 1000 BC). The Sun rises and sets when its hour angle $H$ satisfies

$$\cos H = \frac{\sin h_0 - \sin\varphi \sin\delta}{\cos\varphi \cos\delta},$$

where $\varphi$ is the latitude and $h_0 = -0.83^\circ$ allows for the bending of light near the horizon and the size of
the Sun's disc. The day lasts $2H / 15$ hours ($H$ in degrees). Do the same with $\delta = -\varepsilon$ for the
shortest day and divide:

| Place | Latitude | Longest day | Shortest day | Ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Ujjain | 23.2° N | 13.6 h | 10.7 h | 1.27 |
| Pataliputra | 25.6° N | 13.8 h | 10.5 h | 1.31 |
| Babylon | 32.5° N | 14.3 h | 10.0 h | 1.44 |
| Kabul | 34.5° N | 14.5 h | 9.8 h | 1.48 |
| Nineveh | 36.4° N | 14.7 h | 9.6 h | 1.53 |

The ratio reaches 1.5 at about 35.3° N (about 35.0° N if we ignore refraction). For the physics of day length, see
[Days, years and seasons](https://physics.akadian.ca/topics/gravity/level-2/06-days-years-seasons.html).

**Mars's year in the Indian books.** The table compares the time Mars takes to go once round the stars, and the time the
Earth takes (the sidereal year), in the Indian texts and today:

| Source | Mars, once round the stars | Earth, once round the stars |
|---|---|---|
| *Surya Siddhanta* | 686 d 23 h 56 min 23.5 s | 365 d 6 h 12 min 36.6 s |
| Bhaskara II, *Siddhanta Shiromani* | 686 d 23 h 57 min 1.5 s | 365 d 6 h 12 min 9 s |
| Modern | 686 d 23 h 30 min 41.4 s | 365 d 6 h 9 min 9.5 s |
| Error of the *Surya Siddhanta* | 26 min too long | 3.5 min too long |

Mars's period was right to about 26 minutes in 687 days, better than one part in 38,000. (Ptolemy's value in the same
table, 686 d 23 h 31 min 56.1 s, is closer still, at 1.2 minutes.) More on Mars's year in the physics lesson
[The Mars year](https://physics.akadian.ca/topics/spacetime/level-4/04-the-mars-year.html).

**When did the two zodiacs agree?** Precession moves the equinox about 50.29″ a year. A gap of 24.2° in 2026 took

$$\frac{24.2 \times 3{,}600''}{50.29''\text{ a year}} \approx 1{,}730 \text{ years},$$

so the two zodiacs started together around AD 290, in the age of the classical siddhantas. Each further 72 years or so
moves Makar Sankranti about one more day later in the year.

**How big is the Samrat Yantra's scale?** The shadow moves around the scale once a day, as the Earth turns $2\pi$
radians in 86,400 seconds. If it moves 1 mm a second, the scale's radius is

$$r = \frac{0.001 \text{ m/s}}{2\pi / 86{,}400 \text{ s}} \approx 13.8 \text{ m}.$$

So a scale about 14 m in radius, which fits a building 27 m high. On Mars, where a sol is 88,775 seconds, the same scale
would show the shadow moving 2.7% slower.

**Madhava's series is slow.** Ten terms of $1 - \tfrac13 + \tfrac15 - \cdots$ give $4 \times 0.7605 = 3.0418$, still
wrong in the second decimal place. The Kerala mathematicians knew this, and gave faster series and correction terms;
one of Madhava's, $\pi = \sqrt{12}\left(1 - \frac{1}{3 \cdot 3} + \frac{1}{5 \cdot 3^2} - \frac{1}{7 \cdot 3^3} +
\cdots\right)$, gives π to 11 decimal places in 21 terms.

## 5.12 How should we judge Indian astronomy? { #assessment }

Few parts of the history of science have been argued over so hotly, and the arguments pull in two opposite directions.

**Inflation.** Some writers push Indian astronomy back thousands of years, by reading exact dates into every
astronomical line in the Vedas (section 5.1), or claim that the Kerala school "invented calculus" or that ancient India
knew everything modern science knows. Historians of mathematics have criticised claims such as that differentiation was
understood in India from the 10th century, or that Bhaskara II was "the precursor of Newton and Leibniz". The real
achievements do not need this help.

**Dismissal.** Older European histories often treated Indian astronomy as a copy of the Greeks, a footnote to Ptolemy.
That is just as wrong. Aryabhata's turning Earth and sine table, Brahmagupta's zero, the Kerala series and
Nilakantha's model are original, and Indian methods shaped astronomy in Baghdad and beyond.

**A fair picture.** India built a long, mathematical astronomy that borrowed and lent. It probably took ideas from
Babylon and certainly from the Greeks, it turned them into something of its own, and it handed its tables and
methods on to the Islamic world, and through it to Europe. Many historians see its strength in calculation more than
observation: long series of dated observations, like Babylon's diaries or China's records, are rare in the Indian
sources. Jai Singh's stone instruments came late, when telescopes had already
changed astronomy elsewhere.

      criticism of them: Wikipedia, [Kerala school of astronomy and mathematics](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kerala_school_of_astronomy_and_mathematics)
      (weaker; it quotes Plofker).
    - For the overall picture, K. Plofker, *Mathematics in India* (see Further reading).

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) What is a nakshatra, and how many are there?

??? note "Answer"
    One of the stations of the Moon along its path round the sky, about one for each night. The *Atharvaveda* lists 28;
    later astronomy used 27 equal parts of 13° 20′ each.

**2.** (beginner) What did Aryabhata say about the daily turning of the sky, and what comparison did he use?

??? note "Answer"
    That the stars stand still and the Earth turns on its axis, so the stars only seem to move west. He compared it to a
    man in a moving boat who sees the shore moving backwards.

**3.** (beginner) Why does Makar Sankranti fall on 14 January and not at the winter solstice?

??? note "Answer"
    It is set by the sidereal zodiac, counted from the stars, while the solstice belongs to the seasons. Precession has
    moved the two about 24° apart, and the Sun takes about 24 days to cover that.

**4.** (intermediate) In the Vedanga Jyotisha, a yuga has 62 months and 1,830 days. How long is its month, and how
long its year?

??? note "Answer"
    $1{,}830 / 62 = 29.52$ days for the month, and $1{,}830 / 5 = 366$ days for the year.

**5.** (intermediate) Why do historians argue about whether the 3:2 ratio of day lengths came from Babylon?

??? note "Answer"
    The ratio is right at about 35° N, not on the Ganges, and Babylonian texts use the same ratio, so Pingree saw
    borrowing. But north-western India (Kashmir, Kabul) lies near 34° N, so the ratio could be a local observation; and
    at Babylon itself the true ratio is about 1.44, so it may be a round scheme anywhere. It is unresolved.

**6.** (intermediate) Aryabhata's sine table uses a circle of radius 3,438. Where does that number come from?

??? note "Answer"
    It is the number of minutes of arc in a radian. A circle of circumference $360 \times 60 = 21{,}600$ minutes has a
    radius of $21{,}600 / 2\pi \approx 3{,}438$ minutes, so arcs and sines are measured in the same unit.

**7.** (advanced) At what latitude would the longest day be exactly 3:2 the shortest, if the Earth's tilt were 23.8°
and we ignored refraction?

??? note "Answer"
    The longest day must be 14.4 hours, so the half-day hour angle $H = 14.4 \times 15 / 2 = 108^\circ$. With no
    refraction, $\cos H = -\tan\varphi \tan\varepsilon$, so $\tan\varphi = -\cos 108^\circ / \tan 23.8^\circ = 0.309 /
    0.441 = 0.701$, and $\varphi \approx 35.0^\circ$.

**8.** (expert) The *Surya Siddhanta* gives Mars's period round the stars as 686 d 23 h 56 min 23.5 s. Modern value: 686 d
23 h 30 min 41.4 s. What is the relative error, and how far off would its Mars be after 1,000 years if nothing else
were wrong?

??? note "Answer"
    The error is 25.7 minutes in 686.98 days, a relative error of $25.7 / (686.98 \times 1{,}440) \approx 2.6 \times
    10^{-5}$. In 1,000 years Mars goes round about $365{,}250 / 686.98 \approx 532$ times; the model's Mars falls behind by
    $532 \times 360^\circ \times 2.6 \times 10^{-5} \approx 5^\circ$. That is why siddhantas needed correcting from time
    to time.

## Summary

- Vedic astronomy timed rituals by the Moon and a 360-day year; the Moon's 27 or 28 nakshatras mark its path. Dating the
  Vedas by astronomy is attractive but unreliable.
- The Vedanga Jyotisha set a five-year cycle of 1,830 days and 62 months. The solstice it describes fits about 1400 to
  1200 BC, but the text is probably from the last centuries BC.
- Its 3:2 ratio of longest to shortest day matches Babylonian texts and a latitude of about 35° N. Pingree saw
  borrowing; others see an observation in north-west India.
- Greek horoscopy entered India in the early centuries AD (the Yavanajataka, whose date is now disputed); Varahamihira
  summarised five systems in the 6th century.
- Aryabhata (499) had the Earth turning, explained eclipses by shadows, and gave the first sine table.
- Brahmagupta (628) wrote rules for zero and described the Earth's attraction; Bhaskara II (1150) worked at Ujjain, the
  meridian of Indian astronomy.
- The Kerala school found infinite series for the sine and for π, and Nilakantha's model of 1500 resembles Tycho's.
  Transmission to Europe is unproven.
- Jai Singh's masonry observatories (Jaipur, 1734) include a sundial 27 m high.
- Indian calendars use a sidereal zodiac, now about 24° from the tropical one; the national calendar of 1957 came from
  Saha's committee.
- Indian astronomy reached Baghdad (the Sindhind, 770s) and China (718), and in 2014 India reached Mars orbit.
