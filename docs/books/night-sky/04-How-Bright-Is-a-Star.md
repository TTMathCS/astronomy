# 4. How bright is a star? Magnitudes

*Some stars blaze and some you can only just make out. Astronomers measure the difference with a scale that is more
than two thousand years old and works backwards: the smaller the number, the brighter the star. This chapter explains
the scale, from Ptolemy's six classes to the exact step that Norman Pogson fixed in 1856. It shows how faint your eyes
can reach, follows stars whose brightness changes, and separates how bright a star looks from how bright it really
is. It ends with a question that is still argued: does the eye really judge brightness in steps?*

## 4.1 Which is brightest? { #brightest }

On a clear night, look for the brightest point of light in the sky. Very often it is not a star at all but a planet.
**Venus** outshines everything except the Sun and the Moon.
**Jupiter** comes next, and **Mars**, when it is closest to us, can be just as bright.

The brightest star is **Sirius**, in Canis Major, which you found from Orion's Belt in chapter 3. After it come
Canopus, Alpha Centauri, Arcturus, Vega and Capella. From there the stars get fainter and fainter until they fade into
the dark, and the faintest you can see depends on your eyes and your sky.

A star can look bright for two reasons: because it really gives out a lot of light, or because it is close. Sirius is
both fairly powerful and very near, only 8.6 light-years away. Section 4.7 untangles the two.

## 4.2 Six classes of stars { #classes }

The oldest scale we know comes from Ptolemy's star catalogue in the *Almagest*, written around AD 150 (chapter 3,
section 3.7). He put every star into one of six classes. The brightest were of the **first magnitude**, the next of
the second, and so on down to the **sixth magnitude**, the faintest that can be seen without a telescope.

It is usually said that Ptolemy took the scale from Hipparchus, nearly three centuries earlier. That cannot be proved,
because Hipparchus' catalogue is lost. The one text of his that survives, a commentary on the *Phenomena* of Aratus, a
Greek work on the constellations, never gives brightness as a number. He calls stars "big" or "small", "bright" or
"faint", or even "visible at full moon".

In 964 the astronomer **al-Sufi**, at the court in Isfahan, corrected Ptolemy's star list in his *Book of Fixed
Stars*. He gave each star a magnitude too, and just over half of them agreed with Ptolemy's.

## 4.3 The modern scale { #scale }

Astronomers still use Ptolemy's scale, but now it is measured with instruments and runs in both directions.

- **The smaller the number, the brighter.** A star of magnitude 1 is brighter than a star of magnitude 2.
- **Very bright things have negative magnitudes.** Sirius is −1.46, Venus can reach −4.8, the full Moon is −12.74
  and the Sun is −26.74.
- **Magnitudes need not be whole numbers.** Polaris is 1.98 and Vega 0.03.
- **There is no faintest magnitude.** Telescopes reach far beyond the sixth. The James Webb Space Telescope has found
  galaxies fainter than magnitude 29.

## 4.4 How faint can you see? { #limit }

The faintest star you can see is your **limiting magnitude**. It depends on how dark your sky is, how well your eyes
have adapted (chapter 1), and how sharp they are.

| Where you are | Faintest stars |
| --- | --- |
| In a city | about magnitude 3 |
| In a fairly dark suburb | about 5 |
| The limit of the old star maps, made before telescopes | about 6 |
| A dark country sky far from cities | about 7 |
| The sharpest eyes at the clearest, most remote sites | nearly 8 |

One observer, Curtis, found his limit was 6.53. But when he looked through a hole in a black
screen, so that no stray light reached his eye, he saw a star of magnitude 8.3.

**The horizon steals starlight.** A star near the zenith shines through the least air. As it sinks, its light passes
through more and more air, which dims it. At 30° above the horizon a star's light crosses about twice as much air as
at the zenith, and at the horizon about 38 times as much. It is also what gives the setting Sun its orange
colour. It is also why the limits in the table are for stars high in the sky.

**Twinkling** is another effect of the air. As starlight passes through turbulent layers of air, its brightness,
its colour and even its position flicker. Stars twinkle most near the horizon, where their light
crosses the most air.

**Try it: the horse and rider.** In the handle of the Big Dipper, **Mizar** (magnitude 2.04) has a faint companion
only 12′ away, **Alcor**, at magnitude 3.99. Arab observers called the pair the horse and rider, and they make a good
test of eyesight. Can you see it?

## 4.5 Pogson's step { #pogson }

For centuries the magnitude of a star was a matter of judgement, and each class was thought to be about twice as bright
as the next. There were no instruments to check it.

In 1856 the English astronomer **Norman Pogson**, then at the Radcliffe Observatory in Oxford and later at the
observatory in Madras, India, chose a rule that closely matched the old catalogues: **five magnitudes are exactly a
factor of 100 in brightness.** So one magnitude is the number that, multiplied by itself five times, gives 100:

$$\sqrt[5]{100} \approx 2.512.$$

This is **Pogson's ratio**. The Harvard and Potsdam star catalogues of the 1880s spread it, and it became the
standard.

| Difference in magnitude | 1 | 2 | 3 | 4 | 5 | 10 | 15 |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Brightness ratio | 2.512 | 6.31 | 15.8 | 39.8 | 100 | 10,000 | 1,000,000 |

So Sirius, at −1.46, is about 24 times as bright as Polaris, at 1.98 (section 4.10 shows how). The Sun is about
400,000 times as bright as the full Moon.

**Where is zero?** A scale of steps needs a starting point. The Harvard Photometry of 1884 used the average of 100
stars near Polaris to fix magnitude 5.0. Later the UBV system of Harold Johnson and William Morgan, from the 1950s,
set magnitude 0 by the average of six stars like Vega, which is why Vega itself comes out at 0.03 rather than exactly
0.

## 4.6 Stars that change { #variable }

Most stars shine steadily, but some change their brightness. You can watch three of them with your own eyes.

**Algol, the Demon Star**, in Perseus, stays at magnitude 2.1 for most of the time. Then every 2.87 days (2 days, 20
hours and 49 minutes) it fades to 3.4 and comes back, in about ten hours. It is really a system of three stars. Two of
them pass in front of each other, and the deep dip happens when the fainter one passes in front of the brighter. Its
name comes from the Arabic for "the head of the ghoul", and the Greeks saw it as the head of the Gorgon. Those demon
names suggest that people noticed its winks long ago, though nobody can prove it. The Italian astronomer Geminiano
Montanari recorded its changes in 1667. In 1783 a British amateur, John Goodricke, found the period and suggested that
something dark passes in front of the star, which proved right when Edward Pickering showed in 1881 that Algol's dips
are eclipses.

**Mira, "the wonderful"**, in Cetus, is a red giant star that pulsates. David Fabricius saw it in August 1596
and thought it was a new star, until it faded from view and then came back in 1609. Its period is about 332 days. At
its brightest it usually reaches about magnitude 3.5, sometimes 2.0; at its faintest it drops to about 10, far below
what the eye can see. Johannes Hevelius gave it its name in 1662.

**Betelgeuse**, Orion's shoulder, wanders between about 0.0 and 1.6. From October 2019 it faded noticeably. By
February 2020 it had fallen to a record 1.614, about a third of its usual light. The cause was a cloud of dust, made
from gas the star had thrown out, which blocked part of its surface. Some people wondered if it was about to explode
as a supernova. It was not, though it is expected to one day, most likely within the next 100,000 years.

**Try it.** Compare Algol with nearby stars of known magnitude on a few nights, or on one night over several hours
around a predicted dip. Amateur astronomers have done useful work this way for a long time; the Variable Star
Section of the British Astronomical Association was founded in 1890.

## 4.7 Near and far { #absolute }

How bright a star **looks** is its **apparent magnitude**, the number we have used so far. To compare how bright stars
**really** are, astronomers imagine moving every star to the same distance, 10 parsecs, which is 32.6 light-years. The
magnitude a star would have there is its **absolute magnitude**.

| Star | Apparent magnitude | Absolute magnitude |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Sun | −26.74 | 4.83 |
| Sirius | −1.46 | 1.4 |
| Vega | 0.03 | 0.6 |
| Betelgeuse | about 0.5 | −5.8 |
| Rigel | 0.13 | −7.8 |
| Deneb | 1.25 | −8.4 |

From the same distance Sirius would still beat the Sun, but only by about 3½ magnitudes, not 25. Rigel and Deneb, by
contrast, are giants: at 32.6 light-years they would outshine every planet and cast shadows at night. Deneb looks
fainter than Vega only because it is so much farther away.

The absolute magnitudes of stars run from about −10 for the most powerful to about +20 for the faintest.

## 4.8 Reading brightness around the world { #world }

People have used the brightness of stars for much more than ranking them.

- **The Andes.** For more than 400 years, Quechua- and Aymara-speaking farmers in Peru and Bolivia have watched the
  Pleiades in the week before the feast of San Juan, 24 June. If the cluster looks dim and small, they expect less
  rain about six months later and delay planting their potatoes. In 2000 scientists found why it works: thin, high
  cirrus cloud, which dims the Pleiades, appears in June before an El Niño, which months later brings less rain to the
  Andes.
- **Aboriginal Australia.** Peoples who follow the Seven Sisters also use the brightness of the stars to predict the
  seasonal rains.
- **Arabia.** The horse and rider, Mizar and Alcor, made an eyesight test (section 4.4). The faint star had a telling
  name, *al-Suhā*, "the forgotten" or "the neglected".
- **The Mi'kmaq** tell of a great bear and seven hunters. Mizar is Chickadee, and the faint Alcor beside him is his
  cooking pot.

## 4.9 Magnitudes on Mars { #mars }

The stars are so far away that their magnitudes are the same from Mars as from Earth (chapter 3, section 3.10). What
changes is everything in the solar system.

- **The Sun is fainter.** Mars is about 1.52 times as far from the Sun as the Earth, so it gets only about 43% as much
  sunlight. That is still as bright as a slightly cloudy afternoon on Earth. The Sun's magnitude from Mars is about
  −25.8 instead of −26.74.
- **The brightest thing at night is Phobos.** This moon of Mars reaches magnitude −9 to −10, bright, but far short
  of our full Moon.
- **The daytime sky hides the stars.** The air of Mars is thin, but full of dust that scatters sunlight, so the sky
  is too bright by day to see stars.
- **Mars itself** is one of the brightest objects in our sky at its best, reaching −2.94 from Earth, as bright as
  Jupiter. Chapter 14 explains why its brightness changes so much.

## 4.10 Working it out { #working }

**From magnitudes to brightness.** If two stars have magnitudes $m_1$ and $m_2$, the ratio of their brightness $b$ is

$$\frac{b_1}{b_2} = 10^{0.4\,(m_2 - m_1)},$$

and the other way round,

$$m_1 - m_2 = -2.5 \log_{10} \frac{b_1}{b_2}.$$

The 0.4 is just 2/5: five magnitudes give $10^{0.4 \times 5} = 10^2 = 100$, Pogson's rule.

- Sirius and Polaris: $10^{0.4\,(1.98 + 1.46)} = 10^{1.376} \approx 24$.
- The Sun and the full Moon: $10^{0.4\,(-12.74 + 26.74)} = 10^{5.6} \approx 400{,}000$.
- Algol in a dip: $10^{0.4 \times 1.3} = 10^{0.52} \approx 3.3$ times fainter.
- Mira from 2.0 to 4.9: $10^{0.4 \times 2.9} = 10^{1.16} \approx 14.5$, the "almost 15 times" its range is said to be.

**Distance and magnitude.** Light spreads out as it travels, so a star $d$ times farther away looks $d^2$ times
fainter (the inverse square law). In magnitudes that is a change of $5 \log_{10} d$. Comparing a star at distance $d$
parsecs with the same star at 10 parsecs gives

$$m - M = 5 \log_{10} \frac{d}{10}.$$

Sirius is 2.64 parsecs away: $M = -1.46 - 5 \log_{10} 0.264 = -1.46 + 2.89 = 1.43$, the 1.4 in section 4.7.

The Sun is 1 AU away, and a parsec is 206,265 AU, so $d = 1/206{,}265$ parsecs and
$m - M = 5 \log_{10} (1 / 2{,}062{,}650) = -31.57$. Then $m = 4.83 - 31.57 = -26.74$, just what NASA gives.

How far away could you still see the Sun? Set $m = 6$: $5 \log_{10}(d/10) = 6 - 4.83 = 1.17$, so
$d = 10 \times 10^{0.234} \approx 17$ parsecs, about 56 light-years. Beyond that our Sun would vanish from the naked
eye.

**The Sun from Mars.** Mars is 227.956 / 149.598 = 1.524 times as far from the Sun as the Earth.

| | Mars | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mean distance from the Sun | 1.524 AU | 1 AU |
| Sunlight, $1/d^2$ | 43% | 100% |
| The Sun's magnitude, $-26.74 + 5 \log_{10} d$ | −25.83 | −26.74 |
| Brightest moon at night | Phobos, −9 to −10 | the Moon, −12.74 |

From the table, our full Moon is $10^{0.4\,(12.74 - 9.5)} \approx 20$ times as bright as Phobos at its best.

## 4.11 Does the eye judge in steps? { #senses }

The magnitude scale treats equal *ratios* of brightness as equal steps: from magnitude 1 to 2 is a factor of 2.512,
and so is 5 to 6. Is that how we actually see?

**Fechner's answer: yes.** In 1860 Gustav Fechner, building on Ernst Weber, published the first book of
psychophysics, the study of how we sense the world. Weber had found that the smallest change you can notice is a
fixed fraction of what is already there. Fechner went on to argue that sensation grows as the **logarithm** of the
stimulus. If he is right, equal ratios of light really do feel like equal steps, and the magnitude scale is a scale of
sensation. Note the dates: Pogson fixed his ratio in 1856, four years before Fechner's book. Astronomers were using a
logarithmic scale before anyone had named the law behind it.

**Stevens's answer: not quite.** The American psychophysicist Stanley Smith Stevens (1906–1973) asked people to put
numbers on how strong things felt, and found that sensation grows as a **power** of the stimulus, $\psi = k I^a$, with
a different exponent $a$ for each sense. For a point of light he found $a = 0.5$, and for a 5° patch seen in the dark
$a = 0.33$. With $a = 0.5$ a star 100 times as bright as another looks only $100^{0.5} = 10$ times as bright, and one
magnitude looks like $2.512^{0.5} \approx 1.6$ times, not 2.512.

**Who is right?** Weber's law, and so Fechner's, holds roughly over a wide middle range but breaks down for very faint
light near the limit of vision, the very place where astronomers count the faintest stars. Stevens's law is often said
to cover a wider range, down to zero. But his method has been criticised for fitting curves to averages, which hides
the large differences between one person and another. It has even been shown that a power law can be derived from
Fechner's.

This is why astronomers no longer trust the eye to define magnitudes. Since Pogson, the scale has been fixed by
measurement: a factor of 100 in light is five magnitudes, whatever it feels like. The ancient guess that each class
was twice as bright as the next was a judgement of how the stars *looked*; the modern 2.512 is a statement about how
much light *arrives*.

    - Stevens's power law, Stevens's dates, the wider range, the exponents 0.5 for a point source and 0.33 for a 5°

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Which is brighter, a star of magnitude 1 or one of magnitude 3?

??? note "Answer"
    The star of magnitude 1. On the magnitude scale smaller numbers mean brighter.

**2.** (beginner) What is the brightest star in the night sky, and what is its magnitude?

??? note "Answer"
    Sirius, at magnitude −1.46. Venus, Jupiter and sometimes Mars are brighter, but they are planets.

**3.** (beginner) Why do stars look fainter near the horizon?

??? note "Answer"
    Their light passes through much more air, which dims it: about twice as much at 30° up, and about 38 times as much
    at the horizon, as at the zenith.

**4.** (intermediate) How many times brighter is a magnitude 1 star than a magnitude 6 star? And than a magnitude 2
star?

??? note "Answer"
    100 times than the magnitude 6 star, by Pogson's rule. About 2.512 times than the magnitude 2 star.

**5.** (intermediate) What makes Algol dim every 2.87 days?

??? note "Answer"
    Two of the stars of Algol pass in front of each other. When the fainter one passes in front of the brighter one,
    it blocks part of its light, and Algol drops from magnitude 2.1 to 3.4 and back in about ten hours.

**6.** (intermediate) Deneb looks fainter than Vega. Is it really less powerful?

??? note "Answer"
    No. Deneb's absolute magnitude is −8.4 and Vega's is 0.6, so Deneb gives out far more light. It looks fainter
    because it is much farther away.

**7.** (advanced) Jupiter at its brightest is −2.94 and Venus −4.8. How many times brighter is Venus? And what would
the Sun's magnitude be from Jupiter, 5.2 AU from the Sun?

??? note "Answer"
    $10^{0.4\,(4.8 - 2.94)} = 10^{0.744} \approx 5.5$ times. From Jupiter the Sun would be
    $-26.74 + 5 \log_{10} 5.2 = -26.74 + 3.58 = -23.16$.

**8.** (expert) Under Stevens's power law with $a = 0.5$, how much brighter would a first-magnitude star *look* than a
sixth-magnitude one? Why does this not make Pogson's scale wrong?

??? note "Answer"
    The light ratio is 100, so it would look $100^{0.5} = 10$ times as bright. Pogson's scale does not claim to measure
    sensation: it is defined by the amount of light, five magnitudes for a factor of 100. How bright that looks is a
    question for psychology, and the answer depends on the law and on the person.

## Summary

- Magnitudes run backwards: the smaller the number, the brighter, and very bright things are negative.
- The Sun is −26.74, the full Moon −12.74, Venus up to −4.8 and Sirius −1.46.
- Ptolemy sorted stars into six classes; al-Sufi re-judged them by eye in 964.
- The faintest star you can see ranges from about 3 in a city to nearly 8 at the darkest sites.
- Stars near the horizon are dimmed by the extra air their light crosses, and twinkle more.
- Since Pogson in 1856, five magnitudes are exactly a factor of 100, so one magnitude is 2.512.
- Algol, Mira and Betelgeuse change in brightness, and you can follow them by eye.
- Absolute magnitude is how bright a star would look from 10 parsecs; it shows how powerful a star really is.
- From Mars the stars look the same, but the Sun is about −25.8 and Phobos is the brightest thing at night.
- Whether the eye senses brightness as a logarithm or a power is still argued, so the scale is fixed by measurement.
