# 12. Eclipses

*A few times a year the Sun, the Moon and the Earth line up, and one of them passes into another's shadow. When the
Moon blocks the Sun, day turns briefly to night and the Sun's glowing outer atmosphere appears round a black disc.
When the Earth's shadow falls on the full Moon, the Moon turns a deep red. This chapter explains how eclipses happen,
why they do not happen every month, how to watch them safely, and how eclipses have shaped history and science. It
lists the eclipses coming in the next few years, visits Mars, where the moons are too small ever to cover the Sun, and
ends by racing the Moon's shadow across the Earth.*

    - why there is not an eclipse every month

## 12.1 Lining up { #lineup }

Everything lit by the Sun casts a shadow into space. An eclipse happens when one body passes into another's shadow.

- A **solar eclipse** happens at **new Moon**, when the Moon passes between the Sun and the Earth, and the Moon's shadow
  falls on the Earth.
- A **lunar eclipse** happens at **full Moon**, when the Earth is between the Sun and the Moon, and the Moon passes
  into the Earth's shadow.

Every shadow in space has two parts. In the dark central cone, the **umbra**, the Sun is completely hidden. Around it
is the **penumbra**, where only part of the Sun is hidden. If you stand in the Moon's umbra you see a total eclipse of
the Sun; in its penumbra, a partial one.

## 12.2 Why not every month? { #seasons }

There is a new Moon and a full Moon every month, so why are eclipses rare?

The Moon's orbit is tilted about **5.1°** to the Earth's orbit (chapter 11, section 11.9). At most new Moons the Moon
passes a little above or below the Sun as seen from Earth, and its shadow misses us. At most full Moons it passes above
or below the Earth's shadow.

The Moon's orbit crosses the Earth's at two points, the **nodes**. An eclipse can happen only when a new or full Moon
falls near a node. That lines up about twice a year, in periods called **eclipse seasons**, each lasting about 35 days
and coming about 173 days apart. Each season brings two or three eclipses, at least one of them solar. So every year
has at least two and at most five solar eclipses, no more than two of them total.

## 12.3 A total eclipse of the Sun { #total }

The Sun is about 400 times wider than the Moon, and about 400 times farther away. So the two look almost exactly the
same size in our sky, and the Moon can just cover the Sun. This is pure coincidence, and it makes total eclipses of
the Sun some of the most spectacular sights in nature.

As the last sliver of Sun disappears, sunlight shines through valleys between the mountains on the Moon's edge,
making a string of bright points, **Baily's beads**, named after Francis Baily, who explained them in 1836. When only
one is left it shines like the jewel of a **diamond ring**. Then comes **totality**. The sky goes dark, and the
Sun's faint outer atmosphere, the **corona**, appears as faint streamers round the black Moon. Red flames, the
**prominences**, may show at the edge.

Totality does not last long. The Moon's umbra is usually 100 to 160 km wide when it reaches the ground, and races
across the Earth from west to east. At the very best, close to the equator with the Moon near its closest, the path can
be up to 267 km wide and totality can last over 7 minutes. The longest eclipse yet calculated, on 16 July 2186, will
reach 7 minutes 29 seconds over northern Guyana. Somewhere on Earth a total eclipse happens about every 18 months, but
any one place sees one only about once every 360 to 410 years, so most people have to travel to see one.

**Annular eclipses.** The Moon's distance changes, so its size in the sky changes too. On average it looks about 2.1%
smaller than the Sun, so when it is far away it cannot cover the Sun completely. A thin ring of Sun is left all round
it: an **annular eclipse**, from the Latin for "ring". About 60% of central eclipses are annular.

**Partial eclipses** are seen from the much wider penumbra, more than 6,400 km across. The Moon takes a bite out of the
Sun, but the sky does not go dark and the corona cannot be seen.

## 12.4 Watching safely { #safety }

**Never look at the Sun without proper protection**, not even when most of it is covered. Looking straight at the Sun
can damage your eyes for good, and you need protection at every stage of a solar eclipse except totality.

- Ordinary **sunglasses are not safe**, however dark.
- Use **eclipse glasses** or solar viewers made to the international standard **ISO 12312-2**. The American Astronomical
  Society lists these as safe for looking straight at the Sun, as long as they are not scratched or torn.
- Or look **indirectly**. Make a pinhole in a card and let sunlight through it onto a second card or the ground: you
  will see a small image of the Sun, with the Moon's bite out of it. Never look at the Sun through the pinhole. The
  holes of a kitchen colander do the same, and during the partial phases they throw little crescents on the ground.
- Never look at the Sun through binoculars, a telescope or a camera unless a proper solar filter is fixed over the
  front. Without one you will be badly hurt at once, and eclipse glasses do not help: the focused light burns
  straight through them.

There is one exception. **During totality**, and only then, the Sun's bright face is completely hidden, and it is safe
to look with the naked eye at the corona. As soon as the first bead of sunlight reappears, the glasses must go back on.

??? note "Sources"
    - Permanent eye damage from looking at the Sun, special eye protection or indirect viewing needed outside the
      total phase, and the total phase safe to view without protection: Wikipedia,
      [Solar eclipse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_eclipse) (weaker).
    - Standard sunglasses unable to filter out the damaging radiation, ISO 12312-2 products safe according to the
      American Astronomical Society, and usable as long as not scratched or torn: Wikipedia,
      [Solar viewer](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_viewer) (weaker).
    - The pinhole projector made from a hole punched in a card, not looking through the pinhole, the colander's

## 12.5 The red Moon { #lunar }

A lunar eclipse is much easier to see than a solar one. It can be watched from **anywhere on the night side of the
Earth**, it often lasts an hour or more, and it is completely **safe** to look at.

- In a **total lunar eclipse** the whole Moon passes into the Earth's umbra. Totality can last up to nearly **107
  minutes**, and the whole eclipse, from first touch to last, up to 236 minutes.
- In a **partial** eclipse only part of the Moon enters the umbra.
- In a **penumbral** eclipse the Moon passes only through the penumbra and dims so slightly that many people do not
  notice. About a third of lunar eclipses are penumbral.

**Why red?** Even in the umbra the Moon does not vanish. Some sunlight passes through the Earth's atmosphere, round the
edge of the planet, and is bent into the shadow. On the way the air scatters out the blue light, as it does at sunset,
so the light that reaches the Moon is orange and red. That is why a lunar eclipse is sometimes called a **blood moon**.
How dark and how red it is depends on the dust and clouds in the Earth's air: the dustier the air, the deeper the
red. Astronomers rate it on the five-point
**Danjon scale**, proposed by André-Louis Danjon in 1921.

**Proof that the Earth is round.** Aristotle noticed that the edge of the Earth's shadow on the Moon is always curved,
and argued that the Earth must be a sphere (history book,
[chapter 7, section 7.2](../history/07-Greece-and-Alexandria.md#round)). In India **Aryabhata** explained eclipses by
shadows, not by the demons Rahu and Ketu swallowing the Sun and Moon (history book,
[chapter 5, section 5.5](../history/05-India.md#aryabhata)).

## 12.6 Eclipses in history and science { #history }

Eclipses frightened, puzzled and taught people everywhere, and many of their stories are told in the history book.

- **Babylon** found that eclipses repeat after 223 months, the **saros**, and used it to warn of coming lunar eclipses
  (history book, [chapter 2, section 2.7](../history/02-Mesopotamia.md#saros)).
- A Chinese classic tells of two court astronomers, **Xi and He**, punished for failing to predict an eclipse; it is a
  late text, not a reliable record (history book, [chapter 4, section 4.3](../history/04-China.md#oracle)).
- The Greek historian Herodotus says **Thales** predicted the eclipse of 28 May 585 BC, which stopped a battle;
  modern scholars doubt it (history book, [chapter 7, section 7.1](../history/07-Greece-and-Alexandria.md#ionians)).
- The first recorded description of the **corona** comes from Constantinople in AD 968. The corona was recognised as
  part of the Sun's atmosphere in 1842, and the first photograph of a total eclipse was taken in 1851.
- At the eclipse of 18 August 1868, watched from India and Siam, astronomers studying the prominences found the light
  of an unknown element, later named **helium** after the Greek word for the Sun (history book,
  [chapter 18, section 18.5](../history/18-Light-Split-into-Colours.md#helium)).
- At the eclipse of **29 May 1919**, British expeditions to the island of Príncipe off West Africa and to Sobral in
  Brazil photographed stars near the darkened Sun. Their positions were shifted, as Albert Einstein's theory of
  gravity predicted, because the Sun's gravity bends starlight. Arthur Eddington presented the results in London, and
  newspapers made Einstein famous around the world.

??? note "Sources"
    - The saros, Xi and He, Thales, and helium in 1868: this site's history book, chapters 2, 4, 7 and 18, and their
      sources.
    - The corona first recorded in Constantinople in 968, identified as part of the Sun's atmosphere in 1842, and the
      first photograph of a total eclipse on 28 July 1851: Wikipedia,
      [Solar eclipse](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solar_eclipse) (weaker).
    - The 1919 expeditions to Príncipe and Sobral organised by Dyson and Eddington, the eclipse of 29 May 1919, the
      deflection predicted by Einstein, the results presented in London, and Einstein's worldwide fame: Wikipedia,
      [Eddington experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eddington_experiment) (weaker).

## 12.8 Eclipses on Mars { #mars }

There will never be a total eclipse of the Sun on Mars. Its moons are far too small.

**Phobos** looks at most 0.20° wide from Mars, while the Sun looks about 0.35° wide, so Phobos can cover only a part
of it. When it crosses the Sun it makes a quick, shallow annular eclipse that lasts only about **30 seconds**, because
Phobos races round Mars in under 8 hours. The rover **Opportunity** photographed Phobos crossing the Sun in March
2004, and the rover **Perseverance** caught it again in images released in 2024. Phobos's shadow sweeps over the
ground at about **2 km/s**: in one view from orbit it took about 20 seconds to pass over a lander.

**Deimos** is only about a tenth of the Sun's width, so when it crosses the Sun it looks like a small dark spot moving
across its face. Opportunity and Spirit photographed Deimos crossings in March 2004. In 2020 the lander **InSight**
measured one through the power from its solar panels: the sunlight dropped by about 0.9%, for about two minutes.

**The Earth crossing the Sun.** From Mars, the Earth sometimes passes directly across the Sun, as Venus and Mercury
sometimes do for us. The last such transit of Earth was on 11 May 1984, and the next will be on **10 November 2084**. No
one has ever seen one. Arthur C. Clarke wrote a story, "Transit of Earth", about an astronaut on Mars watching the one
of 1984.

## 12.9 Working it out { #working }

**Sizes in the sky.** An object of diameter $D$ at distance $d$ looks $D/d$ radians wide (times 57.3 for degrees).

- The Sun looks between 1,887″ and 1,952″ wide (0.524° to 0.542°) as the Earth's distance changes.
- The Moon is 3,474 km across, between 363,300 km and 405,507 km away: $3474/363{,}300 \times 57.3 = 0.548^\circ$ at its
  closest and $3474/405{,}507 \times 57.3 = 0.491^\circ$ at its farthest.

So the Moon can look a little bigger than the Sun (total eclipse) or a little smaller (annular eclipse).

**Eclipse seasons.** The nodes slide westward 19.35° a year (chapter 11, section 11.9), so the Sun meets the same node
again after less than a year:

$$365.25 \times \frac{360}{360 + 19.35} = 346.6 \text{ days},$$

the **eclipse year**. The Sun passes the two nodes half of that apart: $346.6 / 2 = 173.3$ days between eclipse seasons.
So eclipse seasons come about 19 days earlier each year.

**Mars.** The Sun is 1.524 times farther from Mars, so it looks $0.533^\circ / 1.524 = 0.35^\circ$ wide. Phobos, at most
0.20°, covers at most $0.20 / 0.35 = 0.57$ of its width. Deimos, at 2.5′ $= 0.042^\circ$, covers
$0.042 / 0.35 = 0.12$, about a tenth.

| | Mars | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Width of the Sun in the sky | about 0.35° | 0.524° to 0.542° |
| Width of the largest moon | Phobos 0.14° to 0.20° | Moon 0.491° to 0.548° |
| Total solar eclipses | never | about every 18 months somewhere |
| Longest "eclipse" of the Sun | Phobos about 30 s; Deimos about 2 min | totality up to about 7½ min |
| Speed of the moon's shadow on the ground | Phobos about 2 km/s | at least about 0.47 km/s |

## 12.10 Racing the shadow { #race }

Why is totality never more than about 7½ minutes? Because the Moon's shadow moves so fast.

The Moon goes round the Earth at about **61 km a minute**, about 1 km/s, from west to east. Its shadow goes a little
slower, because the Earth is also going round the Sun, so the line from the Sun to the Earth slowly turns the same
way, once a year. At the Moon's distance that turning is worth

$$\frac{2\pi \times 384{,}400 \text{ km}}{525{,}960 \text{ minutes in a year}} \approx 4.6 \text{ km a minute},$$

so the shadow moves at about $61 - 4.6 \approx 56$ km a minute. But the Earth is turning in the same direction too: at
the equator its surface moves at about **28 km a minute**. So the shadow overtakes the ground at the difference, and
near the equator around noon that is as slow as it gets:

$$56 - 28 = 28 \text{ km a minute} \approx 1{,}700 \text{ km/h}.$$

That matches the measured figure: the umbra always moves at more than 1,700 km/h. Away from the equator the ground
moves more slowly, and the shadow often strikes the curved Earth at a slant, which makes it sweep faster still.

Now the length of totality: it is roughly the width of the shadow divided by its speed over the ground. For the widest
possible shadow, 267 km, at the slowest speed:

$$\frac{267}{28} \approx 9\tfrac{1}{2} \text{ minutes}.$$

The real limit is lower, about 7½ minutes, because the widest shadows come when the Moon is at its closest, and that
is when it moves fastest (chapter 8, section 8.9), and because the shadow's track never runs exactly along the
equator. For a typical shadow 120 km wide moving at 50 km a minute, totality lasts only about $120 / 50 = 2.4$
minutes.

That is why the longest eclipses happen near the equator, around midday, with the Moon at its closest (bigger shadow)
and the Earth at its farthest from the Sun (smaller Sun). The eclipse of 2 August 2027 comes a month after the Earth is
farthest from the Sun, and will last 6 minutes 22 seconds near Luxor. The last eclipse longer than 7 minutes was on
30 June 1973, which lasted 7 minutes 3 seconds; the longest ever calculated, on 16 July 2186, will last 7 minutes 29
seconds.

On Mars the race is far more lopsided. Phobos goes round Mars faster than Mars turns (chapter 6, section 6.8), and its
shadow crosses the ground at about 2 km/s, more than 7,000 km/h, so its "eclipses" are over in seconds.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) At what phase of the Moon can a solar eclipse happen, and at what phase a lunar eclipse?

??? note "Answer"
    A solar eclipse at new Moon; a lunar eclipse at full Moon.

**2.** (beginner) Why is there not an eclipse every month?

??? note "Answer"
    The Moon's orbit is tilted about 5° to the Earth's, so at most new and full Moons the Moon passes above or below
    the line from the Sun to the Earth. Eclipses happen only when a new or full Moon falls near a node, in the eclipse
    seasons about twice a year.

**3.** (beginner) When, if ever, is it safe to look at a solar eclipse without eclipse glasses?

??? note "Answer"
    Only during totality, when the Sun's face is completely covered. At all other times you need ISO 12312-2 glasses
    or an indirect method such as a pinhole.

**4.** (intermediate) What is the difference between a total and an annular eclipse of the Sun?

??? note "Answer"
    In a total eclipse the Moon looks bigger than the Sun and covers it completely. In an annular eclipse the Moon is
    farther away, looks smaller than the Sun, and leaves a ring of sunlight round it.

**5.** (intermediate) Why does the Moon turn red in a total lunar eclipse?

??? note "Answer"
    Sunlight passing through the Earth's atmosphere is bent into the shadow, and the air scatters out the blue light,
    leaving red and orange, as at sunset.

**6.** (intermediate) What did the eclipse of 29 May 1919 show?

??? note "Answer"
    Stars near the eclipsed Sun appeared shifted from their usual positions, showing that the Sun's gravity bends
    starlight, as Einstein's theory predicted.

**7.** (advanced) Why can there never be a total eclipse of the Sun on Mars?

??? note "Answer"
    The Sun looks about 0.35° wide from Mars, but Phobos, the bigger-looking moon, is at most 0.20° wide, so it can
    never cover the whole Sun.

**8.** (expert) Explain why the longest total eclipses happen near the equator around midday.

??? note "Answer"
    The Moon's shadow moves east at about 56 km a minute, and the Earth's surface moves the same way at up to 28 km a
    minute at the equator. Near the equator at midday the ground moves fastest in the shadow's direction and the shadow
    falls nearly straight down, so it crosses the ground slowest and totality lasts longest.

## Summary

- A solar eclipse is the Moon's shadow on the Earth, at new Moon; a lunar eclipse is the Earth's shadow on the Moon, at
  full Moon.
- The Moon's tilted orbit means eclipses happen only near the nodes, in two eclipse seasons a year.
- The Sun is 400 times wider and 400 times farther than the Moon; when the Moon is close it covers the Sun (total),
  when far it leaves a ring (annular).
- Around totality you can see Baily's beads, the diamond ring, the corona and prominences; totality lasts at most
  about 7½ minutes.
- Only ISO 12312-2 glasses or indirect viewing are safe; only totality can be watched with the naked eye.
- A total lunar eclipse turns the Moon red with light bent through the Earth's air, and is safe to watch.
- Eclipses gave us the saros, helium and a test of Einstein's gravity.
- Coming total eclipses: Spain and North Africa in 2027, Australia in 2028, western Canada in 2044.
- On Mars, Phobos and Deimos only ever take a small, quick bite out of the Sun.
