# 11. When the Moon rises

*The Sun rises at nearly the same time from one day to the next. The Moon does not: it comes up about 50 minutes later
each day, on average. But the average hides a lot. Around the autumn equinox the full Moon rises only a little later
each evening, and farmers once worked by its light; in spring it is more than an hour later each night. This chapter
explains why, and follows the Moon's rising point as it swings along the horizon, further in some years than in
others, as the builders of Chimney Rock in Colorado seem to have known. It looks at why the rising Moon looks so big,
how the Moon drives the tides, and how moonrise works on Mars, where one moon rises in the west. It ends with the
18.6-year cycle behind it all.*

## 11.1 Fifty minutes later { #later }

The Moon goes round the Earth in the same direction as the Earth spins. Each day it moves about 13° east against the
stars (chapter 10, section 10.9), so after the Earth has turned once, it must turn about 13° more before the Moon is
back in the same place in the sky. That takes about 50 minutes. So, on average:

- the Moon rises about **50 minutes later** each day;
- the time from one moonrise to the next, the **lunar day**, is about **24 hours 50 minutes**.

Watch for a week and the difference adds up: a Moon that rose at 7 pm on Monday rises after midnight by the next
Monday. That is why the full Moon is up all evening, but a week later the evening sky is dark, and the last-quarter
Moon only comes up around midnight.

The 50 minutes is only an average, though. Depending on the time of year and where the Moon is in its orbit, the gap
between one moonrise and the next can be much shorter or much longer.

    - The Moon orbiting in the same direction as the Earth spins, and taking about 24 hours 50 minutes to return to the

## 11.2 The harvest Moon { #harvest }

The **harvest Moon** is the full Moon nearest the September equinox, which can fall up to two weeks before or after
it. The full Moon after it is the **hunter's Moon**. The names are recorded in English from the early 1700s.

What makes the harvest Moon special is not its size or colour, but its timing. From mid-northern latitudes, for about
a week around the full Moon, the Moon rises close to sunset night after night, only a little later each evening.
Before electric light, farmers could keep bringing in the harvest by moonlight after the Sun had set.

Why does it happen? The Moon travels close to the ecliptic, the Sun's path (chapter 8). Each night it is about 12°
further along that path, compared with the Sun. How much later that makes it rise depends on how steeply the path
meets the eastern horizon.

- **In autumn**, at 45°N, the full Moon's path meets the horizon at only about 22°. Moving 12° along it, the Moon drops

- **In spring**, the path meets the horizon at about 68°, so the Moon drops about 11° and rises roughly **an hour**
  later each night.

In the southern hemisphere it is the other way round: the harvest Moon effect comes around the March equinox, which is
autumn there.

## 11.3 High Moon, low Moon { #high }

The full Moon is opposite the Sun in the sky. So it does the opposite of what the Sun does:

- In **winter**, when the noon Sun is low, the full Moon rides **high** at midnight and stays up for many hours. At 45°N
  around the December solstice it climbs to nearly 70°, as high as the Sun in June.
- In **summer**, when the Sun is high, the full Moon skims **low** across the south, at about 20° at 45°N, and is up for
  only a short night.

The Moon's orbit is tilted about 5° to the ecliptic, so these heights can be a few degrees more or less.

The other phases follow the same rule. The first-quarter Moon is highest in the evening sky around the March equinox
and lowest around the September equinox; the last-quarter Moon is the other way round.

**At the poles** this gives a strange sight. Through the long polar night, when the Sun stays down for months, the Moon
still comes up for almost two weeks every month and circles the sky without setting, then disappears for two weeks.

??? note "Sources"
    - The full Moon highest at the winter solstice and lowest at the summer solstice, and the first-quarter and
      last-quarter Moons highest and lowest at the equinoxes: Wikipedia,
      [Lunar phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_phase) (weaker).
    - The Moon above the horizon at the poles for almost two weeks every month, even while the Sun is down for six
      months: Wikipedia, [Orbit of the Moon](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Orbit_of_the_Moon) (weaker).

## 11.4 Where the Moon rises { #where }

The Sun's rising point swings along the horizon over a year (chapter 8, section 8.4). The Moon's does the same, but in
a month: in two weeks it goes from rising in the north-east to rising in the south-east, and in two more weeks it is
back.

How far it swings changes from year to year. The Moon's orbit is tilted about 5° to the Sun's path, and that tilt
sometimes adds to the Earth's 23.4° tilt and sometimes takes away from it. The cycle takes **18.6 years**.

- In a year of **major lunar standstill**, the Moon reaches about 28.7° north and south of the celestial equator. It
  then rises and sets further north and further south than the Sun ever does. The most recent was in December 2024;
  the one before was in 2006.
- In a year of **minor lunar standstill**, the Moon reaches only about 18.1° north and south, and its rising point
  swings less than the Sun's. The last was in October 2015, and the next is due in 2034.

Ancient builders seem to have watched this long cycle. At **Chimney Rock** in Colorado, a community of Ancestral
Puebloans lived on a high ridge about 1,000 years ago, in the time of the great centre at Chaco Canyon (history book,
[chapter 11, section 11.7](../history/11-The-Americas.md#north)). Seen from the great house at the top of the ridge,
the Moon rises between two rock pillars, Chimney Rock and Companion Rock, for about 16 months around each major
standstill. The evidence suggests that the great house was first built in **1076**, during a standstill, and
expanded and finished in **1093**, during the next one. Alignments to moonrise or moonset at the standstills have also
been proposed at Chaco Canyon and at Hopewell sites in Ohio, and the stone circle of Callanish in Scotland has been
suggested as a lunar observatory.

## 11.5 The giant rising Moon { #illusion }

A full Moon rising behind trees or buildings can look enormous, far bigger than the same Moon high in the sky a few
hours later. It is not. Photographs taken at both times show the Moon the same size, and the horizon Moon is in fact
about **1.5% farther away**, so very slightly smaller.

Try it yourself. Close one eye and hold a small pebble, about 8 mm wide, at arm's length, so that it covers the full
Moon high in the sky. Then hold the same pebble the same way when the Moon is rising. It covers it just the same.

This is the **Moon illusion**, and it is one of the oldest puzzles in science. Aristotle wrote about it around 330 BC,
and Ptolemy tried to explain it, first by the bending of light in the air, later as a trick of seeing. In his *Book of
Optics*, written in Cairo between 1011 and 1022, **Ibn al-Haytham** argued that we judge distance from the things we
see between us and an object. Near the horizon, with land and buildings in between, the Moon seems far away, and so the
brain sees it as big. By the 17th century the effect was accepted as a matter of the mind, not of the light. Even now
there is no explanation that everyone agrees on.

The rising Moon also often looks yellow, orange or red. That is real: its light passes through more air near the
horizon, which takes out the blue, just as at sunset.

## 11.6 The Moon and the tides { #tides }

If you live by the sea, you already have a Moon clock. On many coasts the tide comes in and goes out twice a day, with
high tides about **12 hours 25 minutes** apart: exactly half a lunar day. So high tide, like moonrise, comes about 50
minutes later each day. The tides follow the Moon because it is the Moon's pull, more than the Sun's, that raises
them: the Sun's tidal pull is only 46% of the Moon's.

The phase matters too.

- At **new and full Moon**, the Sun, Moon and Earth are in a line, and the Sun's pull adds to the Moon's. The tides
  are at their biggest, with the highest highs and lowest lows: **spring tides**. The name has nothing to do with the
  season; it comes from an old meaning of "spring", to jump or rise up.
- At **first and last quarter**, the Sun and Moon are 90° apart, and the Sun's pull partly cancels the Moon's. The
  tides are at their smallest: **neap tides**.

The biggest tides in the world are in the **Bay of Fundy**, between New Brunswick and Nova Scotia in Canada. The water
there rises and falls by about **16 metres**, the height of a five-storey building, against a world average of about
1 metre. The funnel shape of the bay makes the water slosh back and forth in step with the tide, building it up.

??? note "Sources"
    - Tides rising and falling about twice a day, the period of about 12 hours 25.2 minutes as half a tidal lunar day,
      the lunar day of about 24 hours 50 minutes, spring tides at new and full Moon and the origin of the name, and

    - The Bay of Fundy between New Brunswick and Nova Scotia, its tidal range of about 16 metres, the highest in the

## 11.7 Moonrise on Mars { #mars }

Chapter 6, section 6.8, showed that Mars's two moons go opposite ways across its sky. Their rising times are just as
odd.

**Phobos** rises in the **west** and sets in the east. It goes round Mars so fast that it overtakes the turning of the
sky, rising again every **11 hours 6 minutes**, about twice a sol, and crossing the sky in 4 hours 15 minutes or less.
And it orbits so low, right over Mars's equator, that it cannot be seen at all from latitudes beyond **70.4°**: there
it is always below the horizon. A Martian standing near the poles would never see it.

**Deimos** rises in the east like our Moon, but it only just lags behind the turning sky. At the equator, **2.48 days**
pass between its rising and its setting, and then it stays down for about three days more before rising again.

So a Mars almanac would need very different moonrise tables: one moon rising twice a day in the west, the other
roughly once every five and a half days in the east.

??? note "Sources"
    - Phobos rising in the west and setting in the east about twice each Martian day, every 11 h 6 min, crossing the
      sky in 4 h 15 min or less, and invisible from latitudes above 70.4° because of its low, equatorial orbit:
      Wikipedia, [Phobos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Phobos_(moon)) (weaker).
    - Deimos rising in the east and setting in the west, and 2.48 days (2.41 sols) between rising and setting for an
      observer at the equator: Wikipedia, [Deimos](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Deimos_(moon)) (weaker).
    - The time between Deimos's risings, and so the three days it is down, is worked out in section 11.8.

## 11.8 Working it out { #working }

**The lunar day.** In a synodic month of 29.53 days the Earth turns 29.53 times against the Sun but only 28.53 times
against the Moon, so

$$\text{lunar day} = 24 \text{ h} \times \frac{29.53}{28.53} = 24.84 \text{ h} \approx 24 \text{ h } 50 \text{ min}.$$

**The harvest Moon.** Near the equinoxes the full Moon is close to one of the points where the ecliptic crosses the
celestial equator. As that point rises, the ecliptic meets the horizon at an angle of
$90^\circ - \varphi \mp 23.44^\circ$: the minus sign for the September full Moon, the plus sign for the March one. At
$\varphi = 45^\circ$ that is $21.6^\circ$ and $68.4^\circ$.

A night later the Moon is $12.2^\circ$ further along its path, so it is $12.2^\circ \times \sin(\text{angle})$ below the
horizon at the same time: $4.5^\circ$ in September, $11.3^\circ$ in March. A point on the celestial equator climbs away
from the eastern horizon at $15^\circ \cos\varphi$ an hour, $10.6^\circ$ an hour at 45°N. So the delay is

$$\frac{4.5}{10.6} \text{ h} \approx 25 \text{ min}, \qquad \frac{11.3}{10.6} \text{ h} \approx 64 \text{ min}.$$

This simple model leaves out the Moon's 5° tilt and its changing speed, so real delays differ by several minutes. In
2007, close to a major standstill, the harvest Moon rose about 30 minutes later each night seen from about 40°N.

**High and low.** The full Moon is about opposite the Sun, so its declination is about minus the Sun's. Its height at
midnight is $90^\circ - \varphi + \delta_{\text{Moon}}$: at 45°N, $90 - 45 + 23.4 = 68.4^\circ$ in December and
$90 - 45 - 23.4 = 21.6^\circ$ in June, give or take 5°.

**Rising points.** As in chapter 8, the rising direction measured from north obeys $\cos A = \sin\delta / \cos\varphi$.
At 45°N:

| | Greatest declination | Rises up to this far from due east |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Moon, minor standstill | 18.1° | 26° |
| Sun | 23.4° | 34° |
| Moon, major standstill | 28.7° | 43° |

**The Moon illusion.** When the Moon is overhead you are one Earth radius, 6,371 km, nearer to it than the Earth's
centre is; at the horizon you are not. The Moon is about 384,400 km away, so the difference is up to
$6{,}371 / 384{,}400 = 1.7\%$, in line with the 1.5% quoted for a Moon high in the sky.

**Moonrise on Mars.** A moon rises again when the sky's turning has gained one full turn on it, or it on the sky. Mars
turns against the stars in 24.62 hours. For Phobos, orbiting in 7.65 hours:

$$\frac{1}{7.65} - \frac{1}{24.62} = 0.0901 \text{ per hour} \quad\Rightarrow\quad 11.1 \text{ hours}.$$

For Deimos, orbiting in 30.3 hours: $\frac{1}{24.62} - \frac{1}{30.3} = 0.00761$ per hour, so 131 hours, about 5.5 days
between risings; 2.48 days up, about 3 days down.

| | Mars | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Time from one moonrise to the next | Phobos 11 h 6 min; Deimos about 5.5 days | about 24 h 50 min |
| Rises in the | Phobos west; Deimos east | east |
| Time above the horizon, at the equator | Phobos up to 4 h 15 min; Deimos 2.48 days | about 12 h 25 min on average |
| Never seen from | Phobos: beyond 70.4° latitude | everywhere sees it |

## 11.9 The 18.6-year cycle { #nodes }

The Moon's orbit is tilted about 5.1° to the ecliptic. It crosses the ecliptic at two points, the **nodes**: the
ascending node, where the Moon passes from south to north, and the descending node. The line joining them does not
stay put. The Moon's orbit slowly swivels, and the nodes slide westward round the ecliptic, about 19.35° a year,
going all the way round in **18.6 years**.

This one motion explains section 11.4.

- When the **ascending node** is near the March equinox point, the Moon's tilt adds to the Earth's. Its path climbs
  furthest north of the celestial equator where the Sun's path does, at the June solstice point, and goes about
  $23.4^\circ + 5.1^\circ \approx 28.5^\circ$ north and the same south: a **major standstill**.
- About 9.3 years later the node is near the September equinox point, the two tilts work against each other, and the
  Moon's path reaches only about $23.4^\circ - 5.1^\circ \approx 18.3^\circ$: a **minor standstill**.

The measured limits are 28.7° and 18.1°, a little different from these round numbers because the tilt of the Moon's
orbit itself wobbles slightly: it grows by about 0.135° whenever the Sun lines up with the nodes.

The cycle also tunes the harvest Moon. Near a major standstill the Moon's path around the March equinox point lies
even closer to the autumn evening horizon than the ecliptic does, so the harvest Moon rises with even less delay; near
a minor standstill the effect is weaker. And because eclipses can only happen when the Sun is near a node (chapter 12),
the slow sliding of the nodes also sets the timing of the eclipse seasons from year to year.

The same cycle shows up in the Earth itself. Over 18.6 years the Earth's axis nods slightly, by about 9 seconds of
arc, because of the Moon's motion. This is called **nutation**.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) On average, how much later does the Moon rise each day, and why?

??? note "Answer"
    About 50 minutes. The Moon moves about 13° east each day, so the Earth must turn about 13° more than once to bring
    it back to the same place.

**2.** (beginner) What is the harvest Moon?

??? note "Answer"
    The full Moon nearest the September equinox. For a few nights around it, from mid-northern latitudes, the Moon rises
    close to sunset each evening.

**3.** (beginner) Is the full Moon higher at midnight in December or in June, seen from Canada?

??? note "Answer"
    In December. The full Moon is opposite the Sun, so it rides high when the Sun is low.

**4.** (intermediate) Why does the rising Moon look so big, and how can you show that it is not?

??? note "Answer"
    It is the Moon illusion, a trick of the brain, probably to do with how we judge distance near the horizon. A small
    pebble held at arm's length covers it just the same when it is high and when it is rising.

**5.** (intermediate) Why are tides biggest around new and full Moon?

??? note "Answer"
    Then the Sun, Moon and Earth are in a line, and the Sun's pull adds to the Moon's: spring tides.

**6.** (intermediate) Why can nobody see Phobos from near the poles of Mars?

??? note "Answer"
    Phobos orbits very low, right above Mars's equator, so from latitudes beyond 70.4° it is always below the horizon.

**7.** (advanced) At 40°N, at what angle does the Moon's path meet the eastern horizon at moonrise near the September
equinox, and about how much later does the Moon rise the next night?

??? note "Answer"
    $90 - 40 - 23.4 = 26.6^\circ$. The Moon drops $12.2 \times \sin 26.6^\circ = 5.5^\circ$, and climbs at
    $15 \cos 40^\circ = 11.5^\circ$ an hour, so it rises about $5.5 / 11.5 = 0.48$ hours, roughly half an hour, later.

**8.** (expert) Why does the Moon reach 28.7° from the celestial equator in some years and only 18.1° in others?

??? note "Answer"
    The Moon's orbit is tilted about 5° to the ecliptic, and its nodes slide round the ecliptic every 18.6 years. When
    the ascending node is at the March equinox point, the Moon's tilt adds to the Earth's 23.4° and the Moon goes
    about 28.7° north and south; 9.3 years later the tilts partly cancel and it goes only about 18.1°.

## Summary

- The Moon rises about 50 minutes later each day on average; the lunar day is about 24 hours 50 minutes.
- The harvest Moon, nearest the September equinox, rises only about half an hour later each night at mid-northern
  latitudes, because its path then lies close to the horizon; in spring the delay is about an hour.
- The full Moon rides high in winter and low in summer; at the poles the Moon is up for two weeks each month.
- The Moon's rising point swings along the horizon each month, further at a major standstill (2006, 2024) than at a
  minor one (2015, 2034). Chimney Rock seems to have been built to mark it.
- The giant rising Moon is an illusion; the horizon Moon is in fact slightly farther away.
- High tides come about 50 minutes later each day; spring tides come at new and full Moon. The Bay of Fundy has the
  biggest tides on Earth.
- Phobos rises in the west every 11 hours and cannot be seen beyond 70.4° latitude; Deimos rises about every 5½ days.
- The Moon's nodes slide round the ecliptic every 18.6 years, setting the standstills and nudging the Earth's axis.
