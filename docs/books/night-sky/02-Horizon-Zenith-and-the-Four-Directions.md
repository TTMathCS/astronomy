# 2. Horizon, zenith and the four directions

*Before you can find a star, you need a way to say where it is. This chapter gives you the words and the tools: the
horizon, the point straight overhead, the four directions, and a ruler you always carry with you, your hand. It shows
how to find north from the pole star and south from the Southern Cross, and how peoples around the world have named
and used the directions. It ends on Mars, where a compass is no use at all.*

## 2.1 The dome of the sky { #dome }

Stand in an open field at night and the sky looks like a great dome resting on the ground all around you. The stars
seem to be stuck on the inside of it, all at the same distance. They are not, of course: some are a few light-years
away and some thousands. But the dome is a useful picture, and astronomers still use it to say where things are.

- The **horizon** is where the sky seems to meet the ground or the sea, all the way round you.
- The **zenith** is the point straight over your head.
- The **nadir** is the point straight under your feet, through the Earth. You cannot see it, but it helps to name it.

Both words come from Arabic. *Zenith* comes from *samt al-raʾs*, "the direction of the head". Medieval Latin scribes
shortened it to *samt* and miscopied it, reading the *m* as *ni*. *Nadir* comes from *nazir*, "opposite".

## 2.2 North, south, east and west { #directions }

The four main directions are the **cardinal directions**: north, east, south and west. Halfway between them lie
northeast, southeast, southwest and northwest. Navigators split the circle further, into 16 and then 32 named points.

Astronomers and navigators give a direction as an angle round the horizon, called the **azimuth**, measured from north
towards east:

| Direction | Azimuth |
| --- | --- |
| North | 0° |
| East | 90° |
| South | 180° |
| West | 270° |

So northeast is 45° and southwest is 225°. Chapter 17 adds the second number you need to pin down a star: its height
above the horizon.

??? note "Sources"
    - The four cardinal and four intercardinal directions, 16 and 32 points, and their angles: Wikipedia,
      [Cardinal direction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardinal_direction) (weaker).
    - Azimuth measured from true north, increasing eastward: Wikipedia,
      [Horizontal coordinate system](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Horizontal_coordinate_system) (weaker).

## 2.3 The meridian { #meridian }

Imagine a line drawn on the sky from the north point of your horizon, up through the zenith, and down to the south
point. This line is your **meridian**.

The Earth turns, so the stars drift across the sky through the night. Each one crosses your meridian once on the way
up and over, and at that moment it is at its **highest** in your sky. Astronomers call this **culmination**. The Sun
does the same: it is highest at midday, when it crosses your meridian. Chapter 6 follows the turning sky in detail.

## 2.4 Finding north: the pole star { #north }

In the northern hemisphere there is a star that hardly moves. As the sky turns, all the stars of the north seem to
circle round it. This is **Polaris**, the **pole star** or North Star. It lies less than 1° from the point in the sky
straight above the Earth's North Pole, the **north celestial pole**. So if you face Polaris, you are facing north.

**How to find it.** Find the **Big Dipper**, seven bright stars shaped like a ladle with a long handle. Draw a line
through the two stars at the front of the bowl, **Merak** and **Dubhe**, and carry it on beyond Dubhe. It leads to
Polaris. That is why Merak and Dubhe are called **the Pointers**.

**It is not the brightest star.** Many people think it is, but at magnitude about 1.98 it is only the 47th brightest.
It is famous for where it is, not for how bright it is. And it will not stay the pole star for ever: it will come
closest to the pole, about 0.45°, soon after the year 2100, and then slowly move away (chapter 6 explains why).

**One pattern, many names.** The seven stars of the Big Dipper are known all over the northern world:

- In Britain and Ireland they are **the Plough**.
- In China they are **Beidou**, the Northern Dipper, and in Japan, Korea and Vietnam the Seven Stars of the Northern
  Dipper.
- In India the Rigveda called them "the Bear"; later they became the **Saptarishi**, the Seven Sages.
- In Arabic they are **Banat Naʿsh**, "the daughters of the bier": the four stars of the bowl are a funeral bier, and
  the three of the handle are the mourners walking behind.
- The Sámi of northern Europe see the Big Dipper as the bow of the hunter Fávdna. He never shoots, for fear of
  hitting Polaris, **Boahji**, "the Rivet", which would bring the sky crashing down.

## 2.5 Finding south: the Southern Cross { #south }

The southern hemisphere has a pole star too, **Sigma Octantis**, but at magnitude 5.5 it is too faint to see except
from a dark site, and it is a little more than a degree from the pole. Instead, people use the **Southern Cross**, or
**Crux**, a small, bright cross of four stars, with two very bright stars beside it, **Alpha and Beta Centauri**,
called **the Pointers**.

**How to find south.** Draw a line along the long bar of the cross, from its top star, **Gacrux**, through its foot,
**Acrux**, and carry it on about **4½ times** the length of the cross. That brings you close to the
**south celestial pole**. Drop straight down from there to the horizon, and you are facing south.

Beware of the **False Cross**, made of stars in the constellations Carina and Vela. It is bigger and dimmer, and it
has no Pointers beside it.

**The Cross in many skies.**

- In many Aboriginal Australian cultures, the Cross and the dark Coalsack beside it make the head of the
  **Emu in the Sky**. The Boorong people of Victoria see the Cross as a possum in a tree; the Yolngu of Arnhem Land
  see a stingray; and the Kaurna of the Adelaide Plains see an eagle.
- To the **Māori** it is **Māhutonga**, the anchor of Tama-rereti's canoe, the Milky Way (chapter 1), and the Pointers
  are its rope.
- In **Tonga** it is **Toloa**, a duck flying south; in **Samoa**, **Sumu**, a triggerfish.
- In **Quechua**, the language of the Inca, it is **Chakana**, "stair" or "bridge".
- **Bugis** sailors of Indonesia steered by it, calling it the "incomplete house star".

## 2.6 Measuring the sky with your hand { #hand }

Distances on the sky are **angles**. From the horizon to the zenith is a quarter of a circle, **90°**. To measure
smaller angles you need no tools. Stretch your arm out straight and use your hand:

- your **little finger** covers about **1°**;
- your **fist** covers about **10°**;
- your **outstretched hand**, from thumb tip to little finger tip, covers about **20°**.

Hands and arms differ, so check your own against something you know. The full Moon is only about **half a degree**
across, half the width of your little finger, which surprises most people.

## 2.7 Directions around the world { #world }

North, east, south and west are not the only way to find your way, and not every people uses them in the same way.

- **China.** The sky's four quarters were guarded by four creatures, each with a colour: the **Azure Dragon** of the
  east, the **Vermilion Bird** of the south, the **White Tiger** of the west and the **Black Tortoise** of the north.
  Each stood for a group of constellations along the Sun's yearly path
  through the stars (chapter 8). China, like the Turkic, Tibetan and Ainu peoples, also
  counted the **centre** as a fifth direction.
- **India.** Classical Hindu texts name **ten** directions, each guarded by a god, the **Dikpalas**: the four cardinal
  directions, the four between them, and up and down.
- **The Arabic world.** The directions are *ash-shamal* (north), *ash-sharq* (east), *al-janub* (south) and
  *al-gharb* (west), with *al-wasat* for the centre. The Algarve, in southern Portugal, takes its name from one of them.
- **North America.** Many Indigenous peoples count four directions and a centre, and some add above and below. The
  **Hopi** of the American Southwest name their four directions after the places where the Sun rises and sets at the
  two solstices.
- **Australia.** The **Guugu Yimithirr** people use compass directions even for things close by: not "the cup on
  your left" but "the cup to your north". Some Aboriginal languages have words for five or even six directions.
- **The Pacific and beyond.** In Hawaiʻi and Bali people often say "towards the sea" or "towards the mountains"
  instead. In ancient Egypt, upstream and downstream along the Nile did the same job.

**Steering by the stars.** Polynesian navigators used a **star compass**: they learned the points on the horizon where
particular stars rise and set, and steered for them. Some systems name up to 150 stars. Near the equator, where most
of their voyaging took place, stars rise and set almost straight up and down, so each star marks its point on the
horizon well. The history book tells the story of these
[voyages](../history/12-Oceania-and-Australia.md#wayfinding).

**Facing Mecca.** Muslims pray facing the **qibla**, the direction of the Kaaba in Mecca, and astronomers of the
Islamic world worked out how to find it from anywhere, using spherical trigonometry. There is also a simple way. Twice
a year the Sun passes straight over Mecca: on 27 and 28 May at 12:18 Saudi time, and on 15 and 16 July at 12:27. At
those moments, anywhere the Sun is up, the shadow of an upright stick points directly away from the qibla.

## 2.8 The sky's own directions { #sky-directions }

Astronomers use the four directions on the sky too, but with a twist. On the sky, **north** means towards the north
celestial pole, near Polaris, and **south** towards the south celestial pole. **East** and **west** follow from those.

Here is the twist. A map of the Earth is drawn as if you look **down** on the ground, with north at the top and east
on the right. A map of the sky is drawn as if you look **up**. Lie on your back with your head to the north, and east
is on your **left**. So on a sky chart with north at the top, east is on the left and west on the right. Going round
the edge of such a chart clockwise from north, you meet west, then south, then east.

This trips up nearly everyone the first time. Chapter 18 uses it to read a star chart.

??? note "Sources"
    - North and south on the sky as towards the celestial poles, and the order north, west, south, east going clockwise
      round the sky: Wikipedia, [Cardinal direction](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Cardinal_direction) (weaker).
      Lying on your back is our way of picturing it.

## 2.9 Finding your way on Mars { #mars }

Imagine standing on Mars and trying to find north.

**A compass is useless.** A compass needle lines up with a planet's magnetic field. The Earth has a strong field all
round it. Mars once had one too, about 4 billion years ago, as strong as the Earth's is
today, but it has died away. Mars **no longer has a global magnetic field**, only patches of magnetism frozen into old
rocks. A compass there would not point north.

**Use the stars.** Mars spins, so it has its own north and south celestial poles, and its sky turns round them just as
ours does. They are in a different place among the stars, because Mars's axis points a different way. There is no
bright star right at its north pole, but the two top stars of the Northern Cross, **Sadr** and **Deneb**, point to it.
The star **Kappa Velorum** lies a couple of degrees from its south pole.

**The horizon is closer.** Mars is about half the Earth's size, so its surface curves more sharply. If your eyes are
1.7 m above flat ground, the horizon is about 4.7 km away on the Earth but only about 3.4 km away on Mars
(section 2.10 shows how to work it out).

??? note "Sources"
    - Mars no longer has a global magnetic field, the strong early field about 4 billion years ago comparable to the
      Earth's today, and the magnetism of the crust: Wikipedia,

    - Sadr and Deneb pointing to the north celestial pole of Mars, and Kappa Velorum near its south pole: Wikipedia,

## 2.10 Working it out { #working }

**How far is the horizon?** Stand at height $h$ on a round planet of radius $R$. Your line of sight just grazes the
surface at the horizon, making a right angle with the radius there. By Pythagoras, the distance $d$ to the horizon is

$$d = \sqrt{(R + h)^2 - R^2} = \sqrt{2Rh + h^2} \approx \sqrt{2Rh},$$

since $h$ is tiny next to $R$. For eyes 1.7 m up on the Earth, with $R = 6{,}371$ km $= 6{,}371{,}000$ m:

$$d = \sqrt{2 \times 6{,}371{,}000 \times 1.7} = \sqrt{21{,}661{,}400} \approx 4{,}650 \text{ m} = 4.7 \text{ km}.$$

From a tower 100 m high it is $\sqrt{2 \times 6{,}371{,}000 \times 100} \approx 35{,}700$ m, about 36 km. Going up 59
times higher only takes you about 8 times farther, because $d$ grows with the square root of $h$:
$\sqrt{100 / 1.7} = 7.7$.

**Your hand as a ruler.** An angle in **radians** is the width of a thing divided by its distance; one radian is
$180° / \pi \approx 57.3°$. A fist about 10 cm wide held 60 cm from the eye covers

$$\frac{10}{60} \times 57.3° \approx 9.5°,$$

close to the 10° rule. The Moon, 3,474 km across and on average about 384,400 km away, covers

$$\frac{3{,}474}{384{,}400} \times 57.3° \approx 0.52°.$$

**Mars and the Earth.**

| | Mars | Earth |
|---|---|---|
| Mean radius | 3,389.5 km | 6,371.0 km |
| Horizon for eyes 1.7 m up | $\sqrt{2 \times 3{,}389{,}500 \times 1.7} \approx 3.4$ km | 4.7 km |
| Horizon from 100 m up | $\sqrt{2 \times 3{,}389{,}500 \times 100} \approx 26$ km | 36 km |
| Global magnetic field | none | yes |

## 2.11 The air bends the stars near the horizon { #refraction }

Light bends as it passes through the air, because air slows light down a little, and more so where it is denser. The
air thins out with height, so starlight coming in at a slant is bent on its way down. The result is **atmospheric
refraction**: every star looks a little **higher** than it really is.

The effect is nothing overhead and grows quickly near the horizon. For air at 10 °C and normal pressure:

| Height of the star above the horizon | Lift from refraction |
| --- | --- |
| 90° (zenith) | 0 |
| 45° | less than 1′ |
| 10° | 5.3′ |
| 5° | 9.9′ |
| 2° | 18.4′ |
| 0° (horizon) | 35.4′ |

(A minute of arc, 1′, is a sixtieth of a degree.) At the horizon the lift, about 35′, is a little more than the
width of the Sun, about 32′. So when you watch the bottom of the setting Sun touch the horizon, the whole Sun is
already below it. If the air suddenly vanished, it would disappear. For this reason sunrise and sunset are defined as
the moments when the Sun's centre is truly 50′ below the horizon: 34′ for refraction and 16′ for half the Sun's width.

Near the horizon the bending also changes from night to night with the temperature of the air close to the ground.
As early as 1830 Friedrich Bessel found that, even after correcting for temperature and pressure, his measurements
still varied by about half a minute of arc just above the horizon. Navigators avoid measuring stars less than 20°
up.

**And on Mars?** Refraction comes from the air, and Mars's air at the ground is about 76 times thinner than ours
($1.217 / 0.016 \approx 76$). So on Mars refraction should be far smaller, and the setting Sun should not be lifted
nearly as much.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) What are the zenith and the nadir?

??? note "Answer"
    The zenith is the point straight overhead. The nadir is the point straight below your feet, on the other side of
    the Earth.

**2.** (beginner) How do you find Polaris from the Big Dipper?

??? note "Answer"
    Draw a line through Merak and Dubhe, the two stars at the front of the bowl, and carry it on away from the bowl.
    It leads to Polaris.

**3.** (beginner) About how many fists, held at arm's length, reach from the horizon to the zenith?

??? note "Answer"
    About nine. The horizon to the zenith is 90°, and a fist covers about 10°.

**4.** (intermediate) How do you find south using the Southern Cross?

??? note "Answer"
    Carry the line from Gacrux, the top of the cross, through Acrux, its foot, about 4½ times the length of the cross.
    That reaches close to the south celestial pole. Straight below it on the horizon is south.

**5.** (intermediate) Give two ways of naming directions that are not simply north, east, south and west.

??? note "Answer"
    For example: China's four creatures and colours with the centre as a fifth direction; India's ten Dikpalas,
    including up and down; the Hopi directions of the solstice sunrises and sunsets; "towards the sea" and "towards
    the mountains" in Hawaiʻi and Bali; upstream and downstream in ancient Egypt.

**6.** (intermediate) Why would a compass not help you find north on Mars, and what could you use instead?

??? note "Answer"
    Mars no longer has a global magnetic field, so a compass needle has nothing to line up with. You could use the
    stars instead: Sadr and Deneb point to the north celestial pole of Mars.

**7.** (advanced) How far away is the horizon for eyes 1.7 m above the ground on Mars, where $R = 3{,}389.5$ km?

??? note "Answer"
    $d \approx \sqrt{2Rh} = \sqrt{2 \times 3{,}389{,}500 \times 1.7} = \sqrt{11{,}524{,}300} \approx 3{,}390$ m, about
    3.4 km, against 4.7 km on the Earth.

**8.** (expert) When the bottom of the setting Sun seems to touch the horizon, where is the Sun really? Why are
measurements of stars close to the horizon unreliable?

??? note "Answer"
    Refraction lifts things on the horizon by about 35′, a little more than the Sun's width of about 32′. So the
    whole Sun is already below the true horizon. Close to the horizon the refraction is large and changes with the
    temperature of the air near the ground, so even after corrections it varies; Bessel found about half a minute of
    arc of scatter just above the horizon.

## Summary

- The horizon is where the sky meets the ground; the zenith is straight up and the nadir straight down.
- Azimuth measures direction round the horizon from north (0°) through east (90°), south (180°) and west (270°).
- The meridian runs from north through the zenith to south; each star is highest when it crosses it.
- The Big Dipper's Pointers lead to Polaris, less than 1° from the north celestial pole.
- The Southern Cross and its Pointers lead to the south celestial pole.
- At arm's length a little finger is about 1°, a fist about 10° and an outstretched hand about 20°.
- Peoples around the world have named the directions in many ways, and steered by the stars.
- On a sky chart east and west are swapped, because you look up instead of down.
- On Mars a compass is useless, but Sadr and Deneb point to its north celestial pole, and the horizon is nearer.
- Refraction lifts objects near the horizon by up to about 35′, more than the width of the Sun.
