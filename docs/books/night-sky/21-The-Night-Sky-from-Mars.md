# 21. The night sky from Mars

*Every chapter of this book has ended with a look at Mars. This last chapter puts those pieces together into one
night under the Martian sky. We watch the small Sun set and the long twilight fade, find the poles of the sky and the
Milky Way, follow the two moons, look for the Earth and the Moon side by side, and meet the other planets, the meteors,
the comets and the auroras that have been seen at Mars. Then we follow a whole Martian year of nights, and end by
working out where the Earth will be in the sky of Mars over the next year, and why it vanishes just when Mars comes
closest to us.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    **You will learn:**

    - how the Sun sets and the twilight fades on Mars
    - which stars mark the poles of Mars's sky, and where its Milky Way is
    - how to follow the two moons through a night

    - what other planets, meteors and comets have been seen from Mars
    - what auroras on Mars are like, and how one was first seen from the ground
    - how the sky changes through a Martian year
    - how to compare the Sun, the stars, the Earth and the Moon as seen from the two planets
    - why the Earth's greatest distance from the Sun in Mars's sky changes, and why the Earth vanishes at opposition

    **You need:** the earlier chapters, especially their Mars sections.

    **Levels:** 21.1 to 21.4 are beginner; 21.5 to 21.8 are intermediate; 21.9 is advanced; 21.10 is expert.

## 21.1 Sunset in Jezero { #sunset }

Imagine standing beside the rover Perseverance in Jezero crater, at 18.4° north (chapter 7, section 7.8), as the Sun
goes down.

- **A small Sun.** Mars is about 1.52 times as far from the Sun as the Earth, so the Sun looks only about five eighths
  as wide, 0.35° instead of 0.53°, and gives about 40 per cent as much light (chapter 4, section 4.9).
- **A blue sunset.** By day the sky is butterscotch, but round the setting Sun it turns blue, the opposite of ours
  (chapter 5, section 5.9).
- **A long twilight.** Dust high in the air goes on scattering sunlight, so twilight can last up to two hours
  (chapter 1, section 1.9).
- **A slightly longer day.** A solar day on Mars, a **sol**, lasts 24 hours 39 minutes 35 seconds (chapter 9,
  section 9.7).

    - The Sun from Mars about five eighths of its width from Earth, 0.35°, sending 40 per cent of the light:

    - The rest: the chapters named, and their sources.

## 21.2 The stars come out { #stars }

The stars are so far away that the constellations look the same from Mars as from the Earth (chapter 3, section
3.10), and so do their magnitudes (chapter 4) and colours. With no streetlights (chapter 20, section 20.8), the sky
is darker than most of us have ever seen.

What changes is where the sky turns. Mars's axis points to a different place among the stars:

- **North.** The north pole of Mars's sky lies in Cygnus, about halfway between Deneb and Alderamin (Alpha Cephei),
  less than 10° from Deneb (chapter 6, section 6.8). From Jezero it stands 18.4° above the northern horizon, and
  Deneb never sets. The line from Sadr through Deneb points to it.
- **The Milky Way.** The pole is only a few degrees from the Milky Way, so from the northern hemisphere of Mars the
  rich part of the Milky Way in Cygnus never sets.
- **South.** The south pole of Mars's sky is a couple of degrees from Kappa Velorum, and Canopus never sets from most
  southern latitudes (chapter 6, section 6.8). From Jezero, that pole is below the horizon.

## 21.3 Two moons { #moons }

- **Phobos**, the inner moon, rises in the **west**, crosses the sky in 4 hours 15 minutes or less and sets in the
  east, rising again every 11 hours 6 minutes (chapter 11, section 11.7). It is at most 0.20° wide, under half the
  width of our Moon, but so close that its phase and its size change visibly from hour to hour. At its brightest it
  reaches magnitude −9 to −10 (chapter 4, section 4.9). From Jezero it is easy to see; from beyond 70.4° north or
  south it never rises.
- **Deimos**, the outer moon, rises in the east and creeps across the sky; at the equator it stays up for 2.48 days
  (chapter 11, section 11.7). It is too small for its phases to be seen with the naked eye.

Sometimes Phobos crosses the Sun, in an eclipse that lasts about 30 seconds (chapter 12, section 12.8).

??? note "Sources"
    - Phobos's phases and size changing from hour to hour, and Deimos too small for its phases to be seen with the
      naked eye: Wikipedia, [Astronomy on Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy_on_Mars) (weaker).
    - The rest: the chapters named, and their sources. "Under half the width of our Moon" compares 0.20° with the
      Moon's 31′.

## 21.4 The Earth and the Moon { #earth }

From Mars the Earth is an inner planet, as Venus is for us, so it is always fairly near the Sun: a bright **evening
star** after sunset or **morning star** before sunrise (chapter 13, section 13.8). At its brightest it reaches about
magnitude −2.5, and the Moon shines beside it.

You could watch the Moon go round the Earth with the naked eye. When the two are farthest apart they are an easy
double; about a week later the Moon is in front of or behind the Earth and they merge into one point; a week after
that the Moon is farthest out on the other side. How far apart they look depends on how far away the Earth is: about
25′ when the Earth is closest to Mars, but only about 3.5′ when it is farthest.

## 21.5 The other planets { #planets }

- **Mercury** stays within about 15° of the Sun and would be very hard to see; **Venus** gets up to about 28° from it
  (chapter 13, section 13.8).
- **Jupiter** and **Saturn** are outer planets for Mars as for us, and loop backwards around their oppositions
  (chapter 14, section 14.7).
- **Crossing the Sun.** On **3 June 2014** the rover Curiosity watched Mercury cross the face of the Sun. It was the
  first time anyone had seen a planet cross the Sun from anywhere but the Earth. In March 2024 NASA released
  pictures taken by Perseverance of Deimos, Phobos and Mercury crossing the Sun. The Earth will next cross the Sun as
  seen from Mars on 10 November 2084 (chapter 12, section 12.8).

??? note "Sources"
    - Curiosity's view of the transit of Mercury on 3 June 2014, the first planetary transit seen from a body other
      than the Earth: Wikipedia,
      [Transit of Mercury from Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Transit_of_Mercury_from_Mars) (weaker).
    - Images of transits of Deimos, Phobos and Mercury seen by Perseverance, released on 5 March 2024: Wikipedia,
      [Perseverance (rover)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Perseverance_(rover)) (weaker).
    - The rest: the chapters named, and their sources.

## 21.6 Meteors and comets { #visitors }

- On **7 March 2004** the rover Spirit photographed the first meteor seen on Mars. It is thought to have come from
  the dust of comet 114P/Wiseman–Skiff (chapter 15, section 15.8).
- On **19 October 2014** comet Siding Spring passed extremely close to Mars, so close that its cloud of gas and dust
  may have wrapped round the planet (chapter 15, section 15.8).
- Rovers have found meteorites lying on the ground, and spacecraft have circled Mars since Mariner 9 in 1971
  (chapters 15 and 16).

??? note "Sources"
    - Spirit's meteor of 7 March 2004 and comet 114P/Wiseman–Skiff, and Siding Spring on 19 October 2014: Wikipedia,
      [Astronomy on Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy_on_Mars) (weaker), and chapter 15, section 15.8.
    - Meteorites found by rovers and spacecraft in orbit: chapter 15, section 15.8, and chapter 16, section 16.8, and
      their sources.

## 21.7 Auroras { #aurora }

On Earth, auroras gather round the magnetic poles. Mars has no magnetic field round the whole planet (chapter 2,
section 2.9), so its auroras appear instead over patches of magnetism left in its crust from the days when Mars did
have a field. They are a kind not seen anywhere else in the solar system.

- On **14 August 2004** the SPICAM instrument on the orbiter Mars Express first detected an aurora on Mars, over a
  region where the magnetism in the crust is strongest. Like most seen since, it glowed mainly in ultraviolet light,
  which our eyes cannot see.
- On **18 March 2024**, after an outburst on the Sun sent a storm of fast particles to Mars, Perseverance's cameras
  caught a faint **green** glow across the whole night sky. It came from oxygen atoms giving out light at 557.7 nm,
  the same green as in auroras on Earth. It was the first aurora ever seen from the surface of another planet. That
  one was too faint for human eyes; it showed only in the cameras.

??? note "Sources"
    - Auroras on Mars not at the poles, because Mars has no planet-wide magnetic field, but near magnetic anomalies in
      the crust left from an earlier magnetic field, a distinct kind not seen elsewhere in the solar system, largely
      ultraviolet: Wikipedia, [Astronomy on Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy_on_Mars) (weaker).
    - The aurora of 14 August 2004 detected by SPICAM on Mars Express, over the region of strongest crustal
      magnetism in the Mars Global Surveyor map: Wikipedia, [Aurora](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aurora) (weaker).
    - The green aurora of 18 March 2024 seen by Perseverance's SuperCam and Mastcam-Z after a solar flare and coronal
      mass ejection, oxygen at 557.7 nm, the first from the surface of another planet, too faint for the eye, reported
      in *Science Advances* in 2025: *BBC Sky at Night Magazine*,
      [Green aurora will greet future Mars astronauts](https://www.skyatnightmagazine.com/news/mars-green-aurora-perseverance).

## 21.8 A year of Martian nights { #year }

- **The stars drift slowly.** As on Earth, the stars rise a little earlier each night, so the night sky moves on
  through the year. But a Mars year is 687 Earth days, or 668.6 sols, so the drift is about half as fast as ours
  (section 21.9), and each season's sky stays nearly twice as long.
- **Long, lopsided seasons.** Mars is tilted 25.2°, close to the Earth's 23.4°, so it has seasons, but they are about
  twice as long as ours and of unequal length, because Mars's orbit is more oval (chapter 8, section 8.7).
- **A calendar.** People have designed calendars for Mars, such as the Darian calendar of 24 months (chapter 9,
  section 9.7).
- **The Earth's cycle.** The Earth comes back to the same place in Mars's sky about every 780 days, the same rhythm
  as Mars's oppositions for us (chapter 14).

??? note "Sources"
    - The Mars year of 687 days, 668.6 sols, the tilt of 25.2°, and long, lopsided seasons: chapter 8, section 8.7,
      and its sources. The Darian calendar: chapter 9, section 9.7, and its sources.
    - The 780 days: chapter 14 and its sources, and section 21.9.

## 21.9 Working it out { #working }

**The Sun's width.** The Sun looks 0.53° wide from the Earth. From 1.524 times as far, it looks $0.53 / 1.524 =
0.35°$.

**How fast the stars drift.** A star comes back to the same place after one turn against the stars, a **sidereal
day**. The Earth's is 23 h 56 min 4 s, so stars rise 3 min 56 s earlier each solar day. Mars's sidereal day is
24 h 37 min 22 s and its sol 24 h 39 min 35 s, so stars rise only 2 min 13 s earlier each sol. Over a Mars year that
adds up to one whole sol: $668.6 \times 2 \text{ min } 13 \text{ s} \approx 24.7$ hours.

**Every 780 days.** The Earth gains on Mars by $1/365.256 - 1/686.980$ of a turn a day, so it laps Mars every

$$\frac{1}{1/365.256 - 1/686.980} = 780 \text{ days}.$$

This is the same number seen from either planet: when the Earth laps Mars, Mars is at opposition for us and the
Earth is in front of the Sun for Mars.

**The Moon beside the Earth.** At the Earth's greatest angle from the Sun, the Sun, Earth and Mars make a right
angle at the Earth, so the Earth is $\sqrt{1.524^2 - 1} = 1.150$ AU, or 172 million km, from Mars. The Moon is at
most 384,400 km from the Earth, so the gap looks

$$\frac{384{,}400}{172{,}000{,}000} = 0.00223 \text{ rad} = 0.00223 \times \frac{180 \times 60}{\pi} = 7.7′.$$

That is well above the 1′ or so that a sharp eye can split.

| | Mars | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Sun's width | 0.35° | 0.53° |
| Sidereal day | 24 h 37 min 22 s | 23 h 56 min 4 s |
| Stars rise earlier each day by | 2 min 13 s | 3 min 56 s |
| Brightest inner planet, greatest angle from the Sun | the Earth, about 41° on average | Venus, 46.3° |
| A planet and its moon side by side to the eye | the Earth and Moon, up to about 25′ apart | none |
| The other planet comes back every | 780 days (the Earth) | 780 days (Mars) |

## 21.10 Where is the Earth? { #where }

**The average answer.** Chapter 13 worked out that an inner planet gets farthest from the Sun when the line from the
outer planet just touches the inner planet's orbit, a right angle at the inner planet. With round orbits,
$\sin E = 1 / 1.524$, so $E = 41.0°$.

**Mars's oval orbit.** But Mars's distance from the Sun changes, from 206.65 million km (1.381 AU) at its nearest,
**perihelion**, to 249.26 million km (1.666 AU) at its farthest, **aphelion**. So

$$\arcsin \frac{1}{1.666} = 36.9° \qquad \text{to} \qquad \arcsin \frac{1}{1.381} = 46.4°.$$

The Earth's own distance from the Sun changes a little too. Working through the real orbits from 2000 to 2049, we
find greatest angles from 36.2° to 47.4°. In 2026 and 2027 Mars is near aphelion, so the angles are small: about 38°
(Figure A).

**Gone at opposition.** On **19 February 2027** Mars is at opposition for us (chapter 14): the Sun, the Earth and Mars
are in a line, with the Earth in the middle. Seen from Mars, the Earth is then almost straight in front of the Sun,
with its night side turned towards Mars. In our calculation it passes about 2.7° from the Sun, not quite across it,
and is lost in the glare. So the best time to see Mars from the Earth is the worst time to see the Earth from Mars,
and the other way round. At the Earth's greatest angles from Mars, the right angle is at the Earth, so for us Mars
is about 90° from the Sun: in our morning sky in November 2026 and in our evening sky in June 2027, farther away and
fainter than at opposition.

**How close?** At that moment the Earth is 0.678 AU from Mars, about 101 million km, the same distance as chapter 14
gives for the close approach. Then the Moon can be up to

$$\frac{384{,}400}{101{,}000{,}000} \times \frac{180 \times 60}{\pi} = 13′$$

from the Earth, but no one could see it so close to the Sun.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Why does the Sun look smaller from Mars?

??? note "Answer"
    Mars is about 1.52 times as far from the Sun, so the Sun looks only about five eighths as wide, 0.35° instead of
    0.53°.

**2.** (beginner) Which bright star is near the north pole of Mars's sky?

??? note "Answer"
    Deneb, in Cygnus, less than 10° away. The pole is about halfway between Deneb and Alderamin.

**3.** (beginner) In which direction does Phobos rise?

??? note "Answer"
    In the west. It goes round Mars faster than Mars spins, so it overtakes the turning of the sky.

**4.** (intermediate) Why is the Earth always fairly near the Sun in Mars's sky?

??? note "Answer"
    Its orbit is inside Mars's, so from Mars it is an inner planet, like Venus for us. It can never get more than
    about 47° from the Sun.

**5.** (intermediate) What was new about the aurora Perseverance photographed on 18 March 2024?

??? note "Answer"
    It was the first aurora seen from the surface of another planet, and it was seen in visible light: a faint green
    from oxygen at 557.7 nm. Earlier Martian auroras had been seen from orbit, mainly in ultraviolet.

**6.** (intermediate) In 2027, why can people on Earth see Mars well in February, while someone on Mars could not see
the Earth?

??? note "Answer"
    In February 2027 Mars is at opposition: the Earth is between the Sun and Mars. From the Earth, Mars is opposite
    the Sun and up all night; from Mars, the Earth is almost in front of the Sun and lost in its glare.

**7.** (advanced) Stars on Mars rise 2 min 13 s earlier each sol. How many sols does it take for a star to rise 6
hours earlier? About what fraction of a Mars year is that?

??? note "Answer"
    6 hours is 360 minutes, and $360 / 2.22 = 162$ sols. That is $162 / 668.6$, about a quarter of a Mars year, just as
    on Earth the sky moves on by 6 hours in about three months.

**8.** (expert) Mars is at perihelion, 1.381 AU from the Sun, and the Earth is 1.017 AU from the Sun. What is the
largest possible angle between the Earth and the Sun as seen from Mars?

??? note "Answer"
    $\sin E = 1.017 / 1.381 = 0.736$, so $E = 47.4°$. This matches the largest value we found from the real orbits.

## Summary

- On Mars the Sun looks five eighths as wide, sets in a blue glow, and leaves a twilight of up to two hours.
- The constellations are the same, but the north pole of the sky is near Deneb, with the Milky Way always up in the
  north, and the south pole is near Kappa Velorum.
- Phobos rises in the west twice a sol and changes visibly by the hour; Deimos creeps slowly from the east.
- The Earth is an evening or morning star with the Moon beside it, up to 25′ apart. In 2026 to 2027 look for it in
  the evening in November 2026 and in the morning in June 2027.
- Curiosity saw Mercury cross the Sun in 2014; Perseverance saw a green aurora in 2024, a first from the surface of
  another planet.
- The stars drift about half as fast as ours, through a year of 668.6 sols.
- The Earth's greatest angle from the Sun ranges from about 36° to 47° because Mars's orbit is oval.
- When Mars is closest and brightest for us, the Earth is lost in the Sun's glare for Mars.
