# 13. Finding the planets

*Among the fixed stars, a few bright lights slowly change their places from week to week. These are the planets, and
five of them are easy to see with the naked eye. This chapter shows how to tell a planet from a star, where to look
for each one, and why Mercury and Venus only ever appear in the evening or morning twilight while Mars, Jupiter and
Saturn can be up all night. It gives the best dates to see the planets in 2027 and 2028, looks at how the planets
were named and watched around the world, and visits Mars, where the Earth is the evening star. It ends by working out
when Venus is at its brightest.*

## 13.1 Stars that wander { #wanderers }

The word **planet** comes from the Greek for "wanderer". Ancient sky-watchers saw that the stars keep their patterns
night after night, but that a few bright lights move slowly among them. Besides the Sun and the Moon there were five:
**Mercury, Venus, Mars, Jupiter and Saturn**. The Babylonians, like many others, counted seven wanderers in all.

How can you tell a planet from a star?

- **It is in the wrong place.** If a bright light sits in a constellation where your chart shows no bright star, it is
  probably a planet.
- **It does not twinkle much.** Stars are so far away that they are points of light, and the moving air easily makes
  them flicker (chapter 4, section 4.4). A planet shows a tiny disc, so it usually shines more steadily.
- **It moves.** Note its place against the stars and look again a week or two later.
- **It lies near the Sun's path**, as the next section explains.

## 13.2 Along the Sun's path { #ecliptic }

All the planets go round the Sun in nearly the same plane as the Earth. So from the Earth they are always seen close
to the **ecliptic**, the Sun's yearly path among the stars (chapter 8, section 8.1). The Moon stays close to it too.

The band about 8° either side of the ecliptic is the **zodiac**, and the Moon and the bright planets are always found
in it. The zodiac's constellations, such as Taurus, Leo and Sagittarius, and also Ophiuchus (chapter 8, section 8.1),
are the places to look.

A good way to find the ecliptic on any night is to look at the Moon. A bright "star" lined up with the Moon, or with
where the Sun has just set, is very likely to be a planet.

??? note "Sources"
    - The zodiac as a belt about 8° either side of the ecliptic, in which the Moon and the brightest planets appear:
      Wikipedia, [Zodiac](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zodiac) (weaker), which also lists the zodiac constellations.
    - The planets' orbits tilted at most 7° to the Earth's (Mercury's 7.0°, the rest less): NASA JPL,
      [Approximate Positions of the Planets](https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/planets/approx_pos.html).

## 13.3 Mercury and Venus: morning and evening stars { #inner }

Mercury and Venus are closer to the Sun than we are. So from the Earth they never get far from the Sun in the sky. We
see them in the west after sunset, as an **evening star**, or in the east before sunrise, as a **morning star**, but
never in the middle of the night.

When the planet passes between the Earth and the Sun it is at **inferior conjunction**; when it is behind the Sun, at
**superior conjunction**. Either way it is lost in the Sun's glare. The best time to look is around **greatest
elongation**, when it is farthest from the Sun in the sky.

**Venus** is the brightest thing in the sky after the Sun and the Moon, reaching about magnitude **−4.9**. It is
bright enough to see in broad daylight if you know where to look, and so bright that it is often reported as a UFO. It
can be up to 47° from the Sun, so at its best it stays up long after sunset, or rises long before sunrise. It passes
from evening star to morning star and back every **584 days**. Five of these cycles take almost exactly **eight
years**, so Venus repeats its appearances in our sky every eight years.

**Mercury** is much harder. It is never more than 18° to 28° from the Sun, so it is only seen low down, for a short
time in the evening or morning twilight. The best evening views come in **spring**, and the best morning views in
**autumn**, when the Sun's path stands steeply to the horizon at dusk or dawn. This is the same effect as the harvest
Moon (chapter 11, section 11.2). Look low over a clear, flat horizon in the bright twilight soon after sunset or before
sunrise.

- At **opposition** the planet is opposite the Sun in the sky. It rises at sunset, is highest around midnight and sets
  at sunrise. It is also at about its closest to the Earth, and so at its brightest. This is the best time to look.
- At **conjunction** it is behind the Sun and cannot be seen for weeks.

The time from one opposition to the next is the planet's **synodic period**: about **780 days** for Mars, **399 days**
for Jupiter and **378 days** for Saturn. So Jupiter comes to opposition about a month later each year, and Saturn about
two weeks later, while Mars is at its best only every two years and two months.

**Mars** is reddish (chapter 5). At its best it reaches magnitude −2.9, but its brightness changes a great deal, as
chapter 14 explains.

**Jupiter** is the third-brightest thing in the night sky, after the Moon and Venus.
Its four largest moons, **Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto**, can be seen in ordinary binoculars held steady: tiny
points in a line on either side of the planet, changing position from night to night. Galileo found them in 1610
(history book, [chapter 15, section 15.3](../history/15-Galileo-and-the-Telescope.md#jupiter)).

**Saturn** shines around magnitude 0.5. Its **rings** need a telescope or very large binoculars magnifying
at least 30 times. Every 14 or 15 years the rings turn edge-on to us and seem to vanish, as they did for Galileo in
1612 (history book, [chapter 15, section 15.4](../history/15-Galileo-and-the-Telescope.md#venus)). This last
happened in 2025, and will happen next in 2038 and 2039.

## 13.6 Uranus and Neptune { #faint }

Two more planets are too faint to have been noticed in ancient times.

**Uranus** is around magnitude 5.4 to 5.6. At opposition it can just be seen with the naked eye under a dark sky, and
it is easy in binoculars even from a city, if you know exactly where to look; a chart from an app or a magazine shows
its place among the stars. It moves so slowly, once round the Sun in 84 years, that ancient observers never picked it
out as a planet. **William Herschel** found it in 1781, the first planet discovered since ancient times (history book,
[chapter 17, section 17.1](../history/17-Herschel-and-the-Canals-of-Mars.md#herschel)).

**Neptune**, around magnitude 7.8, is too faint for the naked eye. A telescope or strong binoculars show it as a small
blue disc. It was found in 1846 by calculation: Urbain Le Verrier worked out where an unseen planet must be to pull
Uranus off course, and on 23 September Johann Galle found it within a degree of the predicted place (history book,
[chapter 17, section 17.4](../history/17-Herschel-and-the-Canals-of-Mars.md#rule)).

??? note "Sources"
    - Uranus visible to the naked eye at opposition in dark skies and easy with binoculars even in urban conditions,
      dim and slow, never recognised by ancient observers, an 84-year orbit, and Herschel in 1781: Wikipedia,
      [Uranus](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranus) (weaker). Apparent magnitude 5.38 to 5.57: NASA,
      [Uranus Fact Sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/uranusfact.html).

## 13.7 Planets around the world { #world }

People everywhere watched the planets, and many tracked them with great care.

- In **Babylon**, a clay tablet lists, for 21 years, the dates when Venus vanished and reappeared (history book,
  [chapter 2, section 2.5](../history/02-Mesopotamia.md#venus)).
- The **Maya** Dresden Codex has six pages of Venus tables, giving the days when Venus should appear and disappear as
  morning and evening star, and three tables following Mars through its 780-day cycle (history book,
  [chapter 11, section 11.3](../history/11-The-Americas.md#dresden)).
- In **China** each planet was linked with one of the five elements: Mars with fire (Huǒxīng), Mercury with water
  (Shuǐxīng), Jupiter with wood (Mùxīng), Venus with metal (Jīnxīng) and Saturn with earth (Tǔxīng). These are still
  the planets' names in Chinese. The old astronomical names were different: Venus, for example, was Tàibái.
- In **India** the **navagraha**, the "nine planets", are the Sun, the Moon, the five planets, and **Rahu** and
  **Ketu**, the two nodes of the Moon where eclipses happen (chapters 11 and 12).
- In **1610** Galileo saw that Venus goes through phases like the Moon, which showed that it goes round the Sun
  (history book, [chapter 15, section 15.4](../history/15-Galileo-and-the-Telescope.md#venus)).

## 13.8 Planets from Mars { #mars }

From Mars the Earth is an inner planet, as Venus is for us. So the Earth appears as a bright **evening star** or
**morning star**, never far from the Sun. It can be up to 41° from the Sun (section 13.9). At its brightest it reaches
about magnitude −2.5, and the Moon, about +0.9, can be seen beside it with the naked eye. When the Earth is closest to
Mars the two can be up to 25 minutes of arc apart, almost the width of our Moon, and a watcher on Mars could see the
Moon go round the Earth. Through a telescope both would show as crescents.

On 8 May 2003, close to the Earth's greatest elongation as seen from Mars, the orbiter **Mars Global Surveyor** took
a picture of the Earth and the Moon together.

Mars has three inner planets to look for: Mercury, Venus and the Earth. Mercury stays within 15° of the Sun and would
be very hard to see; Venus can reach 28° from the Sun. Jupiter and Saturn are outer planets for Mars as they are for
us, and come to opposition about every 816 and 734 days.

## 13.9 Working it out { #working }

**Greatest elongation.** At greatest elongation the line from the Earth just touches the inner planet's orbit, so the
angle at the planet is a right angle (figure A). If the planet's orbit has radius $r$ (in AU) and the Earth's is 1,

$$\sin E = r.$$

- Venus: $\sin E = 0.723$, so $E = 46.3^\circ$. The real values, 45° to 47°, differ a little because the orbits are not
  perfect circles.
- Mercury: $\sin E = 0.387$, so $E = 22.8^\circ$. But Mercury's orbit is quite stretched, with eccentricity 0.206, so
  its distance from the Sun ranges from $0.387 \times 0.794 = 0.307$ to $0.387 \times 1.206 = 0.467$ AU. That gives
  greatest elongations from $17.9^\circ$ to $27.8^\circ$, just the 18° to 28° that is seen.

**Synodic period.** If the Earth goes round the Sun in $P_E$ days and the planet in $P$ days, then each day one gains
on the other by $360/P_E - 360/P$ degrees. They line up again when the gain reaches 360°:

$$\frac{1}{S} = \left|\frac{1}{P_E} - \frac{1}{P}\right|.$$

For Mars, $P = 686.98$ days: $1/S = 1/365.256 - 1/686.98$, so $S = 779.9$ days. For Jupiter, $P = 4{,}332.6$ days and
$S = 398.9$ days. For Venus, $P = 224.70$ days and $S = 583.9$ days.

**From Mars.** Put Mars's numbers in place of the Earth's: orbit 1.524 AU and year 686.98 days.

## 13.10 When is Venus brightest? { #brilliancy }

You might expect Venus to be brightest when it is full. But a full Venus is on the far side of the Sun, as far from us
as it can be. When it is closest, at inferior conjunction, its lit side faces away from us. Its brightness is a
balance between these two effects.

**A simple model.** Take circular orbits: Venus at $r = 0.723$ AU, the Earth at 1 AU, and let $\theta$ be the angle at
the Sun between the two planets. By the law of cosines the distance from the Earth to Venus is

$$d^2 = 1 + r^2 - 2r\cos\theta.$$

The angle $\alpha$ at Venus, between the Sun and the Earth, is the **phase angle**, and

$$\cos\alpha = \frac{r^2 + d^2 - 1}{2rd}.$$

The fraction of the disc that is lit is $f = (1 + \cos\alpha)/2$: 1 when full, 0 when new. Take the brightness as the
lit fraction divided by the distance squared:

$$B = \frac{f}{d^2}.$$

| Where Venus is | $\theta$ | $d$ (AU) | lit fraction $f$ | $B$ |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Full, beyond the Sun | 180° | 1.723 | 1.00 | 0.34 |
| Greatest elongation | 43.7° | 0.690 | 0.50 | 1.05 |
| Brightest | 22.4° | 0.430 | 0.27 | 1.44 |

Trying values of $\theta$ shows that $B$ is largest at about $\theta = 22^\circ$, when Venus is a crescent about a
quarter lit and about 40° from the Sun in our sky. The angle $\theta$ changes by $360/583.92 = 0.617^\circ$ a day, so
this happens $22.4/0.617 \approx 36$ days before and after inferior conjunction.

This matches what is seen: Venus is brightest as a crescent, about a month before and after inferior conjunction.
The model is rough, because the clouds of Venus do not scatter light evenly, but it gets the timing right.

The same holds on Mars. The Earth, seen from Mars, is brightest as a crescent, not when it is full.

    - Venus's orbit of 0.723 AU and synodic period of 583.92 days: sections 13.3 and 13.9 and their sources.
    - The model and the arithmetic are ours. The claim about the Earth seen from Mars follows from the same model with

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Give two ways to tell a planet from a star.

??? note "Answer"
    Any two of: it is in a place where the chart shows no bright star; it twinkles less; it moves against the stars from
    week to week; it lies near the ecliptic.

**2.** (beginner) Why can you never see Venus at midnight?

??? note "Answer"
    Venus is closer to the Sun than the Earth is, so it never gets more than about 47° from the Sun in the sky. At
    midnight the Sun is far below the horizon, and Venus is below it too.

**3.** (beginner) What can you see around Jupiter with ordinary binoculars?

??? note "Answer"
    Its four largest moons, Io, Europa, Ganymede and Callisto, as tiny points in a line beside the planet.

**4.** (intermediate) What is opposition, and why is it the best time to see an outer planet?

??? note "Answer"
    The planet is opposite the Sun in the sky, so it is up all night, and it is at about its closest to the Earth, so
    it is at its brightest.

**5.** (intermediate) Jupiter is at opposition on 11 February 2027. Roughly when will its next opposition be?

??? note "Answer"
    About 399 days later, a year and a month: in March 2028 (12 March 2028).

**6.** (intermediate) Who are Rahu and Ketu in the navagraha, and how do they connect to eclipses?

??? note "Answer"
    They are the two nodes of the Moon's orbit. Eclipses happen only when the new or full Moon is near one of them.

**7.** (advanced) Work out the greatest elongation of the Earth as seen from Mars.

??? note "Answer"
    $\sin E = 1/1.524 = 0.656$, so $E = 41.0^\circ$.

**8.** (expert) Why is Venus brighter as a crescent than when it is full?

??? note "Answer"
    A full Venus is on the far side of the Sun, about 1.72 AU away. As a crescent it is much closer, about 0.43 AU at
    its brightest, so it looks about $(1.72/0.43)^2 = 16$ times larger in area. Only about a quarter of it is lit, but
    $16 \times 0.27$ is still about four times more light than the full, distant Venus in this simple model.

## Summary

- Planets move slowly among the stars, twinkle less, and always stay near the ecliptic, inside the zodiac.
- Mercury and Venus are inner planets: evening or morning stars, best at greatest elongation, 18° to 28° for Mercury
  and about 46° for Venus.
- Venus reaches magnitude −4.9 and repeats its pattern every eight years; Mercury is seen only low in twilight.
- Mars, Jupiter and Saturn are best at opposition, up all night; their synodic periods are 780, 399 and 378 days.
- Binoculars show Jupiter's four big moons; a telescope magnifying 30 times shows Saturn's rings.
- In 2027, Venus is a morning star in January, and Mars and Jupiter reach opposition in February.
- Uranus is just visible to the naked eye; Neptune needs binoculars or a telescope.
- From Mars, the Earth is an evening or morning star, up to 41° from the Sun.
- Venus is brightest as a crescent, about 36 days from inferior conjunction.
