# 15. Meteors and comets

*On any clear, dark night you will see a few "shooting stars", and on certain nights of the year you may see dozens
an hour. A few times in a lifetime a bright comet hangs in the sky for weeks with a glowing tail. Both come from the
small, icy and rocky bodies that wander the solar system, and they are linked: most meteor showers are the dust left
behind by comets. This chapter explains what meteors and comets are, gives the best meteor showers and how the Moon
treats them in 2027, tells of fireballs and fallen stones, follows comets through history, visits Mars, and ends by
working out how fast a meteor can hit the Earth.*

    - what meteoroids, meteors and meteorites are

    **You need:** chapters 1 and 10.

## 15.1 Shooting stars { #shooting }

A **shooting star** is not a star. It is a speck of space rock or dust burning up as it rushes into the Earth's air.

- While it is still in space, it is a **meteoroid**. Meteoroids range from dust grains to small asteroids.
- The streak of light it makes as it burns up in the air is a **meteor**.
- If a piece survives and lands, it is a **meteorite**.

Most meteors are made by bits no bigger than a grain of sand or a pea, which burn up long before they reach the
ground. They are bright because they are so fast: meteoroids hit the air at between about 11 and 72 km/s. A meteor
brighter than Venus is called a **fireball**.

On any clear, dark night you can see several meteors an hour. Altogether about 44 tonnes of this material falls on the
Earth every day.

## 15.2 Meteor showers { #showers }

On certain nights each year the number of meteors rises sharply. This is a **meteor shower**. It happens when the
Earth passes through a stream of dust left along the orbit of a comet (or, in a few cases, an asteroid). Since the
Earth crosses each stream at the same point in its orbit every year, each shower comes back on about the same date.

The dust grains in a stream are all moving the same way, on parallel paths. Seen from the ground, parallel paths seem
to spread out from one point, just as parallel railway lines seem to meet in the distance. That point is the
**radiant**, and each shower is named after the constellation where it lies: the Perseids from Perseus, the Leonids
from Leo, the Geminids from Gemini.

The link with comets was found in the 1800s. After the great storm of Leonids in **1833**, Denison Olmsted showed that
the meteors came from a cloud of space dust, with a radiant in Leo. In **1866** Giovanni Schiaparelli showed that the
Leonids share the orbit of a comet, Tempel–Tuttle.

| Quadrantids | early January | can rival the Perseids, but the peak lasts only hours | asteroid 2003 EH1 | thin crescent: good |
| Lyrids | 22–23 April | 5 to 20 | comet Thatcher | just past full: poor |
| Eta Aquariids | about 5 May | rich, best before dawn | Halley's comet | new Moon: very good |
| Perseids | about 12 August (9 to 14 August) | 60 or more | comet Swift–Tuttle | sets in the early hours: fair |
| Orionids | 21–22 October | 50 to 70 in some years | Halley's comet | waning, rises late evening: poor |

The two Halley showers come from the same comet: the Earth crosses its orbit twice a year, in May and in October.

The **Perseids** of **2028** may be special. One calculation suggests the Earth could pass through an extra-thick trail
of dust from Swift–Tuttle on the night of **11–12 August 2028**, and there might be a storm. Predictions like this are
uncertain, but it is worth staying up for.

## 15.4 How to watch { #watch }

You need no telescope or binoculars, just your eyes, a dark sky and patience.

- **Get away from lights**, and give your eyes 20 minutes or more to adapt to the dark (chapter 1).
- **Watch after midnight.** Most showers get better after midnight, when your side of the Earth faces forward along
  its orbit and sweeps up more meteoroids, like the front windscreen of a car in the rain (section 15.10). The best
  time is usually just before dawn, before the sky starts to brighten.
- **Lie back** on a reclining chair or a mat, so you can take in as much of the sky as possible. Meteors appear all
  over the sky, not only near the radiant.
- **Check the Moon.** A bright Moon drowns out all but the brightest meteors.
- **Dress warmly**, even in August.

The rates in the table are for perfect conditions. Astronomers quote the **zenithal hourly rate**: the number one
observer would see in an hour at the peak with the radiant overhead and a perfectly dark sky. You will usually see
fewer.

??? note "Sources"
    - Most showers improving after midnight as the observer's side of the Earth faces the direction of its orbit, and
      the best time generally slightly before dawn: Wikipedia,
      [Meteor shower](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Meteor_shower) (weaker).
    - The zenithal hourly rate: Wikipedia, [Zenithal hourly rate](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Zenithal_hourly_rate)
      (weaker).
    - Dark adaptation taking about 20 to 30 minutes: chapter 1 and its sources. The rest is common practical advice.

## 15.5 Fireballs and meteorites { #fireballs }

Now and then a bigger rock comes in. A very bright meteor, magnitude −4 or brighter, is a **fireball** or **bolide**.

**Chelyabinsk, 2013.** On **15 February 2013** a rock about 18 metres across and weighing about 9,000 tonnes hit the
air over the southern Urals in Russia at about 19 km/s. It exploded about 30 km up, briefly brighter than the Sun, with
the energy of 400 to 500 thousand tonnes of TNT. Nobody was hit by the rock itself, but the shock wave arrived minutes
after the flash and blew in windows: 1,491 people needed medical treatment, mostly for cuts from broken glass.

**Tunguska, 1908.** On **30 June 1908** a huge explosion over central Siberia flattened about 2,150 square kilometres
of forest. When scientists finally reached the place, there was no crater: the object had burst in the air.

**Meteorites.** Most meteorites are **stony**; others are **iron** (mostly iron and nickel) or **stony-iron**. The
largest known in one piece, the **Hoba** meteorite in Namibia, weighs more than 60 tonnes; a farmer found it in 1920
while ploughing. In Canada, a fireball over the Yukon and northern British Columbia on **18 January 2000** dropped the
**Tagish Lake** meteorite, and more than 500 pieces were picked up from the frozen lake.

Some peoples remember old falls in their stories. The Djaru people call the Wolfe Creek meteorite crater in Western
Australia **Kandimalal**, and tell of an evening star that grew hot and fell to the ground with a great explosion
(history book, [chapter 12, section 12.6](../history/12-Oceania-and-Australia.md#aboriginal)).

## 15.6 Comets { #comets }

A **comet** is a loose lump of ice, dust and small rocks, from a few hundred metres to tens of kilometres across, a
"dirty snowball". Most of the time it is far from the
Sun, frozen and invisible. As it comes closer, sunlight warms it and gas and dust stream off, forming a fuzzy cloud
round it, the **coma**, and often one or two **tails**.

- The **ion tail** is gas, made electrically charged by sunlight and swept straight back by the solar wind. It points
  directly away from the Sun.
- The **dust tail** is pushed away by the pressure of sunlight. It bends into a gentle curve.

A great comet's tail can stretch up to 30° across the sky, 60 times the width of the Moon.

Many short-period comets, which come back in less than 200 years, come from the **Kuiper belt** beyond Neptune.
Long-period comets come from the **Oort cloud**, a vast, distant shell of icy bodies reaching halfway to the nearest
star.

About one comet a year can be seen with the naked eye, though most are faint. Bright ones are rare and hard to
predict:

- **Halley's comet** comes back every 74 to 79 years. It last came in 1986, when the European spacecraft Giotto flew
  past it, and it will be back in mid-2061.
- **Hale–Bopp** was visible to the naked eye for a record 18 months around its closest approach to the Sun in April
  1997.
- **NEOWISE** could be seen with the naked eye through July 2020, the brightest comet in the northern sky since
  Hale–Bopp.
- **Tsuchinshan–ATLAS** reached magnitude about −4.9 on 9 October 2024, when it was only 3.5° from the Sun.

## 15.7 Comets and meteors in history { #history }

- **Babylonian** astronomers recorded Halley's comet in 164 BC and 87 BC (history book,
  [chapter 2, section 2.6](../history/02-Mesopotamia.md#diaries)).
- **Chinese** astronomers called comets "broom stars" and recorded them for centuries, including Halley's comet in
  240 BC (history book, [chapter 4, section 4.7](../history/04-China.md#guests)). The oldest known record of a meteor
  shower, the Lyrids of 687 BC, is in the Chinese chronicle *Zuo Zhuan*.
- In **1066** Halley's comet appeared in the year of the Norman conquest of England (history book,
  [chapter 9, section 9.5](../history/09-Byzantium-and-Medieval-Europe.md#skies)).
- In **1577** Tycho Brahe showed that a great comet was far beyond the Moon (history book,
  [chapter 14, section 14.2](../history/14-Tycho-and-Kepler.md#newstar)).
- In **1583** the chronicler Mahmud Kati, in Timbuktu, described a night when stars flew about until dawn, probably
  the Perseids (history book, [chapter 10, section 10.5](../history/10-Africa.md#timbuktu)).
- Edmond Halley predicted that the comet of 1682 would return in 1758, and on **25 December 1758** it was seen again
  (history book,
  [chapter 16, section 16.5](../history/16-Newton-Halley-and-Romer.md#halley)).

## 15.8 Meteors and comets at Mars { #mars }

Mars's air is thin: the pressure at the surface is less than 1% of ours. But it is enough to burn up small meteoroids,
so meteors can be seen there too. On **7 March 2004** the rover **Spirit** photographed the first meteor seen from
Mars. It is now thought to have come from a shower of dust from the comet 114P/Wiseman–Skiff.

On **19 October 2014** the comet **Siding Spring** passed only about 140,000 km from Mars, less than half the
distance from the Earth to the Moon. The spacecraft orbiting Mars came through safely, and they detected comet dust,
thousands of kilograms an hour, rich in magnesium, iron and sodium, raining into the Martian air.

Meteorites have been found on Mars as well. In **January 2005** the rover **Opportunity** came across a
basketball-sized lump of iron and nickel, **Heat Shield Rock**, the first meteorite ever found on another planet.

And some meteorites on Earth come from Mars. Big impacts there blast rocks into space, and a few end up falling on the
Earth, like the stones of **Tissint** that fell in Morocco in 2011 (history book,
[chapter 10, section 10.9](../history/10-Africa.md#mars)).

## 15.9 Working it out { #working }

**Energy.** Moving energy is $\tfrac{1}{2}mv^2$. A 1-gram meteoroid, a small pebble, at 60 km/s carries

$$\tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.001 \times 60{,}000^2 = 1{,}800{,}000 \text{ J}.$$

A one-tonne car at 100 km/h (27.8 m/s) carries $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 1000 \times 27.8^2 = 386{,}000$ J. So a pebble
from space carries more energy than a car on a motorway, nearly five times as much. That is why even a tiny meteoroid
glows so brightly as the air stops it.

**How much falls?** NASA gives about 44 tonnes a day. Another estimate gives about 15,000 tonnes a year:
$15{,}000 / 365 = 41$ tonnes a day. The two agree well.

**Mars and Earth.** The slowest a meteoroid can arrive is the planet's escape speed (section 15.10). The fastest is a
head-on meeting with something falling in from far away.

| | Mars | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Air pressure at the surface | 6.36 millibars | 1,014 millibars |
| Slowest arrival (escape speed) | 5.0 km/s | 11.2 km/s |
| The planet's speed round the Sun | 24.1 km/s | 29.8 km/s |
| Fastest head-on arrival | about $24.1 + 34.1 = 58$ km/s | about $29.8 + 42.1 = 72$ km/s |

## 15.10 How fast can a meteor be? { #speeds }

**The slowest.** Even a meteoroid that drifts up to the Earth from nowhere gets pulled in by the Earth's gravity.
Falling from far away, it arrives at the Earth's **escape speed**, 11.2 km/s. No meteor can be slower than that, and
indeed the slowest are seen entering at about 11 km/s.

**The fastest.** Anything orbiting the Sun near the Earth's distance cannot go faster than the Sun's escape speed at
that distance, or it would leave the solar system. Escape speed is $\sqrt{2}$ times the speed of a circular orbit at
the same distance, so at 1 AU it is

$$\sqrt{2} \times 29.8 = 42.1 \text{ km/s}.$$

That matches the fastest meteoroids measured near the Earth, about 42 km/s. If one of these meets the Earth head-on,
the speeds add:

$$29.8 + 42.1 = 71.9 \text{ km/s},$$

the 72 km/s seen for the fastest meteors. (The Earth's gravity adds a little more, but not much at these speeds.)

**Why more before dawn.** The Earth moves round the Sun at 29.8 km/s. In the evening you are on the trailing side of
the Earth, and only meteoroids fast enough to catch up with the Earth from behind can reach you. In the hours before
dawn you are on the leading side, facing forward along the orbit, and you meet meteoroids coming from ahead as well,
at high speed. At about 6 a.m. you are right at the front. That is why the rates rise after midnight and why the
pre-dawn meteors are, on average, faster.

The Perseids show this well: their rate is greatest in the hours before dawn, when the side of the Earth you are on is
moving forward into the stream.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) What is the difference between a meteoroid, a meteor and a meteorite?

??? note "Answer"
    A meteoroid is the rock or dust in space; a meteor is the streak of light as it burns up in the air; a meteorite is
    a piece that survives and lands.

**2.** (beginner) Why does a meteor shower happen on about the same date every year?

??? note "Answer"
    The Earth crosses the same stream of comet dust at the same point in its orbit each year.

**3.** (beginner) Give two tips for watching a meteor shower.

??? note "Answer"
    Any two of: get away from lights; watch after midnight, ideally before dawn; lie back and take in the whole sky;
    choose a night without a bright Moon; let your eyes adapt; dress warmly.

**4.** (intermediate) Why does a comet's ion tail point away from the Sun, even when the comet is moving away from it?

??? note "Answer"
    The tail is pushed out by the solar wind, not left behind by the comet's motion, so it always points away from the
    Sun. On the way out the comet travels tail first.

**5.** (intermediate) What injured people at Chelyabinsk in 2013?

??? note "Answer"
    Not the rock itself but the shock wave from its air burst, which arrived minutes after the flash and blew in
    windows; most injuries were cuts from broken glass.

**6.** (intermediate) Which two meteor showers come from Halley's comet?

??? note "Answer"
    The Eta Aquariids in May and the Orionids in October.

**7.** (advanced) Show that a 1-gram meteoroid at 60 km/s has more moving energy than a one-tonne car at 100 km/h.

??? note "Answer"
    Meteoroid: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 0.001 \times 60{,}000^2 = 1.8$ million J. Car: $\tfrac{1}{2} \times 1000 \times
    27.8^2 = 0.39$ million J. The meteoroid has nearly five times as much.

**8.** (expert) Why can no meteor hit the Earth faster than about 72 km/s, or slower than about 11 km/s?

??? note "Answer"
    Nothing bound to the Sun near the Earth moves faster than the Sun's escape speed there, $\sqrt{2} \times 29.8 =
    42.1$ km/s; head-on with the Earth at 29.8 km/s that gives 72 km/s. And anything falling onto the Earth is speeded
    up by the Earth's gravity to at least the escape speed, 11.2 km/s.

## Summary

- A meteor is the streak of a meteoroid burning up in the air; a meteorite is a piece that lands.
- Meteor showers happen when the Earth passes through comet dust; the parallel paths seem to spread from a radiant.
- The Perseids and Geminids are the richest showers. In 2027 the Eta Aquariids and Quadrantids have dark skies, and the
  Perseids of 2028 might give a storm.
- Watch from a dark place, after midnight, lying back, on a night without a bright Moon.
- Chelyabinsk in 2013 and Tunguska in 1908 were air bursts; Tagish Lake in 2000 is a famous Canadian fall.
- A comet is a dirty snowball; its ion tail points straight away from the Sun and its dust tail curves.
- Halley's comet returns in 2061; Tsuchinshan–ATLAS in 2024 was the most recent bright comet.
- Meteors are seen on Mars too, and Opportunity found an iron meteorite there.
- Meteors hit the Earth at between 11.2 and about 72 km/s, faster before dawn.
