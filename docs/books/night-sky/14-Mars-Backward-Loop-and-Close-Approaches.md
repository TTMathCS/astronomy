# 14. Mars's backward loop and close approaches

*Every two years and two months Mars puts on a show. It brightens, stops among the stars, moves backward for a couple
of months, stops again and carries on. At the same time it comes closest to the Earth, but some of these close
approaches are twice as close as others. This chapter follows the loop Mars makes in Leo in early 2027, explains why
it happens and how people tried to explain it, shows why the best views of Mars come every 15 or 17 years, and says
what a telescope can and cannot show. It ends by asking why some loops are bigger than others.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    **You will learn:**

    - what Mars's backward loop looks like, and where to watch it in 2026 and 2027
    - why Mars seems to go backward
    - why some close approaches are twice as close as others
    - how bright and how big Mars looks, and what a telescope shows
    - how Greek, Chinese and European astronomers explained the loop
    - which close approaches made history
    - what loops look like from Mars
    - why the best oppositions come every 15 or 17 years
    - why some loops are longer and wider than others

    **You need:** chapters 8 and 13.

    **Levels:** 14.1 to 14.4 are beginner; 14.5 to 14.7 are intermediate; 14.8 is advanced; 14.9 is expert.

## 14.1 Mars turns back { #loop }

Most of the time Mars drifts slowly **eastward** against the stars, a little each night. But as it nears opposition
(chapter 13, section 13.4) it slows down, stops, and starts to move **westward**, backward. After about 72 days it stops
again and carries on east. This is called **retrograde motion**. Mars is at its brightest in the middle of the loop.

You can watch one this winter. From late 2026 to mid-2027 Mars makes its loop in **Leo**, close to Leo's brightest
star, **Regulus**, which lies almost on the ecliptic.

- About **27 November 2026** Mars passes Regulus heading east, about 2° north of it.
- On **11 January 2027** it stops and turns back.
- On **19 February 2027** it is at opposition, brightest and up all night, passing about 4° north of Regulus a few days
  later.
- On **2 April 2027** it stops again.
- About **16 May 2027** it passes Regulus for the third time, heading east again, only about 1° away.

Watching Mars against Regulus every week or two is the easiest way to see the loop with your own eyes.

## 14.2 Why Mars goes backward { #why }

Mars does not really go backward. The effect comes from our own motion.

The Earth is closer to the Sun than Mars, and goes round faster: once a year, while Mars takes 687 days. About every
780 days the Earth catches up with Mars and passes it on the inside track. As we overtake it, Mars seems to slide
backward against the far-off stars, just as a slower car seems to drift backward when you overtake it on a motorway.
Once we are past, Mars goes on eastward again.

## 14.3 Near and far approaches { #close }

At opposition Mars is at its closest to the Earth, but not every opposition is equally close. The Earth's orbit is
nearly a circle, but Mars's is quite stretched: it is 206.7 million km from the Sun at its nearest, **perihelion**, and
249.3 million km at its farthest, **aphelion**.

## 14.4 How Mars looks { #see }

**Brightness.** Mars changes a great deal in brightness. At a perihelic opposition it reaches magnitude **−2.9**, as
bright as Jupiter ever gets. When it is on the far side
of the Sun near aphelion it fades to **+1.9**, no brighter than an ordinary star. That is a change of almost five
magnitudes, about 80 times in brightness (chapter 4).

**Size.** Even at its closest Mars is tiny: at most about **25 seconds of arc** across, less than a seventieth of the
width of the full Moon. At its farthest it is only 3.5″. In February 2027 it will be about 14″.

**The Mars hoax.** In 2003 an email went round the world claiming that Mars would look as big as the full Moon to the
naked eye. It had garbled a sentence saying that Mars, seen through a telescope magnifying 75 times, would look as big
as the full Moon does to the naked eye.

**Through a telescope.** A small telescope at a good opposition shows Mars as a small reddish disc. With steady air
you may see:

- a white **polar cap**, which grows and shrinks with the Martian seasons;
- dark markings, the largest of them **Syrtis Major**;
- sometimes a haze from a **dust storm**; Mars has the largest dust storms in the solar system.

Because of the Earth's air, even large telescopes on the ground can only make out features about 300 km across when
Mars is at its closest.

## 14.5 Explaining the loop { #models }

The loop was the hardest thing in the sky to explain, and every great model of the planets had to deal with it.

- **Greece.** Apollonius, in the 3rd century BC, explained it with circles on circles: each planet moves on a small
  circle, the **epicycle**, whose centre goes round the Earth on a big one, the **deferent**. Ptolemy refined the idea
  in the *Almagest* (history book, [chapter 7, section 7.9](../history/07-Greece-and-Alexandria.md#mars)).
- **China.** Astronomers watched where Mars paused to start or end its loop. Mars pausing in the Heart, in Scorpius,
  was the worst of omens; in 7 BC a prime minister was forced to take his own life over a report that later
  calculations showed was false (history book, [chapter 4, section 4.8](../history/04-China.md#mars)).
- **Copernicus**, in 1543, put the Sun at the centre and the loop explained itself: it is the Earth overtaking Mars
  (history book, [chapter 13, section 13.9](../history/13-Copernicus-and-Maragha.md#mars)).
- **Kepler** used Tycho Brahe's careful measurements of Mars to show that its orbit is an ellipse, and that Mars speeds
  up as it nears the Sun and slows down as it moves away (history book,
  [chapter 14, section 14.7](../history/14-Tycho-and-Kepler.md#mars)).

??? note "Sources"
    - Apollonius's deferents and epicycles in the 3rd century BC: Wikipedia,
      [Apparent retrograde motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apparent_retrograde_motion) (weaker).
    - Kepler showing that Mars sped up near the Sun and slowed down farther away: Wikipedia,
      [Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars) (weaker).
    - Ptolemy, Mars in the Heart, Copernicus and Kepler: this site's history book, chapters 7, 4, 13 and 14, and their
      sources.

## 14.6 Close approaches in history { #history }

Close approaches have always been the time to study Mars.

- In **1672**, at a close opposition, Giovanni Cassini in Paris and Jean Richer in Cayenne, in South America, measured
  Mars's shift against the stars from two places and worked out the first good scale of the solar system (history
  book, [chapter 16, section 16.4](../history/16-Newton-Halley-and-Romer.md#distance)).
- In **August 1877**, at another close approach, Asaph Hall found the two moons, Deimos and Phobos (history book,
  [chapter 17, section 17.5](../history/17-Herschel-and-the-Canals-of-Mars.md#moons)), and Giovanni Schiaparelli mapped
  the "channels" that later grew into the canal story (history book,
  [chapter 17, section 17.6](../history/17-Herschel-and-the-Canals-of-Mars.md#canali)).
- The **Maya** Dresden Codex has tables following Mars through its 780-day cycle (history book,
  [chapter 11, section 11.3](../history/11-The-Americas.md#dresden)).
- Today spacecraft are launched to Mars in bunches about every 26 months, the same rhythm as the oppositions, because
  that is when the Earth and Mars line up for the trip (history book,
  [chapter 21, section 21.1](../history/21-The-Space-Age-at-Mars.md#first)).

## 14.7 Loops seen from Mars { #mars }

From Mars the same thing happens the other way round.

- The **Earth** is an inner planet for Mars, so it goes backward around inferior conjunction, when it passes between
  Mars and the Sun, every 780 days. That is the very moment Mars is at opposition for us. So when Mars is closest and
  brightest in our sky, the Earth seen from Mars is at its closest too, but it shows Mars its dark night side and is
  lost in the Sun's glare.
- **Jupiter** and **Saturn** make backward loops in the Martian sky too, at each of their oppositions, about every 816
  and 734 days (chapter 13, section 13.8).
- Mars's own moons show something stranger. Both go round Mars eastward, but Phobos goes round faster than Mars turns
  and Deimos slower, so in the Martian sky they seem to move in opposite directions (chapter 6, section 6.8).

??? note "Sources"
    - Inner planets going retrograde by a similar mechanism, and Phobos and Deimos both orbiting eastward but appearing
      to move in opposite directions because Phobos is subsynchronous and Deimos supersynchronous: Wikipedia,
      [Apparent retrograde motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Apparent_retrograde_motion) (weaker).
    - The Earth as an inner planet seen from Mars: chapter 13, section 13.8, and its sources. The rest follows from the
      geometry of section 14.2.

## 14.8 Working it out { #working }

**Why 15 or 17 years.** Oppositions come every 780 days. In that time Mars goes round the Sun $780/687 = 1.135$ times,
so each opposition happens $0.135 \times 360^\circ = 48.7^\circ$ farther round Mars's orbit than the last. To come back
to the same part of the orbit, near perihelion, we need a whole number of these steps that adds up to nearly a full
circle:

- 7 steps: $7 \times 48.7^\circ = 341^\circ$, 19° short, after $7 \times 780 = 5{,}460$ days $= 14.9$ years;
- 8 steps: $8 \times 48.7^\circ = 390^\circ$, 30° over, after $8 \times 780 = 6{,}240$ days $= 17.1$ years.

So perihelic oppositions come 15 or 17 years apart: 2003, 2018, 2035.

**Size.** Mars is 6,779 km across. At distance $d$ it looks $6779/d$ radians wide, and one radian is 206,265″.

- 2003, $d = 55.76$ million km: $6779 / 55{,}760{,}000 \times 206{,}265 = 25.1''$.
- 2027, $d = 101.4$ million km: $13.8''$.
- On the far side of the Sun, $d = 401.4$ million km: $3.5''$.

**Brightness.** Light falls off with the square of the distance, both from the Sun to Mars and from Mars to us
(chapter 4, section 4.10). In 2003 Mars was 1.381 AU from the Sun and 55.8 million km from us; in 2027 it will be
1.665 AU and 101.4 million km. The 2027 opposition is fainter by

$$\left(\frac{101.4}{55.8}\right)^2 \times \left(\frac{1.665}{1.381}\right)^2 = 3.30 \times 1.45 = 4.8 \text{ times},$$

which is $2.5 \log_{10} 4.8 = 1.7$ magnitudes. From −2.9 in 2003, that gives about **−1.2** in 2027.

| | Mars | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Closest the other planet comes | Earth, about 56 million km | Mars, about 56 million km |
| How often | every 780 days | every 780 days |
| Phase of the other planet at closest | Earth is "new": dark side towards Mars | Mars is full |
| Brightest the other planet gets | Earth about −2.5 (as a crescent) | Mars −2.9 |

## 14.9 Why some loops are bigger { #bigger }

If the orbits were circles, every loop of Mars would be the same. Put the Earth on a circle of radius 1 AU going round
in 365.256 days, Mars on a circle of 1.524 AU going round in 686.98 days, and follow the direction from one to the
other day by day. A short program finds that Mars stops 36 days before opposition, goes backward for **73 days**,
covering **16°**, and stops again 36 days after. That matches the "about 72 days" usually quoted.

But real loops differ. Worked out from the true orbits:

| Opposition | Where Mars is | Backward for | Arc |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| July 2018 | near perihelion | 62 days (27 June to 28 August) | 11° |
| February 2027 | near aphelion | 81 days (11 January to 2 April) | 20° |
| September 2035 | near perihelion | 61 days (15 August to 15 October) | 11° |

Why are loops near aphelion longer and wider, even though Mars is farther away? It is a race. How fast Mars seems to
slide backward depends on how much faster the Earth is moving than Mars, divided by the distance between them.

- Near perihelion Mars moves fastest, 26.50 km/s (Kepler's second law, chapter 8, section 8.9). The Earth, at about
  29.8 km/s on average, gains only $29.8 - 26.5 = 3.3$ km/s. At 57.6 million km, Mars slides back at
  $3.3 / 57{,}600{,}000 = 5.7 \times 10^{-8}$ radians a second, about **0.28° a day**.
- Near aphelion Mars moves slowest, 21.97 km/s. The Earth gains $29.8 - 22.0 = 7.8$ km/s, more than twice as much. At
  101.4 million km, Mars slides back at $7.8 / 101{,}400{,}000 = 7.7 \times 10^{-8}$ radians a second, about
  **0.38° a day**.

Near aphelion the Earth overtakes Mars quickly and for longer, so the backward slide is faster and lasts longer, and
the loop is wider. Near perihelion Mars almost keeps pace with us, and the loop is short. (A day is 86,400 seconds,
and a radian is 57.3°. Using the Earth's true speed on each date changes the numbers a little but not the result.)

??? note "Sources"
    - Mars's maximum and minimum orbital speeds of 26.50 and 21.97 km/s, and the Earth's mean of 29.78 km/s: NASA,
      [Mars Fact Sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/marsfact.html).
    - The 2018 and 2027 distances: section 14.3. The loop dates and arcs and the circular model are worked out by us,
      the real ones from NASA JPL's [approximate orbital elements](https://ssd.jpl.nasa.gov/planets/approx_pos.html).
    - The 72 days: Wikipedia, [Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mars) (weaker).

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Which way does Mars usually move against the stars, and which way during its loop?

??? note "Answer"
    Usually eastward; during the loop, westward, for about 72 days around opposition.

**2.** (beginner) Why does Mars seem to go backward?

??? note "Answer"
    The faster Earth overtakes Mars on the inside track, so for a while Mars seems to slide backward against the
    distant stars, like a slower car you pass on a motorway.

**3.** (beginner) Which bright star can you use to watch Mars's loop in 2026 and 2027?

??? note "Answer"
    Regulus in Leo. Mars passes it three times: late November 2026, late February 2027 and mid-May 2027.

**4.** (intermediate) Why was Mars so much closer in 2003 than it will be in 2027?

??? note "Answer"
    In 2003 opposition came when Mars was near perihelion, its closest to the Sun, so it was only 55.8 million km
    away. In 2027 opposition comes near aphelion, so it will be about 101 million km away.

**5.** (intermediate) What was wrong with the 2003 email about Mars?

??? note "Answer"
    It claimed Mars would look as big as the full Moon to the naked eye. In fact it was 25″ across, about a seventieth
    of the Moon's width; it would only look as big as the Moon through a telescope magnifying 75 times.

**6.** (intermediate) How did Ptolemy and Copernicus each explain the loop?

??? note "Answer"
    Ptolemy put Mars on an epicycle, a small circle whose centre goes round the Earth. Copernicus put the Sun at the
    centre, so the loop is just the Earth overtaking Mars.

**7.** (advanced) Show why perihelic oppositions come every 15 or 17 years.

??? note "Answer"
    Each opposition is $0.135 \times 360^\circ = 48.7^\circ$ farther round Mars's orbit. Seven steps make 341° in
    14.9 years and eight make 390° in 17.1 years, the two nearest to a full circle.

**8.** (expert) Why is the loop of 2027 longer than the loop of 2018?

??? note "Answer"
    In 2027 Mars is near aphelion and moving slowly, 22 km/s, so the Earth gains on it by 7.8 km/s. In 2018 it was near
    perihelion, moving at 26.5 km/s, and the Earth gained only 3.3 km/s. Even allowing for the greater distance, Mars
    slides back faster in 2027 (0.38° against 0.28° a day) and for longer, so the loop lasts 81 days instead of 62.

## Summary

- Mars normally drifts east; around opposition it goes west for about 72 days. Its 2027 loop is in Leo, near Regulus.
- The loop is caused by the faster Earth overtaking Mars, not by Mars turning round.
- Oppositions come every 780 days. Near Mars's perihelion they are about 56 million km away, near aphelion about 100.
- Perihelic oppositions come every 15 or 17 years: 2003, 2018, 2035. The opposition of February 2027 is a far one.
- Mars ranges from magnitude −2.9 to +1.9 and from 25″ to 3.5″ across; it never looks as big as the Moon.
- A telescope may show a polar cap, Syrtis Major and dust storms.
- Epicycles, Copernicus and Kepler's ellipse were all attempts to explain the loop.
- From Mars, the Earth loops backward when Mars is at opposition for us.
- Loops near aphelion are longer and wider because the Earth gains on the slow-moving Mars faster.
