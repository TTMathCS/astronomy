# 18. Star charts, planispheres and apps

*A map of the sky turns a confusing sprinkle of lights into something you can find your way around. This chapter
explains how to read a star chart and why its east and west look the wrong way round, how a planisphere shows the sky
for any date and time, what star atlases are, how to use a phone app without ruining your night vision, how people
mapped the sky from the Tang dynasty to Bayer, what a chart for Mars would look like, and ends by working out why a
planisphere's window is egg-shaped.*

    - how to set and use a planisphere

## 18.1 What a star chart shows { #chart }

A star chart is a map of the sky. Most charts show:

- **stars** as dots, bigger for brighter stars, so you can match the chart to what you see (chapter 4);
- **constellation lines** joining the main stars into figures. These stick figures are only a help to the eye; the
  official constellations are areas with boundaries, 88 of them, which many charts also draw (chapter 3);
- a **grid** of right ascension and declination (chapter 17);
- symbols for **clusters, nebulae and galaxies**.

The one surprise is the directions. A map of the Earth is drawn as if you look down on the ground, with north at the
top and east on the right. A star chart is drawn as if you look **up**, so with north at the top, **east is on the
left** (chapter 2, section 2.8).

??? note "Sources"
    - The 88 constellations and their boundaries: chapter 3 and its sources. Magnitudes: chapter 4.
    - East on the left when north is at the top: chapter 2, section 2.8, and its sources.

**centre is the zenith**, straight overhead.

To use one, face a direction, say south, and hold the chart up in front of you with the S at the bottom. The lower
half of the chart is then the sky in front of you, from the horizon up to the zenith. Turn round to face north, turn
the chart so the N is at the bottom, and so on.

An all-sky chart is good for one time and date only. An hour later the whole sky has turned 15° westward (chapter 6).

## 18.3 The planisphere { #planisphere }

A **planisphere** is a star chart that you can set for any date and time. It has two discs on one pivot:

- underneath, a round **star chart** centred on the celestial pole, with the **dates** of the year round its rim;
- on top, a cover with a **window**, with the **hours** of the day round its edge.

Turn the star chart until the date lines up with the time, and the window shows the stars above your horizon at that
moment. The edge of the window is your horizon.

A planisphere is made for one latitude, but it works well enough for a band either side of it, so they are sold in
versions for different latitudes. Buy one made for your own. A planisphere for 50° S will not work in Canada.

It can do more than show the sky now:

- **When does a star rise?** Turn the chart until the star sits on the eastern edge of the window, and read the time
  against the date.
- **When is it highest?** Turn it until the star is on the line from the pivot straight down to the south point.
- **What will be up later?** Turn the chart forward and watch new stars come up over the eastern edge.

The first chart called a planisphere was made in **1624** by Jacob Bartsch, Johannes Kepler's son-in-law. Its
ancestor is the **astrolabe**, which also has a star map turning over a fixed plate (history book,
[chapter 8, section 8.4](../history/08-The-Islamic-World-and-Persia.md#stars)).

## 18.4 Star atlases { #atlas }

An **atlas** is a book of charts, each covering one part of the sky in more detail, showing fainter stars and many
more clusters and galaxies. It is what you use with binoculars or a telescope (chapter 19).

- Johann Bayer's ***Uranometria***, printed in Augsburg in **1603**, was the first atlas to cover the whole sky. It
  had a map for each of Ptolemy's 48 constellations, a plate of the southern stars, and two maps of the two halves of
  the sky. Bayer gave the stars Greek letters, Alpha Orionis for Betelgeuse and so on (chapter 3, section 3.8).
- ***Norton's Star Atlas*** first came out in **1910**. Its charts were drawn by a British schoolmaster, Arthur Philip
  Norton, and it is now in its 20th edition, edited by Ian Ridpath.

??? note "Sources"
    - *Uranometria*: published in Augsburg in 1603, the first atlas to cover the entire celestial sphere: Wikipedia,
      [Uranometria](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Uranometria) (weaker). Its 48 maps of Ptolemaic constellations, a
      plate of southern constellations and two hemisphere plates: Wikipedia,
      [Star chart](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_chart) (weaker). Greek letters: chapter 3, section 3.8.
    - *Norton's Star Atlas*: 16 charts, first published in 1910, drawn by the schoolmaster Arthur Philip Norton
      (1876–1955), now in its 20th edition under Ian Ridpath: Wikipedia,
      [Norton's Star Atlas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Norton%27s_Star_Atlas) (weaker).

## 18.5 Apps and planetarium software { #apps }

A **planetarium program** draws the sky for any time and place on a computer screen. **Stellarium** is free and open
source, for Linux, Windows and macOS. Since version 25.1 of March 2025 it has used stars from the Gaia spacecraft's
catalogue, down to about magnitude 21, far fainter than any eye can see.

Phone apps go further. **Stellarium Mobile**, for Android and iOS, can use the phone's sensors: point the phone at the
sky and it shows what you are looking at and names it. Many other apps do the same.

Apps are wonderful for learning the sky, but they have one big drawback: a bright screen ruins your night vision in
a moment, and it takes 20 to 30 minutes to get it back (chapter 1). So:

- switch on the app's **night mode**, which turns the screen red;
- turn the brightness right down;
- look at the screen as little as you can, and learn to find things by eye.

    - Planetarium software simulating the sky at any time: Wikipedia,
      [Planetarium software](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planetarium_software) (weaker).

## 18.6 Using a chart in the dark { #dark }

- **Use a red torch.** Your rods, which give you night vision, hardly respond to red light, so a dim red light lets
  you read a chart without losing your dark adaptation (chapter 1, section 1.4).
- **Start from bright stars.** Find a bright star or pattern you know, then hop from it to fainter ones, comparing
  chart and sky as you go.
- **Measure with your hand.** At arm's length a fist is about 10°, which is how charts with a grid help you judge
  distances (chapter 2, section 2.6).
- **Turn the chart, not your neck.** Hold it so that the part of the sky you are looking at is the right way up.

??? note "Sources"
    - Rods and red light: chapter 1, section 1.4, and its sources. Measuring with your hand: chapter 2, section 2.6.
    - The rest is common practice.

## 18.7 Old maps of the sky { #history }

- The **Farnese Atlas**, a Roman statue of about AD 150 in Naples, shows the Titan Atlas holding up a globe of the
  sky. It is the oldest known picture of the celestial sphere and the classical constellations.
- The **Dunhuang star chart**, from Tang dynasty China (618–907), was found in the Mogao caves on the Silk Road and is
  now in the British Library. It is the oldest known complete star atlas (history book,
  [chapter 4, section 4.4](../history/04-China.md#mansions)). The same section tells of the **Suzhou planisphere**,
  drawn in 1193 and carved in stone in 1247, which still stands.
- In **1395** Korean astronomers carved a chart of **1,467 stars** in black stone, made for the sky as seen from Korea
  (history book, [chapter 6, section 6.4](../history/06-Korea-and-Japan.md#chart1395)).
- In **1515** Albrecht Dürer made the oldest printed star charts in Europe, as woodcuts in Nuremberg.
- In **1603** came Bayer's *Uranometria* (section 18.4).

??? note "Sources"
    - The Farnese Atlas: about AD 150, the oldest known representation of the constellations, in the National
      Archaeological Museum of Naples: Wikipedia, [Farnese Atlas](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Farnese_Atlas)
      (weaker).
    - The Dunhuang chart as the oldest known complete star atlas, in the British Library: Wikipedia,

## 18.8 A chart for Mars { #mars }

The stars are so far away that from Mars they form the same patterns as from the Earth (chapter 3, section 3.10). A
Martian star chart would show the same constellations. Three things would change.

- **The pivot.** A planisphere for Mars would turn about Mars's north celestial pole, in Cygnus, between Deneb and
  Alpha Cephei (chapter 17, section 17.9), not near Polaris.
- **The zodiac.** The orbits of the Earth and Mars are tilted only 1.85° to each other, so Mars's zodiac is almost the
  same as ours. But on Mars the Sun spends 6 days of each Martian year in Cetus.
- **The wanderers.** The Earth would join the planets, as a "morning star" or "evening star" that never goes far from
  the Sun, like Venus for us. To the eye the Earth and the Moon would look like stars; a telescope would show them as
  crescents.

??? note "Sources"
    - The zodiac of Mars almost the same, the two orbits inclined 1.85° to each other, the Sun spending 6 days in
      Cetus, the Earth as a morning or evening star, and the Earth and Moon starlike to the eye but crescents in a
      telescope: Wikipedia, [Astronomy on Mars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Astronomy_on_Mars) (weaker).
    - Mars's north celestial pole: chapter 17, section 17.9, and chapter 6, section 6.8.

## 18.9 Working it out { #working }

**The date ring.** The star chart must turn once round, 360°, in a year, against the hours on the cover. So one day
is

$$\frac{360°}{365.25} = 0.986° \quad\text{or}\quad \frac{24 \text{ h}}{365.25} = 3.94 \text{ min}.$$

Moving the date on by a day has the same effect as moving the time on by about 4 minutes. A month is about 2 hours, so
the sky you see at 9 pm in late January is the sky at 7 pm in late February and at 11 pm in late December (chapter
9).

**Rising times.** A star rises about 4 minutes earlier each night, 2 hours a month. From Toronto, Betelgeuse rises
around 3:30 pm in late January, in daylight. Two months earlier, in late November, it rises 4 hours later, around
7:30 pm, so Orion climbs into the evening sky as winter begins.

**A planisphere for Mars.** On Mars the sky turns once in 24 h 37 min 23 s, and the year is about 668.6 sols:

$$\frac{360°}{668.6} = 0.538° \text{ a sol}, \qquad
\frac{24 \text{ h } 39 \text{ min } 35 \text{ s}}{668.6} \approx 2 \text{ min } 13 \text{ s a sol}.$$

| | Mars | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Pivot of the planisphere | near Deneb, in Cygnus | near Polaris |
| Days on the date ring | about 669 sols | 365¼ days |
| Turn of the chart per day | 0.538° | 0.986° |
| Stars come round earlier each day by | 2 min 13 s | 3 min 56 s |
| Extra "planet" near the Sun | the Earth | none |

    - Betelgeuse rising: from the formula of chapter 17, section 17.11, its hour angle at rising from Toronto is
      −6 h 29 min. In late January the sidereal time in Toronto is about 5 h at 9 pm, so Betelgeuse (5 h 55 min) rises
      at a sidereal time of about $5{:}55 - 6{:}29 = 23{:}26$, 5 h 34 min of sidereal time (5 h 33 min of clock time)
      before 9 pm, near 3:30 pm. The arithmetic is ours.

## 18.10 Why every flat map is stretched { #projection }

You cannot flatten part of a sphere without stretching it, as anyone who has tried to flatten orange peel knows. Each
chart picks its stretching. Planispheres use one of two ways.

**Polar equidistant.** Each star is placed at a distance from the pivot proportional to its distance from the pole,
$r = 90° - \delta$. The circles of declination are evenly spaced. Along each circle, though, the map is stretched
sideways. A circle of declination δ is really $\cos\delta$ times as long as the equator, but on the map it has radius
proportional to $90° - \delta$. Measured in radians, the sideways stretch is

$$\frac{(90° - \delta) \times \pi / 180°}{\cos\delta}.$$

At δ = +60° that is $0.524 / 0.5 = 1.05$, almost true. At δ = 0° it is $1.571 / 1 = 1.57$. At δ = −30° it is
$2.094 / 0.866 = 2.42$: stars near the southern edge are spread sideways to almost two and a half times their true
spacing.

**Why the window is egg-shaped.** The window's edge is your horizon. From latitude φ, the north point of the horizon
lies φ below the pole, the east and west points lie on the celestial equator, 90° from the pole, and the south point
lies $180° - \varphi$ from the pole. On a polar equidistant chart for Toronto (φ = 43.7°) these are

$$r_N = 43.7°, \qquad r_E = r_W = 90°, \qquad r_S = 136.3°.$$

The south edge of the window is more than three times as far from the pivot as the north edge, so the window is an
egg, narrow end to the north.

**Stereographic.** The astrolabe and some planispheres use $r = 2\tan\left(\dfrac{90° - \delta}{2}\right)$ instead.
This keeps the shapes of small constellations right, and it turns every circle on the sky into a circle on the map, so
the horizon becomes a true circle. But the scale grows towards the edge as
$1/\cos^2\left(\dfrac{90° - \delta}{2}\right)$ compared with the pole: on the celestial equator it is
$1/\cos^2 45° = 2$, so Orion comes out twice as big as a constellation of the same size near the pole.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) On a star chart with north at the top, which side is east, and why?

??? note "Answer"
    The left. A star chart is drawn as if you look up at the sky, not down at the ground.

**2.** (beginner) On an all-sky chart, what do the edge and the centre stand for?

??? note "Answer"
    The edge is your horizon, and the centre is the zenith, straight overhead.

**3.** (beginner) How do you set a planisphere?

??? note "Answer"
    Turn the star chart until the date on its rim lines up with the time on the cover. The window then shows the sky
    at that moment.

**4.** (intermediate) Why should you buy a planisphere made for your own latitude?

??? note "Answer"
    The window is cut to match the horizon at one latitude. At a very different latitude it shows the wrong part of
    the sky.

**5.** (intermediate) What is the danger of using a phone app at night, and how can you lessen it?

??? note "Answer"
    A bright screen spoils your dark adaptation, which takes 20 to 30 minutes to come back. Use the app's red night
    mode, turn the brightness down and look at the screen as little as you can.

**6.** (intermediate) What would be different about a planisphere for Mars?

??? note "Answer"
    It would turn about Mars's pole, in Cygnus near Deneb, its date ring would have about 669 sols, and the Earth would
    appear as an extra planet near the Sun. The constellations would be the same.

**7.** (advanced) The sky you see at 10 pm on 1 March: at about what time is it the same on 1 February?

??? note "Answer"
    A month earlier the stars are about 2 hours later, so at about midnight.

**8.** (expert) On a polar equidistant planisphere, how much are stars at declination −40° stretched sideways?

??? note "Answer"
    $r = 130° = 2.269$ radians, and $\cos(-40°) = 0.766$, so the stretch is $2.269 / 0.766 = 2.96$, almost three times.

## Summary

- A star chart shows stars by brightness, constellation figures and boundaries, and the coordinate grid; east is on the
  left because you look up.
- An all-sky chart is a circle: the edge is the horizon and the centre the zenith.
- A planisphere shows the sky for any date and time; set the date against the hour, and the window's edge is your
  horizon.
- Atlases such as Bayer's *Uranometria* (1603) and *Norton's Star Atlas* (1910) map the sky in detail.
- Apps such as Stellarium name what you point at; use night mode and low brightness.
- Use a red torch, start from bright stars and measure with your hand.
- The Farnese Atlas, the Dunhuang chart and the Korean stone of 1395 are among the oldest maps of the sky.
- A chart for Mars would show the same constellations but pivot near Deneb, with the Earth as a planet.
- Every flat map of the sky is stretched; that is why a planisphere's window is egg-shaped.
