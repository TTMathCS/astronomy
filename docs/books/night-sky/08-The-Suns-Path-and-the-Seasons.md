# 8. The Sun's path and the seasons

*The Sun does not rise in the same place every day. In summer it comes up north of east, climbs high and stays up
long; in winter it rises south of east, crawls low across the south and sets early. This chapter follows the Sun
through the year: its path among the stars, why its height changes, and why that makes summer and winter. It visits
the festivals that mark the year's turning points around the world, and then goes to Mars, where the seasons are
longer and lopsided. It ends with Kepler's law that explains why the seasons are not all the same length.*

## 8.1 The Sun's path among the stars { #path }

We cannot see the stars behind the Sun in daylight, but we can work out where it is among them. The stars that set
just after the Sun in the evening are close to it; a few weeks later they are lost in its glare and others have taken
their place (chapter 6, section 6.5). The Sun is moving against the stars, eastward, about **1° a day**, and in a year
it goes all the way round.

Its path is called the **ecliptic**. It is really the plane of the Earth's orbit, seen from inside: as the Earth goes
round the Sun, we see the Sun move against the stars beyond it.

- The constellations along the ecliptic are the **zodiac**. Thirteen constellations cross it: the twelve familiar
  ones and **Ophiuchus**, the serpent-bearer, which is sometimes called the "13th sign".
- The planets and the Moon are always found near the ecliptic too, because their orbits lie in nearly the same plane.
  The Moon's orbit is tilted only 5.1° to it.

The twelve signs of the zodiac began in Babylon (this site's history book,
[chapter 2, section 2.9](../history/02-Mesopotamia.md#zodiac)).

## 8.2 Why there are seasons { #tilt }

The Earth's axis is tilted by about **23.4°** to its orbit, and it keeps pointing the same way in space, towards
Polaris, all year round. So for half of the year the northern hemisphere leans towards the Sun, and for the other half
it leans away.

When your hemisphere leans towards the Sun, two things happen:

- **The Sun climbs higher.** High sunlight falls on the ground more directly, so each square metre gets more energy.
  Low sunlight is spread out over more ground, like a torch beam shone at a slant.
- **The days are longer.** The Sun is up for more hours, so there is more time to heat the ground and less night to
  cool it.

Together these make summer. Six months later the same hemisphere leans away, the Sun is low and the days are short:
winter. That is why the two hemispheres always have **opposite seasons**: Christmas comes in summer in Australia.

**It is not the distance.** Many people think summer comes when the Earth is closest to the Sun. But the Earth is
closest, at about 147.1 million km, in **early January**, in the middle of the northern winter, and farthest, at about
152.1 million km, in **early July**. The difference changes the sunlight by only about 7% (section 8.8 shows the
working), far less than the effect of the tilt.

The hottest weather also comes some weeks after the longest day, because land and sea take time to warm up. In the
northern hemisphere June, July and August are the warmest months.

## 8.3 Solstices and equinoxes { #turning }

Four moments divide the Sun's year.

| When | Name | What happens |
| --- | --- | --- |
| around 20 March | March equinox | the Sun crosses the celestial equator going north |
| 20 to 22 June | June solstice | the Sun is furthest north; longest day in the north, shortest in the south |
| around 23 September | September equinox | the Sun crosses the celestial equator going south |
| 20 to 22 December | December solstice | the Sun is furthest south; shortest day in the north, longest in the south |

**Equinox** comes from the Latin for "equal night". On the equinoxes, day and night are about equal everywhere on
Earth. They are not exactly equal: the Sun is a disc, not a point, and the air bends its light up over the horizon
(chapter 2, section 2.11), so the day is a little longer than the night.

**Solstice** comes from the Latin *sol*, "Sun", and *sistere*, "to stand still". Around the solstices the Sun stops
moving north or south from day to day, pauses, and turns back.

Because "summer solstice" means June in one hemisphere and December in the other, astronomers prefer to say the
**June solstice** and the **December solstice**.

??? note "Sources"
    - The solstices around 20 to 22 June and 20 to 22 December, longest and shortest days, "June solstice" and
      "December solstice", and *sol* and *sistere*: Wikipedia, [Solstice](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Solstice)
      (weaker).
    - The equinoxes around 20 March and 23 September, *aequinoctium*, day and night approximately equal but not exactly,
      because of the Sun's size and refraction: Wikipedia, [Equinox](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equinox) (weaker).

- **Around 21 June** the Sun rises in the north-east, about 34° north of east, climbs to 68° at noon, and sets in the
  north-west. It is up for about 15½ hours.
- **At the equinoxes** it rises due east, reaches 45° at noon, and sets due west, after 12 hours.
- **Around 21 December** it rises in the south-east, reaches only 22° at noon, and is up for only about 8½ hours.

So over the year the sunrise point swings along the horizon, from north-east to south-east and back. At each end it
slows, stops and turns, which is just what *solstice* means. Watch the sunrise from the same spot for a year, against
a hill or a building, and you will see it.

People have done exactly that for thousands of years. Newgrange in Ireland lets the winter-solstice sunrise shine
down its passage, Stonehenge lines up with the solstice Sun, and at Taosi in China and Chankillo in Peru rows of
slots and towers marked the sunrise point through the year. The history book tells their stories
([chapter 1, sections 1.5](../history/01-Before-Writing.md#solstice-buildings),
[1.7](../history/01-Before-Writing.md#taosi) and [1.8](../history/01-Before-Writing.md#chankillo)).

## 8.5 The tropics and the polar circles { #tropics }

Two pairs of lines on the globe come straight from the tilt.

**The tropics.** The **Tropic of Cancer**, at about **23.44°N**, is the furthest north where the Sun can ever be
straight overhead; it happens there once a year, at the June solstice. The **Tropic of Capricorn**, at 23.44°S, is its
partner in the south. Between them, the point where the Sun is overhead moves north and south through the year, so
anywhere in the tropics has the Sun overhead at noon on two days a year.

- In Hawaiʻi, the only US state where this happens, those days are called **Lahaina Noon**: shadows vanish under
  people's feet.
- When the overhead point passes over the Kaaba in Mecca, shadows point directly away from it, so the direction of
  prayer, the **qibla**, can be found from shadows.

**The polar circles.** The **Arctic Circle**, at about **66.56°N**, is 90° minus the tilt. North of it, the Sun stays
up for 24 hours on at least one day in summer and stays down on at least one day in winter. The air lifts the Sun a
little, so in practice the midnight Sun can be seen from about 65.7°N (chapter 7, section 7.2). The **Antarctic
Circle** is its partner in the south.

## 8.6 The turning points around the world { #festivals }

The solstices and equinoxes are the year's natural landmarks, and festivals all over the world are tied to them.

- **Nowruz**, "New Day", the Iranian New Year, falls on the March equinox, around 21 March. Its roots lie in
  Zoroastrianism, and it has been kept across West and Central Asia, the Caucasus, the Balkans and South Asia for more
  than 3,000 years. In the 11th century **Omar Khayyam** reformed the Iranian calendar to
  fix the new year at the equinox.
- **Dongzhi**, the winter solstice, is one of the 24 solar terms of the Chinese calendar (history book,
  [chapter 4, section 4.5](../history/04-China.md#calendar)). It is kept in China, as *Tōji* in Japan, *Dongji* in
  Korea and *Đông chí* in Vietnam. In the old way of thinking, the days lengthening again after it meant that the
  positive force, *yang*, was growing.
- **Inti Raymi**, the festival of Inti, the Sun, was the greatest festival of the Inca Empire. It marked the June
  solstice, which in the southern hemisphere is the **winter** solstice and the shortest day, and the Inca new year.
  The Spanish banned it; a reconstruction was first staged in 1944, and it is now the biggest celebration of the year
  in Cusco (history book, [chapter 11, section 11.5](../history/11-The-Americas.md#inca)).
- **Midsummer** is traditionally celebrated in June, when the northern hemisphere gets the most direct sunlight.
- At **Chichén Itzá** in Mexico, near the equinoxes the afternoon Sun throws a row of shadows down the stairs of the
  great pyramid, like a serpent crawling down. Thousands come to watch, but nobody knows whether the builders planned
  it (history book,
  [chapter 11, section 11.9](../history/11-The-Americas.md#claims)).

## 8.7 Seasons on Mars { #mars }

Mars is tilted by **25.2°**, a little more than the Earth, so it has seasons too: spring, summer, autumn and winter in
each hemisphere. But two things make them very different from ours.

**They are long.** A Mars year lasts 687 Earth days, or 668.6 sols, so each season lasts about six of our months.

**They are lopsided.** Mars's orbit is far more stretched than the Earth's. At its closest Mars is 206.7 million km
from the Sun, at its farthest 249.3 million km, so it gets about **45% more sunlight** at its closest than at its
farthest; the Earth's difference is only about 7%. Mars comes closest just before the southern summer begins, so the
south has short, hot summers and long, cold winters, while the north has milder ones. The seasons are not even the
same length:

| Season (north / south) | Length on Mars | Length on Earth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| northern spring / southern autumn | 193.3 sols | 92.8 days |
| northern summer / southern winter | 178.6 sols | 93.6 days |
| northern autumn / southern spring | 142.7 sols | 89.8 days |
| northern winter / southern summer | 154.0 sols | 89.0 days |

**The air freezes.** Mars's air is 95% carbon dioxide. In the long polar night of each winter, so much of it
freezes onto the polar cap as dry ice that **25 to 30%** of the whole atmosphere is laid down at one pole or the other
each year: some 3 to 4 trillion tonnes. The northern cap gains a layer of dry ice about a metre thick in winter; the
southern cap keeps a permanent dry-ice cover about 8 m thick.

**Dust storms.** Dust storms are most common when Mars is near the Sun, in the southern spring and summer, and they
can sometimes spread round the whole planet.

The tilt of Mars also wanders far more than ours. A few million years ago it was **45°**, and over millions of years
it may swing anywhere between 0° and 60°. The Earth's tilt is held steady by the Moon.

## 8.8 Working it out { #working }

Write $\varphi$ for your latitude and $\delta$ for the Sun's declination, which runs from $+23.44^\circ$ at the June
solstice through $0^\circ$ at the equinoxes to $-23.44^\circ$ at the December solstice.

**Noon height.** As for any star crossing the meridian (chapter 6, section 6.9):

$$h_{\text{noon}} = 90^\circ - \varphi + \delta.$$

At 45°N: $90 - 45 + 23.44 = 68.4^\circ$ in June, $45^\circ$ at the equinoxes, $90 - 45 - 23.44 = 21.6^\circ$ in
December.

**Sunlight on the ground.** Sunlight arriving at height $h$ spreads over more ground the lower it is. The energy per
square metre of level ground is proportional to $\sin h$. At noon at 45°N:

$$\frac{\sin 68.4^\circ}{\sin 21.6^\circ} = \frac{0.930}{0.368} \approx 2.5.$$

So the June noon Sun delivers two and a half times as much power to each square metre as the December noon Sun, for
more hours.

**Day length.** The Sun sets when its hour angle reaches $H_0$, where

$$\cos H_0 = -\tan \varphi \, \tan \delta, \qquad \text{day length} = \frac{2 H_0}{15^\circ} \text{ hours}.$$

At 45°N in June, $\cos H_0 = -1 \times 0.4335$, so $H_0 = 115.7^\circ$ and the day is $231.4 / 15 = 15.4$ hours. In
December it is $24 - 15.4 = 8.6$ hours. If $\tan \varphi \tan \delta$ is more than 1, there is no sunset at all: that
happens when $\varphi > 90^\circ - 23.44^\circ = 66.56^\circ$, the Arctic Circle.

**Where it rises.** Measured from north, the sunrise direction $A$ obeys $\cos A = \sin \delta / \cos \varphi$. At 45°N
in June, $\cos A = 0.398 / 0.707 = 0.563$, so $A = 55.8^\circ$, which is $90 - 55.8 = 34.2^\circ$ north of east.

**Distance.** Sunlight falls off as the square of distance (chapter 4, section 4.10), so the ratio of sunlight at the
closest and farthest points is $(r_{\text{far}}/r_{\text{near}})^2$.

| | Mars | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Tilt | 25.19° | 23.44° |
| Tropics at | 25.2°N and S | 23.4°N and S |
| Polar circles at | $90 - 25.2 = 64.8^\circ$ | $90 - 23.4 = 66.6^\circ$ |
| Closest and farthest from the Sun | 206.65 and 249.26 million km | 147.10 and 152.10 million km |
| Sunlight, closest ÷ farthest | $(249.26/206.65)^2 = 1.45$ | $(152.10/147.10)^2 = 1.07$ |
| Year | 668.6 sols | 365.25 days |
| Longest and shortest season | 193.3 and 142.7 sols | 93.6 and 89.0 days |

## 8.9 Why the seasons are not equal { #kepler }

If the Earth went round the Sun in a perfect circle at a steady speed, each season, from equinox to solstice and so on,
would last exactly a quarter of a year, 91.3 days. They do not:

| Northern season | Days |
| --- | --- |
| spring | 92.8 |
| summer | 93.6 |
| autumn | 89.8 |
| winter | 89.0 |

The northern summer is more than four and a half days longer than the northern winter. Why?

The answer is **Kepler's second law**: a line from the Sun to a planet sweeps out **equal areas in equal times**.
When the planet is close to the Sun the line is short, so to sweep the same area it must move faster. When the planet
is far away, it moves more slowly. Each season covers the same angle round the Sun, a quarter of a turn, but the
planet takes longer to cover it when it is far away.

The Earth is farthest from the Sun in early July, so it moves most slowly during the northern summer, and that season
is the longest. It is closest in early January, so it moves fastest and the northern winter is the shortest.

How much faster? Equal areas means the planet's speed of turning round the Sun goes as $1/r^2$. So the ratio of
turning speeds at closest and farthest is the same as the sunlight ratio in section 8.8: 1.07 for the Earth, 1.45
for Mars. On the Earth, 7% makes a few days' difference between seasons. On Mars, 45% makes a huge one: northern spring
lasts 193.3 sols, northern autumn only 142.7, a difference of 51 sols, almost two Earth months. Mars must be farthest
from the Sun during northern spring and summer, and closest during northern autumn and winter, the southern spring and
summer, just as section 8.7 said.

There is a neat twist. Kepler found this law by studying **Mars**. Working on Tycho Brahe's careful observations of
the planet, he had the equal-areas rule by late 1602, and in his *Astronomia nova* of 1609 he showed that Mars's orbit
is an ellipse (history book, [chapter 14, sections 14.7](../history/14-Tycho-and-Kepler.md#mars) and
[14.9](../history/14-Tycho-and-Kepler.md#onmars)). The lopsided Martian seasons are the same law at work.

Today the Earth's closest approach falls in the northern winter, softening it a little, and the northern summer is
long. This will not last for ever: the dates of closest and farthest approach slowly move through the seasons, going
all the way round in 22,000 to 26,000 years. In about half that time the northern summer will be the short season.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) What causes the seasons?

??? note "Answer"
    The tilt of the Earth's axis, about 23.4°. When your hemisphere leans towards the Sun, the Sun is higher and the
    days are longer: summer. When it leans away, the Sun is low and the days are short: winter.

**2.** (beginner) Is the Earth closest to the Sun in summer?

??? note "Answer"
    No. It is closest in early January, during the northern winter. The change in distance alters the sunlight by only
    about 7%.

**3.** (beginner) Where does the Sun rise on the equinoxes?

??? note "Answer"
    Due east, everywhere on Earth, and it sets due west.

**4.** (intermediate) Why can the Sun be straight overhead in Hawaiʻi but never in Canada?

??? note "Answer"
    The Sun can only be overhead between the tropics, 23.44°N and 23.44°S. Hawaiʻi is inside the tropics; Canada is
    far north of the Tropic of Cancer.

**5.** (intermediate) Why is Inti Raymi, held in June, a winter festival?

??? note "Answer"
    The Inca Empire was in the southern hemisphere, where the June solstice is the shortest day of the year: the
    winter solstice.

**6.** (intermediate) Why are the southern summers on Mars hotter than the northern ones?

??? note "Answer"
    Mars's orbit is quite stretched, and Mars is closest to the Sun around the start of the southern summer, when it
    gets about 45% more sunlight than at its farthest.

**7.** (advanced) At latitude 30°N, how high is the Sun at noon on the June and December solstices, and how many times
more power per square metre does the June noon Sun deliver?

??? note "Answer"
    June: $90 - 30 + 23.44 = 83.4^\circ$. December: $90 - 30 - 23.44 = 36.6^\circ$. Ratio
    $\sin 83.4^\circ / \sin 36.6^\circ = 0.993 / 0.596 \approx 1.7$.

**8.** (expert) Why is northern spring on Mars about 51 sols longer than northern autumn?

??? note "Answer"
    By Kepler's second law, a planet moves fastest when closest to the Sun and slowest when farthest. Each season
    covers a quarter of the orbit, so a season spent far from the Sun lasts longer. Mars is far from the Sun in
    northern spring and close in northern autumn, and its orbit is stretched enough that its turning speed changes by
    45%, making the difference large.

## Summary

- The Sun moves east along the ecliptic about 1° a day, through the zodiac; the planets and Moon stay near it.
- The seasons come from the 23.4° tilt of the Earth's axis, which keeps its direction in space, not from changes in
  distance.
- The equinoxes, around 20 March and 23 September, bring roughly equal day and night; the solstices, around 21 June
  and 21 December, bring the longest and shortest days.
- At 45°N the noon Sun climbs from 22° in December to 68° in June, and the day grows from 8½ to 15½ hours.
- The Sun can be overhead only between the tropics; it can stay up all day only inside the polar circles.
- Nowruz, Dongzhi, Inti Raymi and midsummer mark the year's turning points.
- Mars's seasons are about twice as long and lopsided: southern summers are short and hot, and a quarter of the air
  freezes onto the winter pole.
- Kepler's second law makes the seasons unequal: 93.6 days for northern summer against 89.0 for winter on the Earth,
  and a difference of 51 sols on Mars.
