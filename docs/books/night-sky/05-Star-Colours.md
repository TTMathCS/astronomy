# 5. Star colours

*At first all stars look white. Look longer and you will see that Betelgeuse is orange, Rigel blue-white and Antares a
deep red, a colour so like the planet Mars that its name means "rival of Ares", the Greek Mars. This chapter shows
that a star's colour is a thermometer: red stars are cool and blue ones hot. It follows the women at Harvard who
sorted hundreds of thousands of stars into seven classes, the red stars that peoples around the world named, and an
old puzzle about Sirius, which some ancient writers called red. It ends with a question that sounds simple: why are
there no green stars?*

## 5.1 Colours you can see { #look }

Only the brightest stars show their colour to the naked eye, but the ones that do are worth looking for.

## 5.2 Colour means temperature { #hot }

Heat a piece of metal in a flame and it first glows **red**, then **orange**, and at very high temperatures **white**.
A dimmed light bulb looks redder than a bright one for the same reason: its filament is cooler. Before the metal
glows at all it is already giving out light we cannot see, infrared, which you can feel as warmth on your skin.

Stars work the same way. Anything hot gives out light spread over a range of wavelengths, and the hotter it is, the
shorter the wavelength where most of its light comes out. A cool star puts out most of its light at the red end of
the spectrum and beyond, so it looks orange or red. A hot star puts out most of its light at the blue end and beyond,
so it looks blue-white. **A star's colour is its thermometer.**

## 5.3 What colour is the Sun? { #sun }

Ask someone to draw the Sun and they will probably make it yellow. But seen from space, or high in a clear sky, the
Sun is **white**. It only looks yellow, orange or red when it is low, because its light then passes through so much
air (chapter 4, section 4.4). Very rarely, near the horizon, it can even look green or blue.

Even so, some cultures picture the Sun as yellow and others as red.

The Yolŋu of northern Australia, whose canoe you met in chapter 3, say that the Sun-woman, **Walu**, lights a small
fire each morning, which we see as the dawn. She paints herself with red ochre, and some of it spills onto the clouds,
making the sunrise. When she comes down from the sky at the end of her journey, more ochre rubs off onto the clouds,
making the sunset.

??? note "Sources"
    - The Sun's colour as white from space or high in the sky; yellow, red, orange or magenta when low, and rarely
      green or blue; cultures picturing it as yellow or red: Wikipedia, [Sun](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun)
      (weaker).
    - Walu, the dawn fire and the red ochre: Wikipedia,
      [Australian Aboriginal astronomy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Australian_Aboriginal_astronomy) (weaker).

## 5.4 Seeing colours for yourself { #try }

Your eyes have two kinds of light sensors (chapter 1, section 1.4). The **rods** work in dim light but see no colour.
The **cones** see colour but need more light. So a faint star always looks white, and only bright stars wake the cones
enough to show their colour. That is why the colour of Betelgeuse is easy to see and the colour of a magnitude 4 star
is not.

- **Compare in pairs.** Colour is easiest to judge side by side: Betelgeuse with Rigel, or Antares with a white star
  near it.
- **Look at Albireo.** To the naked eye this star in Cygnus is a single star of magnitude 3. Even a small telescope
  splits it into two stars 35″ apart, one amber and one blue-green. It is one of the best colour contrasts in the sky.

??? note "Sources"
    - Rods and cones: chapter 1, section 1.4, and its sources.
    - Albireo: magnitude 3 to the naked eye, split by a low-power telescope into an amber star (3.1) and a blue-green
      star (5.1), 35″ apart, one of the best contrasting doubles: Wikipedia,
      [Albireo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Albireo) (weaker).

## 5.5 O, B, A, F, G, K, M { #classes }

Spread a star's light into a rainbow, a **spectrum**, and you see dark lines across it, made by the gases in the star's
outer layers. In the 1860s and 1870s the astronomer **Angelo Secchi** sorted stars into a few groups by their
lines. The big step came at Harvard College Observatory, where a team of women, the **Harvard Computers**, studied
the spectra of hundreds of thousands of stars on photographic plates.

- **Williamina Fleming**, born in Dundee, Scotland, had been a maid in the house of the observatory's director,
  Edward Pickering. She classified more than 10,000 stars for the first edition of the Henry Draper Catalogue, and
  with Pickering sorted them into 17 classes by the strength of their hydrogen lines. She also found the Horsehead
  Nebula in 1888.
- **Antonia Maury**, in 1897, moved the stars now called B ahead of those called A.
- **Annie Jump Cannon**, nearly deaf after scarlet fever, dropped most of the letters in 1901 and kept **O, B, A, F, G,
  K, M** in that order. She classified about 350,000 stars by hand, more than anyone else in history.

Nobody yet knew what the order meant. In the 1920s the Indian physicist **Meghnad Saha** worked out how the atoms in a
hot gas lose their electrons as the temperature rises. Using his ideas, **Cecilia Payne** at Harvard showed in her
1925 thesis that the sequence is a **sequence of temperature**, from the hottest O stars to the coolest M stars. She
also found that stars are made mostly of hydrogen and helium, a result that leading astronomers, including Henry
Norris Russell, at first refused to accept.

## 5.6 Measuring colour { #index }

Judging colour by eye is personal, so astronomers measure it. They take a star's magnitude twice, once through a blue
filter (**B**) and once through a green-yellow filter (**V**), and subtract. The
result is the **colour index**, **B−V**.

- Blue stars are brighter in B, so B is the smaller number and B−V is small or negative.
- Red stars are brighter in V, so B−V is large.
- Vega is the zero point, with B−V = 0.

| Star | B−V | Temperature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Rigel | −0.03 | 12,100 K |
| Vega | 0.00 | about 8,900 to 10,100 K |
| The Sun | 0.656 | 5,772 K |
| Arcturus | 1.23 | 4,286 K |
| Aldebaran | 1.44 | 3,900 K |
| Antares | 1.83 | 3,660 K |
| Betelgeuse | 1.85 | 3,779 K |

One of the bluest stars, Theta Muscae, has −0.41. One of the reddest, the carbon star R Leporis, has +5.74. Because
B−V comes from a difference of two measurements, it does not depend on how far away the star is, only on its colour,
unless dust in space reddens the light on the way.

## 5.7 Red stars around the world { #red }

A red star stands out, and peoples everywhere named the reddest ones for their colour. **Antares** collected more
such names than any other.

- **Greece:** *Antares* means "rival of Ares", because its colour is so like the planet Mars (Ares to the Greeks).
- **Babylon:** one of its possible names was *Kakkab Bir*, "the Vermilion Star".
- **Egypt:** it was "the red one of the prow".
- **China:** it was sometimes called *Huǒxīng*, the "fiery star", for its reddish look.
- **Hawaiʻi:** *Hōkūʻula*, "red star", a name that could also mean Aldebaran or Mars.
- **Aotearoa:** to the Māori it is *Rēhua*, the chief of all the stars, father of Puanga (Rigel).
- **Victoria, Australia:** the Wotjobaluk knew it as Djuit, son of Arcturus, with the stars on either side as his
  wives.

**Mars** itself, the "Red Planet", got colour names too. The Greeks called it *Pyroeis* as well as Ares. In
Babylon it was the planet of Nergal, the god of war and destruction. In East Asia it is traditionally the **fire star**,
火星, written with the same characters as the Chinese name for Antares.

## 5.8 Was Sirius red? { #sirius }

Sirius is plainly white. Yet around AD 150 Ptolemy listed it as **reddish**, along with Betelgeuse, Antares,
Aldebaran, Arcturus and Pollux, all of which really are orange or red. The Roman writer Seneca even said it was a
deeper red than Mars. The puzzle was first raised by an English amateur astronomer, Thomas Barker, at the Royal
Society in 1760, and it has been argued over ever since.

**The case for a change.** If stars can change in brightness (chapter 4), perhaps they can change colour. In 1985
two German astronomers pointed to an 8th-century manuscript of Gregory of Tours, which calls a bright star *rubeola*,
"reddish", and argued it was Sirius. They suggested that Sirius's companion, the white dwarf Sirius B, had been a red
giant only 2,000 years ago.

**The case against.** Not every ancient observer agreed. The poet Manilius in the 1st century called Sirius
"sea-blue", and Chinese records from the 2nd century BC to the 7th century AD consistently describe it as white.
Other scholars think Gregory meant Arcturus. And the physics does not work:

- A star does not turn from a red giant into a white dwarf in a few thousand years; the timescale is far longer, and
  such a change would have left a glowing cloud around Sirius, which is not there.
- A cloud of dust in space thick enough to make Sirius look red would also have dimmed it by several magnitudes.

**The best-supported answer: the horizon.** Near the horizon, the air reddens every star, just as it reddens the
setting Sun, and twinkling makes its colour flash (chapter 4, section 4.4). In several Mediterranean cultures, how
Sirius looked when it first rose at dawn each year, and when it set, was thought to carry astrological meaning, so it
was watched and recorded close to the horizon more than any other star. The Chinese, without that tradition, recorded
it as white. Some ancient writers may also have used "red" as a poetic word for bad luck.

## 5.9 Colours on Mars { #mars }

Mars is red because its surface is covered in iron oxide, the same substance as rust, along with an iron mineral
called goethite. Up close the ground can look butterscotch, golden, brown or tan.

The sky of Mars is the opposite of ours.

- **By day** our sky is blue, because the gas molecules of the air scatter blue light more than red. On Mars that
  effect is weak. The sky is coloured instead by fine dust full of iron oxide, which absorbs blue
  light and scatters red, orange and yellow. So the daytime sky of Mars is **butterscotch**.
- **At sunset** our sky turns red near the Sun. On Mars the sky turns rose-coloured, but close to the setting Sun it
  is **blue**.

At night, though, the stars high in the sky of Mars show the same colours as on Earth. Betelgeuse is just as orange
and Rigel just as blue-white, because the colour comes from the star itself.

## 5.10 Working it out { #working }

**Wien's law.** A body at temperature $T$ gives out the most light, per nanometre of wavelength, at

$$\lambda_{\text{peak}} = \frac{b}{T}, \qquad b \approx 2{,}898{,}000 \text{ nm K}.$$

| Star | Temperature | Peak wavelength | Where that is |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Rigel | 12,100 K | 239 nm | ultraviolet |
| Sirius | 9,940 K | 292 nm | ultraviolet |
| The Sun | 5,772 K | 502 nm | middle of the visible |
| Arcturus | 4,286 K | 676 nm | red |
| Betelgeuse | 3,779 K | 767 nm | just past red, infrared |
| Antares | 3,660 K | 792 nm | infrared |

Planets glow too, but they are so cool that their light is all far infrared. Your skin, at about 300 K, glows most at
about 10 micrometres.

| | Mars | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Average surface temperature | 214 K | 288 K |
| Peak of its own glow, $b/T$ | 13.5 μm | 10.1 μm |
| Colour of its daytime sky | butterscotch | blue |
| Sky near the setting Sun | blue | red |

**From colour to temperature.** If we treat a star as a perfect black body, its B−V index gives its temperature.
Ballesteros' formula is

$$T = 4600 \text{ K} \left( \frac{1}{0.92\,(B-V) + 1.7} + \frac{1}{0.92\,(B-V) + 0.62} \right).$$

For the Sun, $B-V = 0.656$, so $0.92 \times 0.656 = 0.604$ and

$$T = 4600 \left( \frac{1}{2.304} + \frac{1}{1.224} \right) = 4600\,(0.434 + 0.817) \approx 5{,}760 \text{ K},$$

within 0.3% of the true 5,772 K. It works almost as well for Arcturus (4,250 K against 4,286 K) and Aldebaran (3,890 K
against 3,900 K). But for Betelgeuse it gives 3,330 K, against 3,779 K, and for Rigel 10,500 K against 12,100 K. Real
stars are not perfect black bodies, and the B and V filters suit stars of middle temperatures best.

      [Wien's displacement law](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Wien%27s_displacement_law) (weaker).
    - Ballesteros' formula, and B−V suiting mid-range objects: Wikipedia,
      [Color index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_index) (weaker).
    - Average temperatures of Mars (about 214 K) and Earth (288 K): NASA,

## 5.11 Why are there no green stars? { #green }

The Sun's light, measured per nanometre, peaks at about 500 nm, which is green. Yet nobody has ever seen a green Sun
high in the sky, or a green star. Leaving aside some white dwarfs with metal-rich atmospheres and tricks of contrast
in dim light, there are no green, cyan, indigo or violet stars. Why not?

**A peak is not a colour.** A glowing body does not give out one wavelength. It gives out a broad spread, and at the
Sun's temperature that spread covers the whole visible band almost evenly (figure A). Our eyes add it all up, and red,
green and blue together look **white**. A star only looks coloured when the spread is tilted: tipped towards red for
a cool star, towards blue for a hot one. As the temperature rises, the colour of a glowing body moves along a fixed
path: deep red, orange, yellowish, white, then bluish white. Colour scientists call this path the **Planckian locus**.
It never passes through green, because no temperature makes a glowing body give out much more green than red and
blue together. Even a star whose light did peak in green would look white to us.

**The peak depends on how you measure.** Even "peaks in green" is less solid than it sounds. If you measure the Sun's
light per unit of frequency instead of per nanometre, its peak moves to about 883 nm, in the near infrared. The same
light, sorted a different way, has a different peak. A peak wavelength is a property of a graph, not of what the eye
sees.

**Then why is Albireo B called blue-green?** Look again at section 5.4. The fainter star of Albireo is a blue star,
seen in a telescope right beside an amber one. Colours judged side by side in dim light can fool the eye, and that
kind of colour-contrast effect is what the rule leaves out.

So the colours of the stars run only from red to blue-white, through orange, yellow and white, and that one line of
colours is the temperature scale of figure B.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Name a red or orange star and a blue-white star that you can see in Orion.

??? note "Answer"
    Betelgeuse, at Orion's shoulder, is orange-red. Rigel, at his foot, is blue-white.

**2.** (beginner) Which is hotter, a red star or a blue star?

??? note "Answer"
    The blue star. Red stars are the coolest, about 2,300 to 3,900 K for class M, and blue stars the hottest, more
    than 10,000 K.

**3.** (beginner) Why do faint stars all look white?

??? note "Answer"
    In dim light only the rods of the eye work, and they see no colour. Only bright stars give enough light for the
    cones, which see colour.

**4.** (intermediate) What does the order O, B, A, F, G, K, M stand for, and who showed it?

??? note "Answer"
    It is a sequence of temperature, from the hottest stars (O) to the coolest (M). Annie Jump Cannon fixed the
    order in 1901; Cecilia Payne showed in 1925 that it follows temperature, using Meghnad Saha's theory.

**5.** (intermediate) Why does Antares have the name it has?

??? note "Answer"
    *Antares* means "rival of Ares" in Greek. Its red colour is so like the planet Mars, Ares to the Greeks, that it
    was named as Mars's rival.

**6.** (intermediate) Give one reason why ancient writers might have called Sirius red when it is white.

??? note "Answer"
    For example: they watched it most closely when it was low on the horizon at its first rising of the year, when the
    air reddens it and twinkling makes it flash colours; or they used "red" as a poetic word for bad luck.

**7.** (advanced) Arcturus is at 4,286 K. Where does its light peak? And where does a fire at 1,500 K peak?

??? note "Answer"
    $2{,}898{,}000 / 4{,}286 \approx 676$ nm, in the red. $2{,}898{,}000 / 1{,}500 \approx 1{,}930$ nm, about 2,000 nm,
    deep in the infrared, which is why most of a fire's heat is felt rather than seen.

**8.** (expert) The Sun's light peaks at about 500 nm, which is green. Why is the Sun not green?

??? note "Answer"
    The Sun gives out a broad spread of wavelengths that covers the whole visible band almost evenly, and the eye
    sees that mixture as white. No temperature gives much more green than red and blue together, so the colours of
    glowing bodies go from red through white to blue-white without ever passing through green. And the peak itself
    moves, to about 883 nm, if the light is measured per unit of frequency.

## Summary

- Only bright stars show colour to the eye; Betelgeuse, Rigel and Antares are the best to start with.
- A star's colour tells its temperature: red stars are cool, below about 4,000 K, and blue ones hot.
- The Sun is white; it looks yellow, orange or red only when it is low.
- The classes O, B, A, F, G, K, M run from hottest to coolest; the Sun is G2, and most ordinary stars are red dwarfs
  of class M.
- Fleming, Maury and Cannon built the classes at Harvard; Saha's theory and Payne's thesis showed they follow
  temperature.
- B−V measures colour: small or negative for blue stars, large for red ones.
- Antares and Mars have red names in many languages; in Chinese both can be called "fire star".
- Sirius was probably called red because it was watched low on the horizon, not because it changed.
- Mars has a butterscotch sky by day and blue sunsets, but its stars have the same colours as ours.
- There are no green stars: a glowing body's colours run from red through white to blue-white.
