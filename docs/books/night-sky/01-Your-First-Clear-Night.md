# 1. Your first clear night

*You need nothing to start: no telescope, no app, not even a star chart. Pick a clear night, find the darkest place
you can, and wait. Your eyes slowly change, and stars you could not see at first begin to appear. This chapter
explains what happens in your eyes while you wait, how many stars you can hope to see, and what you can find on a
first night, from the Milky Way to a galaxy 2.5 million light-years away. It ends on Mars, which has the same stars
in a very different sky.*

## 1.1 A clear night and a dark place { #night }

The night sky is free, and the only tools you need are your eyes. What matters most is where and when you look.

- **Darkness.** Street lights, shop signs and lit windows hide the faint stars. Get as far from them as you can, or
  at least stand where no light shines straight into your eyes.
- **No Moon, or a thin one.** A bright Moon washes out faint things. The best nights for the Milky Way are the few
  days around new Moon, when the Moon is not up at night.
- **Clear, dry air.** Haze and damp air dim the sky even when there are no clouds.
- **The right time.** The sky turns through the night and through the year, so different stars are up at different
  times. Chapter 6 explains why.

Then give it time. The first few minutes are the worst: you will see a handful of bright stars and perhaps a planet.
Stay out for half an hour and the sky fills up. The rest of this chapter explains why.

## 1.2 Waiting for the dark: twilight { #twilight }

The sky does not go dark the moment the Sun sets. For a while sunlight still lights the air high above you, and that
air lights the sky and the ground. This glow is **twilight**, and astronomers split it into three parts by how far
the Sun has sunk below the horizon:

| Part | Sun below the horizon | What you see |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Civil twilight | 0° to 6° | The horizon is clear; the brightest stars and planets appear |
| Nautical twilight | 6° to 12° | The outlines of things on the ground, but no detail |
| Astronomical twilight | 12° to 18° | More and more stars; the faintest, about magnitude 6, appear as it ends |

When the Sun is 18° down, the light from the sky is nearly zero, and evening twilight becomes **night**. In the
morning the same steps happen in reverse. Faint, spread-out things such as galaxies can need the full dark of night.

**How long it takes.** The Sun moves across the sky at 15° an hour. Near the equator it goes straight down, so civil
twilight can last as little as 24 minutes. Farther from the equator it sets at a slant, and twilight lasts longer:
at Greenwich in England, at 51.5° north, civil twilight lasts from 33 to 48 minutes, depending on the time of year.
Beyond about 48°34′ north or south, near midsummer, the Sun never gets 18° below the horizon, and the sky never
becomes fully dark at all.

## 1.3 Letting your eyes adapt { #adapt }

Walk out of a bright room into the night and at first you see almost nothing. Wait, and your eyes slowly become
more sensitive. This is **dark adaptation**. Coming in from bright sunlight, the eye takes about **20 to 30 minutes**
to adapt fully, and by then it is **10,000 to 1,000,000 times** more sensitive than in full daylight.

Two kinds of light-sensing cells in the back of the eye do the work. **Cones** see colour and fine detail in
bright light. **Rods** see only in shades of grey, but they work in very dim light. The cones adapt first, in
**9 to 10 minutes**. The rods are slower: they need **30 to 45 minutes**, and by another measure they reach their
very best only after about **two hours**.

The rods are slow because of the pigment that catches light inside them, **rhodopsin**. Light breaks it apart, and
it has to be rebuilt before the rod can work again. That is why a burst of bright light spoils your night vision.
Dark adaptation is also much quicker and deeper in young people than in old.

## 1.4 Rods, cones and red light { #rods }

**How many?** The rods far outnumber the cones, but the counts depend on the source. One gives about 92 million rods
and 4.6 million cones in a human eye; another gives about 120 million rods and 6 to 7 million cones. Either way there
are about 20 rods for every cone. A single rod can respond to a single particle of light, a **photon**, and is about
100 times more sensitive to one than a cone is.

**Why red?** Light comes in colours, each with its own wavelength, measured in nanometres (nm). Rods are most
sensitive to blue-green light of about 500 nm and hardly respond at all to red light beyond about 640 nm. So a dim
red light lets your cones read a chart while your rods stay dark-adapted. Astronomers use red torches for this, and
the same trick lights the control rooms of submarines and the cockpits of aircraft at night.

**Colours fade at dusk.** As the light fades and the rods take over, the eye becomes most sensitive to bluer light
than it is in daylight. Reds look darker: a red flower bright against green leaves at noon can look almost black
against them at dusk. This is the **Purkinje effect**. It matters to astronomers too: when people judge how bright a
star is by comparing it with stars of other colours, a red star can fool them.

**Bilberry jam: does it help?** In both world wars British airmen were known to eat a lot of bilberry jam, believing
it helped them see on night missions. Bilberries are rich in the blue, red and purple plant pigments called
anthocyanins, and the evidence about them is mixed:

- *For:* in a double-blind test by Nakaishi and others, people given anthocyanins from black currants adapted to the
  dark faster. Only one of the three doses made a clear difference on its own.
- *Against:* in two double-blind tests by Kalt and others in 2014, anthocyanins from blueberries made no difference to
  how long dark adaptation took.

The honest answer is that the evidence does not settle it. Waiting half an hour in the dark works for everyone.

## 1.5 Looking to one side { #averted }

Here is a strange trick. To see a very faint star, do not look straight at it. Look a little to one side while you
keep your attention on it, and it often appears. This is **averted vision**.

It works because of where the rods are. The very centre of your view falls on a small spot at the back of the eye,
the **fovea**, which is packed with cones and has almost no rods. So at night the middle of your view is the least
sensitive part. The rods are most crowded about **20 degrees** from the centre of view.

The trick is old. Aristotle seems to have described it while looking at the star cluster now called M41, and
generations of sailors standing lookout learned it to spot faint lights of ships and shores at night. With a
telescope it works the same way: look towards the edge of the view. Moving the telescope gently back and forth also
helps, because the eye notices moving things more easily than still ones.

Not everyone agrees on the reason. Some researchers point out that the eye is not most sensitive to stars at 20
degrees from the centre, where the rods are most crowded, so rod numbers alone may not explain it. Few careful studies
have measured the effect at all.

## 1.6 How many stars can you see? { #count }

Astronomers measure how bright a star looks by its **magnitude**. The scale runs backwards: the bigger the number,
the fainter the star. Sirius, the brightest star in the night sky, is magnitude −1.46, and on a clear night the
faintest stars you can see are about **magnitude 6**. Chapter 4 explains the scale properly.

How faint you can see depends most of all on where you stand. From dark country places about 200 km from big cities
the limit is about magnitude 7, and some skilled observers at very remote sites see nearly to magnitude 8. In a
city the limit can be as bright as magnitude 2. New York gives a good example:

From the middle of Manhattan you may see only about 15 stars at once. Under a perfectly dark sky you might see about
2,500. Light pollution takes away more than 99 in every 100 of the stars.

**The whole sky.** Estimates differ. Another source puts the number of stars brighter than magnitude 6 in a typical
dark sky at about 5,600. The Yale Bright Star Catalogue, which lists every star down to magnitude 6.5, holds
9,110 objects.

**The Bortle scale.** In February 2001 John E. Bortle published a nine-step scale in the
magazine *Sky & Telescope* to rate how dark a place is, from what you can see there:

| Class | Name | Faintest star by eye |
| --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | Excellent dark-sky site | 7.6 to 8.0 |
| 3 | Rural sky | 6.6 to 7.0 |
| 5 | Suburban sky | 5.6 to 6.0 |
| 8 | City sky | 4.1 to 4.5 |
| 9 | Inner-city sky | 4.0 or brighter |

It is widely used, but it has critics. A 2014 study suggests Bortle may have been too hopeful about how faint a
typical observer can see, even under the darkest skies, and his descriptions lean on sights of the northern
hemisphere's sky.

## 1.7 The Milky Way, a river of many names { #milky-way }

On a dark night, away from the Moon, you will see a faint band of light about 30° wide arching across the sky. This is
the **Milky Way**. It is our own galaxy seen from inside: a flat disc of stars so far away that the eye cannot pick
them out one by one, so they blur into a glow. In 1610 Galileo turned a telescope on it and saw that it was made of a
huge number of faint stars. The dark patches inside it, such as the Great Rift and the Coalsack, are clouds of dust
that block the light of the stars behind.

The band is always there, but for part of the year it is up in the daytime and lost in sunlight. Light pollution
hides it too: maps of the brightness of the night sky show that **more than a third of the people on Earth** cannot see
the Milky Way from where they live.

Every people who saw it gave it a name and a story:

- **China, Korea, Japan and Vietnam:** the Silver River, or River of Heaven. The Weaver Girl (the star Vega) and the
  Cowherd (Altair) are kept apart on its two banks and meet once a year, on the seventh day of the seventh lunar
  month, on a bridge of crows and magpies. The festival is Qixi in China and Tanabata in Japan.
- **India:** *Akasaganga*, the Ganges of the sky.
- **Southern Africa:** the San say that long ago there were no stars, until a girl threw the embers of a fire into
  the sky and made the Milky Way.
- **Aboriginal Australia:** the Kaurna of the Adelaide Plains see a river in the sky world, *Wodliparri*, with
  campfires along it. The Arrernte of Central Australia also see a river, with the camps of two peoples on either
  side. Others see an emu in the dark clouds along it.
- **Aotearoa (New Zealand):** to the Māori it is the canoe of Tama-rereti, with the Southern Cross and the Pointers as
  its anchor and rope.
- **The Americas:** the Cherokee call it "where the dog ran", after a dog that spilled stolen cornmeal as it fled.
  The Inca saw a river, the *Mayu*, and named animals in its dark clouds.
- **Finland and Estonia:** the Pathway of the Birds.
- **Greece:** milk spilled from the goddess Hera. The Greek name for it, *Galaxias*, comes from *gala*, milk.

The history book tells more of these stories: the [Emu in the Sky](../history/01-Before-Writing.md#australia), the
[Inca's dark animals](../history/11-The-Americas.md#inca), the
[stars of southern Africa](../history/10-Africa.md#south) and the
[oldest living sky in Australia](../history/12-Oceania-and-Australia.md#aboriginal).

## 1.8 The farthest things your eyes can see { #farthest }

Every star you can see without a telescope belongs to our own galaxy. But on a dark night your eyes can reach beyond
it.

**The Andromeda Galaxy.** On a moonless night far from city lights, look for a faint smudge in the constellation
Andromeda, which is easiest to find from its neighbours Cassiopeia and Pegasus. It is a whole galaxy of stars, about
**2.5 million light-years** away. A light-year is the distance light travels in a year, so the light you see left it
2.5 million years ago. Under most skies it is one of the most distant things the eye can see, because it is so big.
Around the year 964 the Persian astronomer **Abd al-Rahman al-Sufi** described it in his *Book of Fixed Stars* as a
"small cloud".

**The Magellanic Clouds.** From the southern hemisphere you can see two glowing patches that look like pieces broken
off the Milky Way. They are two small galaxies: the Large Magellanic Cloud, about 163,000 light-years away, and the
Small Magellanic Cloud, about 206,000. They cannot be seen from far in the north. Europeans named them after
Ferdinand Magellan: Antonio Pigafetta, who sailed on his voyage round the world in 1519 to 1522, described them.
The name did not catch on among astronomers until John Herschel used it in 1847. Arabic writers of the 9th and 10th
centuries quoted reports of bright white stars below Canopus, unseen in Iraq. These may have been the Clouds, but
they are now thought to be other stars south of Canopus.

## 1.9 A first night on Mars { #mars }

Imagine your first clear night on Mars. The stars are the same ones you know, but almost everything else is
different.

- **The day sky hides the stars.** Mars's air is thin, about 6.36 millibars at the ground against 1014 on Earth,
  but it is very dusty. The dust scatters so much light that the daytime sky is bright and no stars show. By day the
  sky is the colour of butterscotch.
- **Sunset is blue.** Around sunrise and sunset the sky is rose-coloured, but near the Sun it turns blue, the opposite of
  Earth.
- **Twilight is long.** Dust high in the air keeps scattering light after the Sun has set. Twilight on Mars can last
  up to two hours, much longer than on Earth.
- **A different pole star.** Mars's axis points to a different part of the sky. The two top stars of the Northern
  Cross, Sadr and Deneb, point to the north pole of Mars's sky, and the star Kappa Velorum lies a couple of degrees
  from its south pole.
- **The Earth as a double star.** From Mars, the Earth shines as a bright point with the Moon as a fainter
  companion beside it. When it is farthest from the Sun in Mars's sky, the Earth shines at about magnitude −2.5,
  about as bright as Jupiter looks from the Earth.
- **Two small moons.** Phobos, the larger and closer of the two, looks about 4.1 arcminutes across, small next to our
  Moon's 31, but big enough to see its shape.

Your eyes would still need their half hour in the dark.

## 1.10 Working it out { #working }

**How much does the pupil help?** The pupil, the black hole in the middle of your eye, widens in the dark. Its width
can range from about 1.5 mm to 8 mm. The light it lets in goes with its area, so with the square of its width:

$$\left(\frac{8}{1.5}\right)^2 = 5.33^2 \approx 28.$$

So a wide-open pupil lets in at most about 28 times more light. But the eye becomes 10,000 to 1,000,000 times more
sensitive in the dark (section 1.3). The rest must come from the rods and their rhodopsin:

$$\frac{10{,}000}{28} \approx 360 \qquad \text{to} \qquad \frac{1{,}000{,}000}{28} \approx 36{,}000 \text{ times}.$$

Most of your night vision comes from chemistry, not from the pupil.

**How long is twilight at the equator?** The Sun crosses the sky at 360° in 24 hours, which is $360 / 24 = 15°$ an
hour. Near the equator it goes straight down, so it reaches 6° below the horizon in $6 / 15 = 0.4$ hours, or 24
minutes: the end of civil twilight. Full night, at 18°, comes $18 / 15 = 1.2$ hours, or 72 minutes, after sunset.

**City against country.** A perfectly dark sky shows about 2,500 stars at once, Midtown Manhattan about 15:
$2{,}500 / 15 \approx 170$ times as many.

**Mars and the Earth.**

| | Mars | Earth |
|---|---|---|
| Distance from the Sun | 227.96 million km (1.524 AU) | 149.60 million km (1 AU) |
| Sunlight arriving | 586.2 W/m² | 1361.0 W/m² |
| A day, from noon to noon | 24.66 hours | 24.00 hours |
| How fast the Sun moves across the sky | $360 / 24.66 = 14.6°$ an hour | $360 / 24 = 15°$ an hour |
| Air pressure at the ground | 6.36 millibars | 1014 millibars |
| Moons | 2 | 1 |

Sunlight on Mars is $586.2 / 1361.0 = 0.43$ of the Earth's. That is just what the distance predicts: light spreads
out with the square of the distance, and $1 / 1.524^2 = 0.43$.

If twilight depended only on how fast the Sun sinks, Mars would be like the Earth: at 14.6° an hour, the Sun would
reach 18° below the horizon in $18 / 14.6 = 1.23$ hours, or 74 minutes, if it went straight down. Yet Martian twilight
can last up to two hours. The extra time comes from the dust high in the air, which goes on scattering sunlight long
after the Sun has set.

## 1.11 How few photons can you see? { #photons }

How dim a light can a fully dark-adapted eye see? In 1942 three scientists, Hecht, Shlaer and Pirenne, set
out to find the smallest number of photons a person can see. They took great care:

- The observers sat in the dark for forty minutes first, to adapt fully.
- The flash fell 20 degrees from the centre of view, where the rods are most crowded (section 1.5).
- It was a tiny spot, 10 arcminutes across, so that it fell only on a small patch of rods.
- Its colour was blue-green, 510 nm, near the rods' best wavelength (section 1.4).
- It lasted a thousandth of a second.

They counted a flash as seen when the observer saw it 60 per cent of the time. Allowing for the light lost in the eye
before it reached the rods, they estimated that **5 to 14 rods**, out of about 500 in the patch, each absorbed one
photon. A handful of photons was enough to see.

Later work pushed this further. In 1972 Sakitt let observers rate each flash instead of just saying yes or
no, and accepted more false alarms. Flashes in which about 6 rods each caught a photon looked "very bright", 3 looked
"a dim light", and two observers rated 2 photons as "slightly doubtful". One observer seemed to sense a single photon,
but was very unsure. So a single rod responds to a single photon (section 1.4), and only a few such signals arriving
together are enough to see a flash.

This is why the eye, after half an hour of patience, can see a galaxy whose light set out 2.5 million years ago.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Name three things that help you see more stars.

??? note "Answer"
    Any three of: a dark place away from lights, no Moon or only a thin one, clear and dry air, and waiting half an
    hour for your eyes to adapt.

**2.** (beginner) How far below the horizon must the Sun be before it is fully night?

??? note "Answer"
    18°. Between 12° and 18° is astronomical twilight; after that the light from the sky is nearly zero.

**3.** (beginner) Why do astronomers use red torches?

??? note "Answer"
    The rods, which give us night vision, hardly respond to red light beyond about 640 nm. A dim red light lets the
    cones read a chart while the rods stay dark-adapted.

**4.** (intermediate) Why can you sometimes see a faint star better by looking a little to one side of it?

??? note "Answer"
    The centre of your view falls on the fovea, which has almost no rods. Looking to one side puts the star's light
    on a part of the eye with many rods, which are most crowded about 20 degrees from the centre. Some researchers
    think rod numbers alone do not explain all of the effect.

**5.** (intermediate) About how many stars can you see at once from the New York suburbs, and from a perfectly dark
sky?

??? note "Answer"
    About 250 from the suburbs, where the faintest stars are about magnitude 4, and about ten times as many, 2,500,
    under a perfectly dark sky.

**6.** (intermediate) Give two ways in which a night on Mars differs from a night on the Earth.

??? note "Answer"
    Any two of: twilight lasts up to two hours; the sky near the setting Sun is blue; Sadr and Deneb point to the
    north pole of the sky; the Earth and Moon shine as a double star; there are two small moons,
    and Phobos looks only about 4.1 arcminutes across.

**7.** (advanced) The pupil widens from 1.5 mm to 8 mm in the dark. How many times more light does it let in? Why
can this not explain all of night vision?

??? note "Answer"
    $(8 / 1.5)^2 \approx 28$ times. The eye becomes 10,000 to 1,000,000 times more sensitive in the dark, so the rods
    and their rhodopsin must give the remaining factor of about 360 to 36,000.

**8.** (expert) In the 1942 experiment, why did Hecht, Shlaer and Pirenne aim the flash 20 degrees from the centre
of view, use blue-green light of 510 nm and keep the spot very small? What did they find?

??? note "Answer"
    20 degrees from the centre is where the rods are most crowded; 510 nm is close to the rods' best wavelength; and a
    tiny spot falls on a small patch of rods. Each choice made the eye as sensitive as possible. They found that a
    flash was seen when only 5 to 14 rods, out of about 500 in the patch, each absorbed a single photon.

## Summary

- The sky is fully dark only when the Sun is 18° below the horizon; twilight before that comes in three parts.
- The eye takes 20 to 30 minutes to adapt to the dark and becomes 10,000 to 1,000,000 times more sensitive.
- Rods see in dim light but not in colour, and hardly respond to red, so red lights keep night vision.
- Whether bilberries help night vision is still argued over.
- Looking a little to one side shows fainter stars, though the reason is debated.
- About 15 stars show from Midtown Manhattan and about 2,500 under a perfectly dark sky.
- The Milky Way has names and stories all over the world; more than a third of people can no longer see it.
- The Andromeda Galaxy, 2.5 million light-years away, and the Magellanic Clouds are visible to the eye.
- On Mars the day sky hides the stars, twilight lasts up to two hours, and the Earth is a bright double star.
- A dark-adapted eye can see a flash when only 5 to 14 rods each catch one photon.
