# 10. The Moon's phases

*The Moon is the one thing in the night sky that changes shape. Over a month it grows from a thin evening crescent to a
full disc and shrinks again to a crescent before dawn. This chapter follows it through the month, explains why it
changes, and shows when and where to look for each phase. It looks at the faint glow on the Moon's dark side, the face
the Moon always keeps turned towards us, how the Moon looks from the southern hemisphere, and the festivals held at
full Moon. Then it goes to Mars, whose two small moons run through their phases in hours. It ends with a puzzle: why
the full Moon is far more than twice as bright as the half Moon.*

## 10.1 A month of Moon-watching { #month }

Start a few days after new Moon, just after sunset. Look low in the west and you will find a thin **crescent**. Look
again the next evening at the same time: it is higher, further east, and a little fatter. Each evening it moves about
12° further east, a bit more than the width of your fist at arm's length (chapter 2, section 2.6).

- About a week after new Moon it is half lit: **first quarter**.
- Over the next week it bulges past half: **gibbous**, which means bulging.
- About two weeks after new Moon it is **full**, rising in the east as the Sun sets.
- Then it shrinks again, rising later each night. About three weeks after new Moon it is half lit again: **last
  quarter**, seen in the morning.
- Last comes a thin crescent low in the east before dawn. Then for a day or two you cannot see it at all: it is
  **new**.

A growing Moon is **waxing**; a shrinking one is **waning**. The whole cycle, from one new Moon to the next, takes
**29.5 days** on average. The four main phases are new, first quarter, full and last quarter; between them come the
waxing crescent, waxing gibbous, waning gibbous and waning crescent.

"Quarter" is a little confusing: at first and last quarter the Moon looks like a half circle. The name means that it
has gone a quarter or three quarters of the way round its cycle, not that a quarter of it is lit.

## 10.2 Why the Moon has phases { #why }

The Moon makes no light of its own. It shines by reflecting sunlight, and at every moment the half facing the Sun is
lit and the other half is dark, just as on the Earth one half is in daytime and the other in night.

As the Moon goes round the Earth, we see that lit half from different angles.

*Figure A. The Moon is always half lit. What changes is how much of the lit half faces us.*

- **New Moon:** the Moon is between us and the Sun. Its lit half faces away from us, and we see only the dark side,
  lost in the Sun's glare.
- **First quarter:** it has gone a quarter of the way round. We look at it side on, and see half of the lit half.
- **Full Moon:** it is on the far side of the Earth from the Sun, and its whole lit half faces us.
- **Last quarter:** three quarters of the way round, side on again, lit on the other side.

You can try this with a lamp and a ball. In a dark room with one lamp on the far side, hold the ball at arm's length
and turn slowly round on the spot. You will see the ball go through every phase.

**It is not the Earth's shadow.** Many people think the phases happen because the Earth's shadow falls across the Moon.
It does not: the dark part of the Moon is simply its own night side. When the Earth's shadow does fall on the Moon, it
is a lunar eclipse, which can happen only at full Moon (chapter 12).

Chinese astronomers worked this out long ago. Zhang Heng wrote that the Sun is like fire and the Moon like water, which
reflects the fire's light; centuries later Shen Kuo compared the Moon to a ball of silver that shines only where light
falls on it (history book, [chapter 4, section 4.6](../history/04-China.md#cosmos)).

## 10.3 When to look { #when }

15° an hour (chapter 9), so a Moon that is a certain angle east of the Sun rises that much later: 1 hour for every
15°.

- **First quarter** is 90° east of the Sun. It rises around noon, is highest around sunset and sets around midnight.
  So it is well placed in the evening.
- **Full Moon** is opposite the Sun. It rises at sunset, is highest around midnight and sets at sunrise: it is up all
  night.
- **Last quarter** is 90° west of the Sun. It rises around midnight and is highest around sunrise, so it is a morning
  Moon, often still up in the blue sky after breakfast.

This means the Moon is often in the **daytime** sky. For much of the month you can find it in daylight if you
know where to look: in the afternoon before first quarter and full, in the morning after full.

On average the Moon rises about 50 minutes later each day. Chapter 11 shows why that average hides a lot of variety.

## 10.4 Earthshine { #earthshine }

Look at a thin crescent Moon in a dark sky. Often the "dark" part is not quite dark: you can see the whole round Moon
glowing faintly, with the bright crescent along one edge. This is **earthshine**. Sunlight bounces off the Earth onto
the Moon, and then back to us. It is sometimes called "the old Moon in the new Moon's arms".

Why is it strongest at crescent? Stand on the Moon and look at the Earth, and you will see that **the Earth has phases
too, opposite to the Moon's**. When we see a thin crescent Moon, the Moon sees a nearly full Earth. The Earth is much
wider than the Moon and reflects about three times as much of the light that falls on it, so a full Earth is very
bright: it lights the Moon's night side some 43 times more brightly than the full Moon lights a night on Earth.

**Leonardo da Vinci** sketched and described earthshine in the 16th century. He thought the light came from sunlight
reflected off the Earth's oceans. We now know that the Earth's clouds reflect far more.

## 10.5 The face we always see { #face }

The Man in the Moon, the Jade Rabbit: whatever picture you see in the Moon's dark patches, it is always the same
one. The Moon always keeps the **same face** turned towards the Earth.

That does not mean it does not spin. It turns once on its axis in exactly the time it takes to go once round the
Earth, so the same side always faces us. This is called **tidal locking**. Pluto and its moon Charon have gone
further: each keeps the same face towards the other.

The match is not perfect. The Moon's orbit is not quite a circle, so its turning sometimes runs ahead of its trip
round the Earth and sometimes behind, and it seems to rock slightly from side to side. Its axis is also tilted by
about 6.7°, so we see a little way over its north and south edges in turn. These small wobbles are called
**libration**. Thanks to them we can see, over time, about **59%** of the Moon's surface, a little more than half.

**The far side is not the dark side.** The side we never see is often called "the dark side of the Moon", but it gets
as much sunlight as the near side: every place on the Moon has about two weeks of day and two weeks of night. At new
Moon, when we see the near side dark, the far side is in full sunlight. "Dark" only ever meant "unknown". No one saw
the far side until the Soviet probe **Luna 3** photographed it in October 1959. The first spacecraft to land there was
China's **Chang'e 4**, on 3 January 2019, and in June 2024 **Chang'e 6** brought back the first rock samples from it.

## 10.6 From the south and the tropics { #south }

The diagrams in most books are drawn for the northern hemisphere. From Australia, South Africa, Argentina or New
Zealand, the Moon looks **upside down**, turned through 180°, because you are looking at it from the other way up.

- In the north, a waxing Moon is lit on the **right** and a waning Moon on the **left**.
- In the south it is the other way round: a waxing Moon is lit on the **left** (figure B, second row).

Wherever you are, the lit side always faces the Sun. That is the rule that never fails.

Near the equator the crescent lies on its back. The line between light and dark looks nearly level in the evening and
morning, and the crescent opens upward like a bowl or a smile. This is the shape most often seen from the tropics,
because a crescent is easiest to see when the Sun has just gone below the horizon, so the Moon is above it and its lit
side points down.

??? note "Sources"
    - The Southern Hemisphere view rotated 180°, the terminator horizontal near the equator in the morning and evening,
      the crescent opening upward when the Moon is above the Sun and this being the orientation most often seen from
      the tropics: Wikipedia, [Lunar phase](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Lunar_phase) (weaker).

## 10.7 Full Moons around the world { #world }

The full Moon lights up the night, and all over the world it marks holidays and names the seasons.

- **The Mid-Autumn Festival**, also called the Moon Festival, is held on the 15th day of the 8th month of the Chinese
  calendar, between 7 September and 8 October. A Chinese month begins at new Moon, so the 15th day falls at full Moon.
  It is a time for family and friends to gather, and people eat **mooncakes**, pastries filled with sweet-bean or
  lotus-seed paste.
- **Vesak**, or Buddha Day, is kept by Buddhists across South and Southeast Asia, Tibet and Mongolia. In the South Asian
  tradition it falls on the full Moon day of the month of Vaisakha, and marks the birth, enlightenment and death of the
  Buddha.
- In English the full Moons of autumn are called the **harvest Moon** and the **hunter's Moon**. Many old calendars
  named their months after the Moons, before solar calendars broke the link.
- A **blue moon**, in the modern sense, is the second full Moon in a calendar month. The idea was started by the
  amateur astronomer James Hugh Pruett in 1946. It happens 7 times in every 19 years, so "once in a blue moon" means
  every two or three years.

The new crescent matters too: the months of the Islamic calendar begin with its first sighting (chapter 9,
section 9.5).

## 10.8 Phases seen from Mars { #mars }

Mars has two moons, and both have phases, but neither looks much like ours.

**Phobos**, the inner moon, is at most **0.20°** across, a third of the width of our Moon, and only 0.14° when it is
low on the horizon. It goes round Mars in 7 hours 39 minutes, and its phases run through their whole cycle in almost
exactly the same time. It crosses the sky in 4 hours 15 minutes or less (chapter 6, section 6.8), so on a single
crossing you could watch it change through more than half its cycle of phases.

**Deimos**, the outer moon, is so small and far that it is only 2.5′ across, a twelfth of the width of our Moon, and
looks almost like a star. At full it is about as bright as Venus is from Earth, and at quarter about as bright as Vega.
You would need a small telescope to see its phases, which take 1.26 days.

**The Earth from Mars** is an inner planet, so it behaves like Venus does for us: a "morning star" or an "evening star",
never far from the Sun. To the eye the Earth and the Moon are points of light; through a telescope both are crescents,
and you could watch the Moon go round the Earth. When they are furthest apart the two are easy to tell apart with the
naked eye, a true double planet.

## 10.9 Working it out { #working }

**Two kinds of month.** Against the stars the Moon goes round the Earth in **27.32 days**, the sidereal month. But in
that time the Earth has moved along its orbit, so the Moon has to go a bit further to line up with the Sun again: the
synodic month, new Moon to new Moon, is **29.53 days**. As with the two kinds of day in chapter 9:

$$\frac{1}{29.53} = \frac{1}{27.32} - \frac{1}{365.25}.$$

Check: $0.03660 - 0.00274 = 0.03386$, and $1 / 0.03386 = 29.53$.

**Daily motion.** Against the stars the Moon moves $360^\circ / 27.32 = 13.2^\circ$ a day. Compared with the Sun it
gains $360^\circ / 29.53 = 12.2^\circ$ a day. At 4 minutes per degree, it rises on average
$12.2 \times 4 \approx 49$ minutes later each day.

**How much is lit.** If the Moon is at an angle $E$ from the Sun as seen from Earth, the fraction of its disc that is
lit is very nearly

$$f = \frac{1 - \cos E}{2}.$$

New Moon, $E = 0^\circ$: $f = 0$. First quarter, $E = 90^\circ$: $f = 0.5$. Full, $E = 180^\circ$: $f = 1$. A crescent
45° from the Sun: $f = (1 - 0.707)/2 = 0.15$.

**When it rises.** At 15° an hour, a Moon $E$ degrees east of the Sun rises about $E/15$ hours after the Sun: first
quarter $90/15 = 6$ hours after sunrise, around noon.

**Phobos on one crossing.** A crossing takes up to 4 h 15 min, and its phases take 7 h 39.5 min (0.3191 days):
$4.25 / 7.66 = 0.55$, more than half the cycle.

**The Earth from Mars.** The Earth's orbit is 1.000 AU from the Sun and Mars's 1.524 AU. Taking both as circles, the
widest angle between the Earth and the Sun seen from Mars is $\arcsin(1/1.524) = 41^\circ$. So from Mars the Earth is
never seen more than $41/15 \approx 2.7$ hours after sunset or before sunrise.

## 10.10 Why the full Moon is so bright { #surge }

At first quarter we see half of the Moon's disc lit. At full we see all of it. So you might expect the full Moon to be
twice as bright as the half Moon. It is far brighter than that: moonlight at full Moon is much more than twice the
moonlight at first or last quarter.

Part of the reason is the angle of the light. At quarter, sunlight strikes much of the visible lit half at a slant, so
those parts look dimmer, just as the low Sun warms the ground less (chapter 8). But even allowing for that, the full
Moon is brighter than a simple model of a smooth, evenly reflecting ball predicts. This extra brightening when the Sun
is directly behind you is called the **opposition surge**, or the Seeliger effect, after the astronomer Hugo von
Seeliger, who first explained it in 1887.

**Shadow hiding.** The Moon's surface is rough on every scale, from mountains to dust grains, and every bump casts a
small shadow. At quarter phase, with sunlight coming from the side, huge numbers of tiny shadows darken the surface.
At full Moon the Sun is directly behind us, so each shadow falls straight behind the bump that casts it, hidden from
our view. All the shadows vanish together, and the whole surface brightens.

**Coherent backscatter.** A second effect may add to it. When the grains on a surface are about as big as a
wavelength of light, and spaced more widely than that, light waves scattered straight back towards the source can
reinforce one another. This brightens the surface further, but only within a narrow range of angles round full.

The same opposition surge is seen on Mars, and on the particles of Saturn's rings, where it comes from the shadows on
the ring particles vanishing. You can see something like it on Earth. On a dewy lawn, with the Sun low behind you, a
bright glow called the **heiligenschein** surrounds the shadow of your head. The dew drops act as tiny lenses, so the
cause is different, but again the light is thrown straight back towards the Sun.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) In which part of the sky, and at what time of day, would you look for a thin waxing crescent?

??? note "Answer"
    Low in the west just after sunset.

**2.** (beginner) Is the dark part of a crescent Moon the Earth's shadow?

??? note "Answer"
    No. It is the Moon's own night side, the half facing away from the Sun. The Earth's shadow falls on the Moon only
    in a lunar eclipse.

**3.** (beginner) Why does the full Moon rise at sunset?

??? note "Answer"
    At full Moon the Moon is opposite the Sun in the sky, so as the Sun sets in the west, the Moon rises in the east.

**4.** (intermediate) What is earthshine, and why is it brightest when the Moon is a thin crescent?

??? note "Answer"
    It is sunlight reflected from the Earth onto the Moon's night side. When we see a thin crescent Moon, the Moon sees
    a nearly full Earth, which lights it most strongly.

**5.** (intermediate) Why do we always see the same face of the Moon, and is the far side always dark?

??? note "Answer"
    The Moon spins once for each trip round the Earth, so the same side always faces us. The far side is not dark: it
    gets about two weeks of sunlight each month, and is fully lit at new Moon.

**6.** (intermediate) From Australia, which side of a waxing crescent is lit?

??? note "Answer"
    The left side. In the southern hemisphere the Moon looks turned through 180° compared with the northern view.

**7.** (advanced) The Moon is 60° east of the Sun. Roughly how much of its disc is lit, and about how long after the Sun
does it rise?

??? note "Answer"
    $f = (1 - \cos 60^\circ)/2 = (1 - 0.5)/2 = 0.25$, a quarter of the disc. It rises about $60/15 = 4$ hours after the
    Sun.

**8.** (expert) Give two reasons why the full Moon is much more than twice as bright as the half Moon.

??? note "Answer"
    At full Moon the Sun is directly behind us, so the shadows of all the bumps and grains on the rough surface are
    hidden behind them (shadow hiding), and light scattered among the grains reinforces itself in the backward
    direction (coherent backscatter, when the grains are about the size of a wavelength of light). At quarter,
    sunlight also strikes much of the lit part at a slant, making it dimmer.

## Summary

- The Moon goes from new through first quarter, full and last quarter and back in 29.5 days.
- It is always half lit by the Sun; the phases show how much of the lit half faces us. They are not the Earth's shadow.
- A Moon $E$ degrees east of the Sun rises about $E/15$ hours after it: first quarter around noon, full at sunset, last
  quarter around midnight.
- Earthshine is sunlight reflected from the Earth; it is strongest at crescent, when the Moon sees a nearly full Earth.
- The Moon is tidally locked, so we always see the same face, about 59% of the surface with libration; the far side is
  not dark.
- From the south the Moon looks upside down; from the tropics the crescent lies on its back.
- The Mid-Autumn Festival and Vesak are held at full Moon.
- Phobos runs through its phases in 7 h 39 min; Deimos looks like a star; the Earth is a morning or evening star from
  Mars.
- The full Moon is much more than twice as bright as the half Moon because of the opposition surge.
