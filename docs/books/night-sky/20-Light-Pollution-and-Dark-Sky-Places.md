# 20. Light pollution and dark-sky places

*For most of history, everyone could see the Milky Way on a clear night. Today most people cannot. This chapter
explains what light pollution is, how much of the night we have lost, how to measure the darkness of your own sky, how
far you need to travel to escape a city's glow, why the lost dark matters to animals and people as well as to
astronomers, what simple changes help, where the protected dark-sky places are around the world, why there is no light
pollution on Mars, and ends by working out why blue light lights up the sky more than amber.*

## 20.1 What light pollution is { #what }

**Light pollution** is light at night that is too bright, in the wrong place, or not needed. It comes in several
kinds:

- **skyglow**, the glow over towns and cities, which hides the fainter stars;
- **glare**, light so bright it dazzles you, such as an unshielded lamp or a car's high beams;
- **light trespass**, light that spills where it is not wanted, such as into a bedroom window;
- **light clutter**, too many bright lights crowded together;
- **over-illumination**, more light than the job needs.

## 20.2 The lost dark { #lost }

From the middle of Manhattan you may see only about 15 stars at once; under a perfectly dark sky, about 2,500
(chapter 1, section 1.6). And cities are no longer the exception:

- In **June 2016** scientists estimated that **a third** of the world's people could no longer see the Milky Way from
  where they live, including 80 per cent of Americans and 60 per cent of Europeans.
- About **83 per cent** of people, and 99 per cent of Europeans and Americans, live under skies more than 10 per cent
  brighter than natural darkness.
- Singapore was found to be the most light-polluted country in the world.
- Light pollution grew by at least **49 per cent** worldwide from 1992 to 2017, and by 16 per cent from 2014 to 2022.

It can go the other way. In the same years, France cut its light pollution by a third through new rules.

## 20.3 How dark is your sky? { #measure }

- **Find the faintest star.** The faintest star you can see tells you how dark the sky is. The Bortle scale turns
  this into nine classes, from 1 for an excellent dark site to 9 for an inner city (chapter 1, section 1.6).
- **Count stars in a constellation.** The project **Globe at Night** asks people to go out on a moonless night, at
  least an hour after sunset, let their eyes adapt, and match what they see in a constellation with a set of charts:
  Orion or Leo in the northern hemisphere, Crux in the southern. It began as a NASA programme in the United States and
  went worldwide in 2009, the International Year of Astronomy. By September 2011 it had almost 70,000 counts. It is a
  good example of **citizen science**: anyone can take part.
- **Use a meter.** A **sky quality meter**, made by the company Unihedron in Grimsby, Ontario, measures the brightness
  of the sky in **magnitudes per square arcsecond**. Like star magnitudes (chapter 4), bigger numbers mean darker: a
  bright city sky reads about 16, the darkest skies on Earth about 22.

??? note "Sources"
    - The Bortle scale: chapter 1, section 1.6, and its sources.
    - Globe at Night: counts on dark moonless nights, at least an hour after sunset, after the eyes adapt, in Orion or
      Leo in the northern hemisphere and Crux in the southern; begun as a NASA programme in the US, expanded
      internationally in the 2009 International Year of Astronomy; almost 70,000 measurements by September 2011; an
      example of citizen science: Wikipedia, [Globe at Night](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Globe_at_Night) (weaker).
    - The sky quality meter, made by Unihedron in Grimsby, Ontario, reading in magnitudes per square arcsecond, from

      [Sky quality meter](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sky_quality_meter) (weaker).

## 20.4 How far to escape? { #distance }

From far away, a city shows as a **dome of light** on the horizon. The farther you go, the fainter it gets, and it
fades faster than you might expect. A rule called **Walker's law** says the glow goes as one over the distance to the
power 2.5.

## 20.5 Not just for astronomers { #harm }

Animals evolved with dark nights, and many depend on them.

- **Sea turtles.** Lights on the shore put off mother turtles from nesting. The hatchlings should find their way to the
  sea by the light of the Moon, but many crawl towards street and hotel lights instead and die.
- **Birds.** Lights on tall buildings and towers can confuse migrating birds.
- **Insects.** Lamps attract many flying insects; spiders that can stand the light build their webs there
  and gain an advantage over those that cannot.
- **Fireflies** use their own light to find mates, and lights at night may make that harder.

People are affected too. Even dim light coming into a bedroom at night is likely to disturb sleep and the body's
night-time hormone, melatonin. And light that shines into the sky is wasted: in the United States alone, the
electricity for wasted light was estimated to cost about 7 billion dollars a year.

??? note "Sources"
    - Turtles, birds, insects and spiders, and fireflies: Wikipedia,

      [Light pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_pollution) (weaker).
    - Light intrusion, even if dim, likely to have measurable effects on sleep disruption and melatonin suppression:
      Wikipedia, [Light pollution](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light_pollution) (weaker).
    - The 7 billion US dollars a year for wasted light in the US: Wikipedia,

## 20.6 Good lighting { #fix }

Unlike most pollution, light pollution stops the moment the light is turned off. Good outdoor
lighting follows a few simple rules:

- **Point it down.** Fittings that are fully shielded, called **full cutoff**, put the light where it is needed and
  none into the sky (Figure A). Most campaigners ask for these.
- **Only when needed.** Timers and motion sensors turn lights off when nobody is there.
- **Warm colours.** Astronomers ask for amber or yellow lights, because the air scatters them less than blue-white
  light (section 20.10). The first white LED street lights, rich in blue, made much more skyglow than the old yellow
  sodium lamps.

**DarkSky International**, first called the International Dark-Sky Association, was founded in **1988** by David
Crawford, a professional astronomer, and Tim Hunter, a doctor and amateur astronomer. It works for better lighting
and names protected dark places. The first city it recognised, on **24 October 2001**, was **Flagstaff**, Arizona,
which had begun controlling its lights in **1958**. Flagstaff is home to Lowell Observatory, where Percival Lowell
looked for canals on Mars and where Pluto was found in 1930 (history book,
[chapter 17, section 17.7](../history/17-Herschel-and-the-Canals-of-Mars.md#lowell)).

## 20.8 No streetlights on Mars { #mars }

Mars has no cities and no streetlights, so it has no light pollution of our kind. Its night sky is lit only by nature:

- **Dust.** Dust high in the thin air scatters sunlight long after sunset, so twilight on Mars can last up to two
  hours (chapter 1, section 1.9).
- **Weak moonlight.** On Earth, the full Moon washes out the fainter stars for several nights each month. Mars's
  brightest moon, Phobos, reaches only magnitude −9 to −10 (chapter 4, section 4.9), so its light hides far fewer
  stars than our Moon's (section 20.9).

Once the twilight has gone, nothing made by people brightens the Martian night. Chapter 21 looks at what you would
see.

??? note "Sources"
    - The long twilight: chapter 1, section 1.9, and its sources. Phobos at −9 to −10: chapter 4, section 4.9, and its
      sources.

## 20.9 Working it out { #working }

**Sky brightness.** A difference of 5 magnitudes per square arcsecond is a factor of 100 in brightness, as with stars
(chapter 4). So a city sky at 16 and the darkest sky at 22, 6 magnitudes apart, differ by

$$100^{6/5} = 10^{0.4 \times 6} = 251 \text{ times}.$$

**Walker's law.** The glow goes as $1 / d^{2.5}$. Going from 10 km to 20 km divides it by $2^{2.5} = 5.7$, so it falls
to $1/5.7 = 18$ per cent; going to 40 km divides it by $4^{2.5} = 32$. To cut the glow ten times, you need
$(d / 10)^{2.5} = 10$, so $d = 10 \times 10^{0.4} = 25$ km.

**Moonlight on Mars.** The full Moon is magnitude −12.74 (chapter 4); Phobos at its brightest is −9 to −10.

| Streetlights | none | 83 per cent of people live under light-polluted skies |

## 20.10 Why blue light glows more { #colour }

**Scattering.** Skyglow comes from light scattered by the air. Two things scatter it: the molecules of the air,
mostly nitrogen and oxygen, and tiny floating particles of dust, smoke and water called **aerosols**. Scattering by
molecules, called **Rayleigh scattering**, goes as one over the fourth power of the wavelength:

$$\text{scattering} \propto \frac{1}{\lambda^4}.$$

Compare blue light of 450 nm with red light of 650 nm:

$$\left(\frac{650}{450}\right)^4 = 1.444^4 = 4.35.$$

The air scatters blue light more than four times as strongly as red. That is why the daytime sky is blue, and why a
blue-rich lamp lights up the night sky more than an amber one giving the same amount of light. Aerosols scatter all
colours more evenly, so in hazy air the colour matters less.

There is a second reason. When your eyes are dark-adapted, you see with your rods, which are most sensitive to
blue-green light of about 500 nm (chapter 1, section 1.4). So the blue glow is exactly the light your night vision
notices most.

**How much light is the whole sky?** A reading in magnitudes per square arcsecond is the brightness of each tiny
square of sky. The whole hemisphere of sky above you is $2\pi$ steradians, and one radian is 206,265″, so it holds

$$2\pi \times 206{,}265^2 = 2.67 \times 10^{11} \text{ square arcseconds}.$$

Adding up that many squares makes the total $2.5 \log_{10} (2.67 \times 10^{11}) = 28.6$ magnitudes brighter than
one square. For the darkest sky, at 22, the whole sky glows like one object of magnitude $22 - 28.6 = -6.6$. For a
city sky at 16, it is $16 - 28.6 = -12.6$: the glow of a city sky, added up, gives roughly as much light as a full
Moon (−12.74), every night of the year.

    - The whole-sky sums are our arithmetic. They treat the sky as evenly bright, which it is not: a city's sky is
      brightest near the horizon towards the city.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Name three kinds of light pollution.

??? note "Answer"
    Any three of skyglow, glare, light trespass, light clutter and over-illumination.

**2.** (beginner) In 2016, about what share of the world's people could no longer see the Milky Way from home?

??? note "Answer"
    About a third, including 80 per cent of Americans and 60 per cent of Europeans.

**3.** (beginner) What simple change to an outdoor lamp keeps its light out of the sky?

??? note "Answer"
    Shield it so all its light points down (a full cutoff fitting).

**4.** (intermediate) A sky quality meter reads 18 in a suburb and 21.5 in the country. Which sky is darker, and
by about how much?

??? note "Answer"
    The country, with the bigger number. The difference is 3.5 magnitudes, so $10^{0.4 \times 3.5} = 10^{1.4} = 25$
    times darker.

**5.** (intermediate) Why do astronomers prefer amber lights to blue-white ones?

??? note "Answer"
    The air scatters blue light much more than amber, so blue-rich lights make more skyglow; and dark-adapted eyes are
    most sensitive to blue-green light.

**6.** (intermediate) Give two ways light at night harms animals.

??? note "Answer"
    Any two: turtle hatchlings crawl towards lights instead of the sea; migrating birds are confused by lit
    buildings; insects swarm to lamps and are eaten; fireflies find it harder to signal to mates.

**7.** (advanced) By Walker's law, how far from a city must you go for its glow to be one hundredth of what it is
5 km away?

??? note "Answer"
    $(d / 5)^{2.5} = 100$, so $d / 5 = 100^{0.4} = 6.3$ and $d = 32$ km.

**8.** (expert) A dark site's sky reads 21.5 magnitudes per square arcsecond. If all the sky's glow above you were
gathered into one point, about what magnitude would it be? Is that brighter than Venus (−4.8)?

??? note "Answer"
    The hemisphere holds $2.67 \times 10^{11}$ square arcseconds, 28.6 magnitudes, so $21.5 - 28.6 = -7.1$. Yes, it is
    brighter than Venus, even at a dark site, though it is spread over the whole sky.

## Summary

- Light pollution is unwanted light at night: skyglow, glare, light trespass, clutter and over-illumination.
- About a third of the world's people cannot see the Milky Way from home, and light pollution is still growing, though
  France has shown it can be cut.
- You can measure your sky by the faintest star, by counting stars for Globe at Night, or with a sky quality meter,
  from about 16 in a city to 22 at the darkest sites.
- A city's glow fades as $1/d^{2.5}$: double the distance, less than a fifth of the glow.
- Light at night harms turtles, birds, insects and fireflies, disturbs sleep, and wastes money.
- Good lighting points down, is on only when needed, and is warm in colour.
- Dark-sky places protect the night from Canada to Chile, Namibia, New Zealand, South Korea, Japan and India.
- Mars has no light pollution; only dust and weak moonlight brighten its night.
- The air scatters blue light as $1/\lambda^4$, so blue-rich lamps light up the sky most.
