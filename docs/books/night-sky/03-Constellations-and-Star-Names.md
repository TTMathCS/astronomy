# 3. Constellations and star names from around the world

*Every people who has watched the night sky has joined its stars into pictures. The same small cluster is seven
sisters in Australia, a storehouse in the Andes and "six stars" in old Japan. This chapter starts with two groups you
can find on your next clear night, Orion and the Pleiades, and follows the stories told about them around the world.
Then it shows how the 48 constellations of Ptolemy became the 88 that astronomers use today, where names such as
Betelgeuse and Aldebaran come from, and who gives a star its official name. It ends with the numbers that explain why
the pictures look just the same from Mars.*

## 3.1 Pictures in the stars { #pictures }

Look up on a clear night and your eyes start joining the brighter stars into shapes almost by themselves: a line, a
box, a cross. People everywhere have done the same, and turned the shapes into animals, heroes, boats and tools.

- A **constellation** is one of the patterns astronomers agree on. In 1922 the International Astronomical Union (IAU)
  accepted a list of **88**, each with a Latin name. In 1928 it gave them official borders, which together cover the
  whole sky, like countries on a map. Every star lies in exactly one constellation.
- An **asterism** is any other pattern. It may be part of a constellation, like the **Big Dipper** (the Plough),
  which is the seven brightest stars of Ursa Major, the Great Bear, or the **Teapot** in Sagittarius. Or it may take
  stars from several constellations, like the **Summer Triangle** of Vega, Deneb and Altair, which belong to Lyra,
  Cygnus and Aquila.

**The stars in a picture are not neighbours.** A constellation is only a direction. Two stars side by side in the sky
can be at very different distances. In Orion, Betelgeuse is somewhere around 400 to 600 light-years away, while Rigel
is about 860. **Aldebaran** looks like the brightest star of the Hyades, the cluster that makes the bull's head of
Taurus, but it only happens to lie in front of them: Aldebaran is about 65 light-years away and the Hyades about 150.

Some patterns are more than chance, though. The three stars of Orion's Belt all belong to one family of stars, and
five of the seven stars of the Big Dipper travel through space together.

**Pictures made of darkness.** Not every picture is made of stars. Across Aboriginal Australia people see the **Emu
in the Sky** in the dark clouds of dust along the Milky Way. Its head is the Coalsack, beside the Southern Cross, and
its body and legs are the dark lane that trails away towards Scorpius. The Wiradjuri call it **Gugurmin**. The Inca
also named animals in the dark clouds (chapter 1, section 1.7).

## 3.2 Start with Orion { #orion }

Orion is the best place to start. It is bright, easy to recognise, and seen from both hemispheres. It is best in the
evening sky from January to April, which is winter in the north and summer in the south. From May to July it is up in
the daytime and lost in sunlight.

Look for **four bright stars in a tall box**: **Betelgeuse**, **Bellatrix**, **Saiph** and **Rigel**. In the middle
of the box are **three stars in a short straight line**: **Orion's Belt**, made of
**Alnitak**, **Alnilam** and **Mintaka**. Below the belt hangs a fainter line of three, the **sword**. Its middle
"star" is not a star at all. It is the **Orion Nebula**, a cloud of gas about 1,300 light-years away where large stars
are being born, the nearest such place to us. It is about 25 light-years across, so in the sky it covers about 1°,
twice the width of the full Moon.

**The belt is a pointer.** Follow it down to the left, towards the south-east, for about 23°, a little more than two
fists at arm's length, and you reach **Sirius**, the brightest star in the night sky. Follow it the other way, up to
the right, for about the same distance, and you reach **Aldebaran**, a red giant star in Taurus, the Bull.

Look at the colours too. **Betelgeuse** is a red supergiant and **Rigel** a blue supergiant: compare them side by side.
Chapter 5 explains what the colours tell us. Betelgeuse also changes in brightness: its
listed magnitude is 0.50, but it wanders between about 0.0 and 1.6.

## 3.3 The Seven Sisters { #pleiades }

Carry on past Aldebaran, about 14° to the north-west, and you find a small, tight group of stars in
the north-west of Taurus: the **Pleiades**. Most people see six or seven stars there. Under a dark sky, sharp eyes can
pick out more, up to 14. In fact the cluster has more than a thousand stars, about 444 light-years away. It is the
star cluster most easily seen with the naked eye. How many can you count?

The Pleiades have been known all over the world since ancient times. The oldest known picture of them is probably on
the **Nebra sky disc**, found in Germany and dated to about 1600 BC. The scholar Stith Thompson noted that they were
"nearly always imagined" as seven sisters, with stories to explain why only six can be seen. Here are some of their
names:

| Māori of Aotearoa | Matariki | |

| Hebrew Bible | Kimah | mentioned three times |
| Quechua of the Andes | Qullqa | "the storehouse" |

A Japanese telescope, the 8.2-metre Subaru on Mauna Kea in Hawaiʻi, is named after the cluster. So is the car maker,
whose badge has six stars.

In **Aboriginal Australia** the Seven Sisters are one of the great stories of the continent. In the songlines of
several peoples, an ancestral man, Wati Nyiru or Yurlu among other names, chases seven sisters across the middle of
Australia, from west to east, until they become stars. In the Western Desert the man is in Orion, still following them
across the sky. In the Kimberley an eagle hawk chases the sisters into the sky, and he becomes the Southern Cross. The
sisters also tell the time of year: when they are close to the horizon at sunset, it is time to collect emu eggs.

The Xhosa of southern Africa used the cluster to count years: men counted their years of manhood from the time in June
when isiLimela first appeared.

## 3.4 One hunter, many stories { #hunter }

To the Greeks Orion was a hunter. Ptolemy placed each star by its part of the body: Betelgeuse, for example, is "in
the right shoulder" of the Hunter. Fainter stars mark his club and an animal skin that he holds up.
Other peoples saw quite different things in the same stars.

- **Egypt:** the stars were the god **Sah**. Orion rises before Sirius, whose rising began the Egyptian year, so Sah was
  closely tied to Sopdet, the goddess of Sirius, and was joined with Osiris. In the Pyramid Texts, from the 24th and
  23rd centuries BC, Sah is one of the gods whose form the dead king takes.
- **Arabia:** Orion was **al-Jawzāʾ**, "the Central One". Betelgeuse comes from *yad al-Jawzāʾ*, "the hand of
  al-Jawzāʾ", and Rigel from *rijl al-jabbār*, "the foot of the giant".
- **China:** the constellation is **Shen** (參), which means "three", after the stars of the belt.
- **India:** the *Rigveda* calls it **Mriga**, the Deer.
- **The Lakota** of North America: the belt is **Tayamnicankhu**, the spine of a bison. The lower half of Orion is
  the Hand, the arm of a chief torn off by the Thunder People as punishment for his selfishness.
- **The Yolŋu** of northern Australia: Orion is **Julpan**, a canoe. Three brothers went fishing, and one ate a
  sawfish that was forbidden under their law. The Sun-woman, Walu, made a waterspout that carried him, his two
  brothers and their canoe up into the sky.

## 3.5 The Chinese sky { #china }

Chinese astronomers divided the sky in their own way, which grew up separately from the Greek one, although the two
may have influenced each other early on. Their star groups are called **officials** (星官, *xīng guān*), and most are
much smaller than the Greek constellations. So there are far more of them:

| Source | Star groups | Stars |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Gan De, 5th century BC, as reported in the Dunhuang Star Chart | 138 | 810 |
| Dunhuang Star Chart, Tang dynasty | 257 | 1,585 |
| Suzhou planisphere, 13th century | 283 | 1,565 |
| Ptolemy's *Almagest*, 2nd century, for comparison | 48 | 1,022 |

The sky is laid out in large regions. Around the north celestial pole lies the **Purple Forbidden Enclosure**, the
stars that never set and can be seen all year round. Two more enclosures straddle the celestial
equator. Around the sky, along the path of the Sun and Moon, run the **Twenty-Eight Mansions** (section 3.6). The far
southern sky was added only in the late Ming dynasty, using European star charts: 23 more groups.

The oldest Chinese star maps that survive date from the Tang dynasty, and the Dunhuang Star Chart is one of them.

## 3.6 Houses of the Moon { #mansions }

Watch the Moon on two nights in a row and you will see it has moved against the stars, by about 13°, a fist and a bit.
It goes all the way round the sky in about 27.3 days. So it spends roughly one night in each of 27 or 28 stretches of
sky, and several peoples marked those stretches with stars, like inns along a road.

- **China** has the **Twenty-Eight Mansions** (*xiù*), seven in each of four quarters of the sky. They follow the
  Moon on its 27.32-day journey round the stars. In Taoism they are linked with 28 generals.
- **India** has the **nakshatras**, 27 or sometimes 28. The *Atharvaveda* lists 28, including one called Abhijit;
  other old texts give 27. The Vedic list begins with **Krittika**, the Pleiades. Later lists begin at the point
  opposite the star Spica, called Chitrā, and in later astronomy the nakshatras became 27 equal parts of the Sun's
  path.

The two systems are very alike but not the same, and nobody knows whether one learned from the other.

**Try it.** On a night when the Moon is up, note which bright star it is near and measure the gap with your hand.
Look again the next night at the same time. It will have moved about 13° against the stars.

## 3.7 From 48 to 88 { #eighty-eight }

The constellations astronomers use today grew up in four steps.

**Babylon.** The oldest Babylonian lists of stars go back to the Middle Bronze Age. The best known, **MUL.APIN**,
names 66 stars and constellations and says when they rise and set through the year. The oldest copy is from 686 BC,
but most scholars think it was first put together around 1000 BC. Its first constellation of the year is MUL.APIN
itself, "the Plough", but this is not our Plough: it lies in the area of Cassiopeia, Andromeda and Triangulum. The
twelve constellations of the zodiac grew out of Babylonian ones.

**Greece and Ptolemy.** The Greeks took over the Babylonian constellations in the 4th century BC, through Eudoxus of
Cnidus. Around AD 150, in the *Almagest*, **Ptolemy** listed 48 constellations: the 12 of the zodiac and 36 others.
Through the Middle Ages, in Europe and in the Islamic world, this was what a constellation meant.

**The far south.** The sky below about −65° had been only partly charted by the astronomers of the north. In the late
16th century Dutch navigators, Pieter Dirkszoon Keyser and Frederick de Houtman, observed the southern stars, and
Petrus Plancius put new constellations on his globes. Johannes Hevelius added more in 1690, and Nicolas Louis de
Lacaille fourteen more in 1763.

**Fixing the list.** By then different maps used different figures, and their edges were vague. In 1922 the IAU
adopted a list of 88 drawn up by Henry Norris Russell, with a three-letter short form for each, such as Hya for
Hydra and Hyi for Hydrus. In 1928 it
adopted borders drawn by Eugène Delporte, published in 1930. They run along lines of the sky's own latitude and
longitude (chapter 17), so every border is a straight north-south or east-west step on a star chart. One of Ptolemy's
48, the ship Argo Navis, has since been split into three, so his 36 others are now 38.

**The zodiac has thirteen.** The Sun's yearly path through the stars (chapter 8) passes through 13 constellations, not
12. The thirteenth is **Ophiuchus**, the serpent-bearer. People sometimes call it a "13th sign", but that mixes up the
constellations with the **signs** of astrology, which are twelve equal pieces of 30° each, counted from the point
where the Sun crosses the equator in March. In Ptolemy's day most stars of each zodiac constellation lay inside the
sign of the same name. They do not now. The Earth's axis slowly turns, like a spinning top, and the starting point
moves 1° every 72 years. Since Ptolemy the longitudes of the stars have grown by about 26°, most of a whole sign.

## 3.8 Where star names come from { #names }

Most of the names we use for the bright stars are Arabic. They came by a long road.

1. **Ptolemy** did not name most stars. He placed each one by its part of the figure: "in the right shoulder of the
   Hunter".
2. The *Almagest* was translated into **Arabic** in the 8th and 9th centuries. Astronomers in the Islamic world turned
   some of Ptolemy's descriptions into names, and added names from the older star lore of Arabia. The best known is the
   *Book of Fixed Stars* of **al-Sufi**.
3. In the 12th century the *Almagest* was translated from Arabic into **Latin**, and the names came into Europe. Many
   were copied or translated wrongly, some by writers who knew little Arabic.

| Name today | Arabic | Meaning | Constellation |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Aldebaran | *ad-Dabarān* | the Follower (of the Pleiades) | Taurus |
| Altair | *(an-Nisr) aṭ-Ṭāʾir* | the Flying (Eagle) | Aquila |
| Vega | *an-Nisr al-Wāqiʿ* | the Falling Eagle | Lyra |
| Deneb | *Dhanab ad-Dajājah* | tail of the hen | Cygnus |
| Rigel | *Rijl al-Jabbār* | foot of the giant | Orion |
| Fomalhaut | *Fam al-Ḥūt* | mouth of the whale | Piscis Austrinus |
| Algol | *(Raʾs) al-Ghūl* | (head of) the ghoul | Perseus |
| Betelgeuse | *Yad al-Jawzāʾ* | hand of al-Jawzāʾ | Orion |

Aldebaran is "the Follower" because it seems to follow the Pleiades across the sky. **Betelgeuse** is a copying
mistake. At the start of a word, the Arabic letters *y* and *b* have the same shape and differ only in their dots. In
the 13th century someone read the *y* of *Yad* as a *b*, and *Yad al-Jawzāʾ* became Betelgeuse.

**Greek letters.** In 1603 the German astronomer **Johann Bayer** gave the brighter stars of each constellation a Greek
letter in his star atlas *Uranometria*, followed by the Latin name of the constellation in the possessive. So
Aldebaran is **α Tauri**, "Alpha of the Bull". When he ran out of Greek letters he went on with Latin ones, and later
astronomers such as Lacaille carried the scheme into the southern sky. The letters are only a rough guide to
brightness. In Orion, Rigel is β Orionis, yet at magnitude 0.13 it is brighter than α Orionis, Betelgeuse, at 0.50.

## 3.9 Official names { #iau }

For a long time the same star could have several names, spelt in several ways. In May 2016 the IAU set up a
**Working Group on Star Names** to choose one name for each star and keep an official list. The IAU separates a
star's **name**, the word people use, from its **designation**, a code such as α Tauri used in catalogues.

The group began with names already in use. Its first two lists, in July and November 2016, held 125 and 102 stars,
**227** in all, Betelgeuse among them. More were added in 2017 (86) and 2018 (17), making **330**, and the list is
still being added to. Since 2023 it has been kept on the exopla.net website.

The group's rules say that:

- names that keep the world's heritage alive are strongly encouraged, and old cultural names are preferred to new ones;
- names should be 4 to 16 characters long, easy to say in some language, and not offensive;
- names must not be too close to the name of another star, planet or moon;
- names of people are not allowed for bright stars, except in rare historical cases;
- names of pets, of businesses, or of events known mainly for war or politics are not allowed.

A few names of people did get through. Two came from a public contest in 2015: Cervantes, after the writer, and
Copernicus, after the astronomer. The old name **Cor Caroli**, "heart of Charles", was kept for a star named after
King Charles I of England by his doctor. In 2017 the group set rules for Chinese names, preferring pinyin spelling.
And in 2024 it launched the **All Skies Encyclopaedia**, a website hosted by the University of Jena to record the star
names and constellations of cultures all over the world.

## 3.10 The same pictures from Mars { #mars }

Stand on Mars on a clear night and you would see Orion, the Pleiades and the Southern Cross exactly as you see them
from Earth. Mars is never more than about 400 million km from us, but even the nearest stars, in the Alpha Centauri
system, are about 100,000 times farther away than that. Moving from Earth to Mars shifts the stars by about 2 seconds
of arc, thirty times too small for the eye to notice. Section 3.11 does the sums.

Even the Sun's path through the stars is almost the same. The orbit of Mars is tilted only 1.85° to the Earth's, so
from Mars the Sun crosses nearly the same band of constellations through the Martian year.

What does change is the pole. Mars's axis points to a different part of the sky, and there the way to the north
celestial pole is from Sadr and Deneb (chapter 2, section 2.9). The names and stories on Earth
would still serve on Mars. Whoever lives there one day may well add some of their own.

## 3.11 Working it out { #working }

**The angle between two stars.** Every star has a position in the sky, given by two numbers like latitude and
longitude on the Earth (chapter 17 explains them): its **declination** $\delta$, the angle north or south of the
celestial equator, and its **right ascension** $\alpha$, the angle around it. For two stars the angle $\theta$ between
them is

$$\cos\theta = \sin\delta_1 \sin\delta_2 + \cos\delta_1 \cos\delta_2 \cos(\alpha_1 - \alpha_2).$$

Take Betelgeuse ($\alpha_1 = 88.79°$, $\delta_1 = +7.41°$) and Rigel ($\alpha_2 = 78.63°$, $\delta_2 = -8.20°$). Then
$\alpha_1 - \alpha_2 = 10.16°$ and

$$\cos\theta = (0.1289)(-0.1427) + (0.9917)(0.9898)(0.9843) = -0.0184 + 0.9661 = 0.9477,$$

so $\theta = 18.6°$, about two fists. The same sum gives 23° from the belt to Sirius and to Aldebaran, and 14° from
Aldebaran to the Pleiades.

For stars close together there is a shortcut. Treat the small patch of sky as flat: the east-west gap is
$(\alpha_1 - \alpha_2)\cos\delta$ and the north-south gap is $\delta_1 - \delta_2$, and you combine them with
Pythagoras. Question 7 tries it on the belt.

**The houses of the Moon.** The Moon goes round the stars in 27.32 days, so each night it moves

$$\frac{360°}{27.32} \approx 13.2°.$$

Split the circle into 28 houses and each is $360° / 28 \approx 12.9°$ wide; into 27 and each is $13.3°$. Either way,
about one house a night.

**Why the pictures do not change.** If you look at a star from two places a distance $b$ apart, and the star is a
distance $d$ away, its direction changes by an angle of about $b/d$ in radians. Multiply by 206,265 to get seconds of
arc. The nearest star system, Alpha Centauri, is $d = 4.159 \times 10^{13}$ km away. The widest shift any planet sees
is from opposite sides of its own orbit, so $b$ is twice the planet's distance from the Sun:

| | Mars | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mean distance from the Sun | 227.956 million km | 149.598 million km |
| Width of the orbit, $b$ | 455.9 million km | 299.2 million km |
| Shift of Alpha Centauri, $206{,}265\, b/d$ | 2.26″ | 1.48″ |
| Times smaller than the eye can see (60″) | 27 | 40 |

Even from the far ends of the orbit of Mars, the nearest stars move by a twenty-seventh of what the eye can notice,
and the stars beyond them move less. Going from Earth to Mars is a smaller step still: at their farthest apart, 401.4
million km, the shift is $206{,}265 \times 401.4 / 41{,}590{,}000 \approx 2.0″$. The constellations are fixed for us
and for any Martian.

## 3.12 Did Ptolemy copy his stars? { #ptolemy }

Ptolemy's star catalogue in the *Almagest* is the oldest that survives complete, with a position and a brightness for
each of just over a thousand stars. Hipparchus had made a catalogue about 265 years earlier, but it has not survived
in its original form: it was absorbed into Ptolemy's. So did Ptolemy measure his stars himself, or copy them? The
question has been argued for four hundred years, and the numbers show why.

**A check anyone can do.** Ptolemy says his star longitudes are for AD 138, the start of the reign of the emperor
Antoninus Pius, and that the longitudes had grown by 2°40′ since Hipparchus. He used Hipparchus' rate for precession,
1° in 100 years. Over 265 years that gives

$$\frac{265}{100} = 2.65° = 2°39′,$$

almost exactly his 2°40′. The true rate is 1° in 72 years, which gives

$$\frac{265}{72} = 3.68° = 3°41′.$$

So if Ptolemy took Hipparchus' positions and added 2°40′, his longitudes would come out about 1° too small, as if
they had been measured about 72 years before AD 138, around AD 65. Careful studies of the whole catalogue do find the
longitudes too small, matching AD 48 to 58 rather than 138.

**How it was explained.** Tycho Brahe was the first to notice the offset. Since then people have suggested that:

- Ptolemy worked out all his positions from Hipparchus' with the wrong rate (Delambre, 1817);
- the stars were really measured a century earlier by Menelaus of Alexandria (Björnbo, 1901);
- the offset is many small errors added together, including the use of out-of-date figures for the Sun;
- his instrument was set up wrongly and gave a steady error.

**What the errors say.** Take away the steady offset and smaller errors remain. About 18 to 20 of them are also found
in what can be rebuilt of Hipparchus' catalogue, so some of Ptolemy's positions do go back to Hipparchus. But
Hipparchus' worst mistakes are not in the *Almagest*, and Hipparchus listed some stars that Ptolemy left out. That
points to a catalogue built on Hipparchus' work but measured again and corrected, not simply copied. Not every test
agrees: some attempts to date the observations from the slow motions of the stars themselves point back to
Hipparchus' time. And copying by hand added its own errors, because some of the symbols for numbers look alike.

The history book tells the rest of the story, including a piece of Hipparchus' catalogue found in 2022:
[chapter 7, section 7.8](../history/07-Greece-and-Alexandria.md#ptolemy).

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) What is the difference between a constellation and an asterism? Give an example of an asterism.

??? note "Answer"
    A constellation is one of the 88 areas of sky the IAU recognises, with official borders. An asterism is any other
    star pattern, such as the Big Dipper in Ursa Major, the Teapot in Sagittarius, or the Summer Triangle.

**2.** (beginner) How do you find Sirius from Orion?

??? note "Answer"
    Follow the line of Orion's Belt towards the south-east, down to the left, for about 23°, a little more than two
    fists. The brightest star there is Sirius.

**3.** (beginner) Give three names for the Pleiades from different peoples, and what they mean.

??? note "Answer"
    For example: Subaru in Japan, "to cluster together"; Qullqa in the Andes, "the storehouse"; MUL.MUL in Babylon,
    "star star"; isiLimela in southern Africa, the "digging stars"; the Seven Sisters in Greece and Aboriginal
    Australia.

**4.** (intermediate) Someone says Ophiuchus is the "13th sign of the zodiac". What is wrong with that?

??? note "Answer"
    It mixes up constellations and signs. The Sun's path does cross 13 constellations, Ophiuchus among them. But the
    signs of astrology are twelve equal pieces of 30°, counted from the March equinox, and because of precession they
    no longer line up with the constellations of the same names.

**5.** (intermediate) How did Betelgeuse get its name, and why does it start with a B?

??? note "Answer"
    It comes from the Arabic *Yad al-Jawzāʾ*, "the hand of al-Jawzāʾ". In Arabic writing *y* and *b* differ only in
    their dots, and in the 13th century the *y* was misread as a *b*.

**6.** (intermediate) Why did India and China divide the Moon's path into 27 or 28 parts?

??? note "Answer"
    The Moon goes round the stars in about 27.3 days, so it spends about one night in each of 27 or 28 stretches of
    sky.

**7.** (advanced) Alnitak is at $\alpha = 85.19°$, $\delta = -1.94°$ and Mintaka at $\alpha = 83.00°$,
$\delta = -0.30°$. Use the flat-sky shortcut to find the length of Orion's Belt.

??? note "Answer"
    East-west: $(85.19 - 83.00) \times \cos 1.1° \approx 2.19°$. North-south: $1.94 - 0.30 = 1.64°$. So the belt is
    $\sqrt{2.19^2 + 1.64^2} \approx 2.7°$ long, about three little fingers at arm's length.

**8.** (expert) Ptolemy said the stars' longitudes had grown by 2°40′ since Hipparchus. Why does this number make
historians suspect copying, and why do most not think he simply copied the whole catalogue?

??? note "Answer"
    2°40′ is just what Hipparchus' rate of 1° per century gives for 265 years. The true rate, 1° in 72 years, gives
    3°41′. Ptolemy's longitudes are indeed about 1° too small, as if Hipparchus' positions had been moved on by the
    wrong amount. But after the steady offset is taken away, Hipparchus' worst errors are missing from the
    *Almagest*, and Hipparchus had stars Ptolemy does not. So the catalogue looks like Hipparchus' work measured
    again and corrected, though some positions do go back to him.

## Summary

- A constellation is one of the 88 official areas of sky; an asterism is any other pattern, such as the Big Dipper.
- The stars of a picture are usually at very different distances; they only lie in the same direction.
- Orion's box and belt are easy to find, and the belt points to Sirius one way and Aldebaran the other.
- The Pleiades have a name and a story almost everywhere, very often as seven sisters.
- The same stars make a hunter, a god, a deer, a bison's spine and a canoe, depending on who is looking.
- China used hundreds of small star groups; China and India both marked the Moon's houses, 27 or 28 of them.
- Babylon, Greece, Dutch navigators and the IAU between them turned the sky into 88 constellations with fixed borders.
- Most bright-star names are Arabic, some badly copied; since 2016 the IAU has kept an official list.
- From Mars the constellations look the same, because even the nearest star shifts by only about 2″.
- Ptolemy's 2°40′ shows he used Hipparchus' rate of precession, but his catalogue was not simply a copy.
