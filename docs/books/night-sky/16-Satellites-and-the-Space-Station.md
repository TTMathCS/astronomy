# 16. Satellites and the space station

*Watch a clear sky for half an hour after dusk and you will probably see a "star" glide silently across it, steady
and unblinking, then fade away in the middle of the sky. That is a satellite. There are now more than ten thousand of
them, and one, the International Space Station, has people on board and can outshine every star. This chapter shows
how to tell satellites from planes, why they shine only in the hours around dusk and dawn, how to catch the space
station, how high satellites fly, how it all began, what circles Mars, and ends by working out how long after sunset
a satellite stays in sunlight.*

## 16.1 Moving lights { #moving }

A **satellite** is anything that orbits a planet. The Moon is the Earth's natural satellite; the ones in this chapter
are made by people. Thousands are up there now: the Starlink network alone had about 11,150 in orbit in October 2026.

There are three kinds of moving light in the night sky:

- A **meteor** is a quick streak that is gone in a moment (chapter 15).
- A **plane** has flashing lights, and it can turn.
- A **satellite** is a steady point of light that moves smoothly in a straight line across the sky. It does not flash
  and it does not change direction. It often fades out before it reaches the horizon, as it moves into the Earth's
  shadow.

Satellites in low orbits cross the sky at about the same rate as a plane flying high overhead, though some are faster
and some slower.

??? note "Sources"
    - About 11,150 Starlink satellites in orbit in October 2026: Wikipedia,
      [Starlink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starlink) (weaker).
    - The space station looking like a plane or a very bright star moving across the sky, but without flashing lights
      and without changing direction: NASA, [Spot the Station](https://www.nasa.gov/spot-the-station/).
    - Fading in or out as it moves into or out of the Earth's shadow: Wikipedia,
      [International Space Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Space_Station) (weaker).
    - Low satellites appearing to move at a similar speed to high aircraft, but not all at the same speed: Wikipedia,

## 16.2 Why only at dusk and dawn? { #sunlit }

A satellite makes no light of its own. We see it because it reflects sunlight, like the Moon and the planets.

During the day the sky is too bright to see it. In the middle of the night it is dark, but low satellites are in the
Earth's shadow too. The time to look is in between: in the hours after sunset and before sunrise, when your sky is
dark but a satellite a few hundred kilometres up is still high enough to catch the sunlight.

## 16.3 The International Space Station { #iss }

The **International Space Station** (ISS) is the largest spacecraft people have ever built.

- **Size.** 109 m from end to end, about the length of a football field. It has a mass of about 420 tonnes.
- **Height and speed.** It orbits about 400 km up, at 7.67 km/s, about 28,000 km/h. That is around 29 times faster
  than an airliner. It goes round the Earth in about 93 minutes, 15.5 times a day.
- **Path.** Its orbit is tilted 51.6° to the equator, so it passes over every place between 51.6° north and 51.6°
  south. It travels from west to east.
- **Brightness.** Overhead and in full sunlight it reaches magnitude about −4, as bright as Venus. Apart from brief
  flares (section 16.5), it is the brightest thing people have put in the sky.
- **People.** Five space agencies from 15 countries run it. The first part, the Russian module Zarya, was launched on
  **20 November 1998**, and the first crew moved in on **2 November 2000**. People have lived on board ever since,
  usually seven at a time. Its 17.6 m robot arm, **Canadarm2**, was Canada's part.
- **The end.** It is planned to keep working until the end of 2030.

China has its own station, **Tiangong**. Its core module, Tianhe, was launched on **29 April 2021**. It flies 340 to
450 km up, on an orbit tilted 41°, and reaches magnitude −2.2 or brighter, about as bright as Jupiter.

## 16.4 Catching a pass { #spot }

The station is easy to see if you know when to look.

1. **Find out when.** NASA's free **Spot the Station** app lists the coming passes for your town and can send you an
   alert before each one.
2. **Find the direction.** Use the compass directions of chapter 2. It travels from west to east, so it appears
   somewhere in the western half of the sky.
3. **Be outside a few minutes early.** A pass from horizon to horizon can take up to about 10 minutes, though you may
   see only part of it, because it slips into or out of the Earth's shadow.
4. **Watch for more.** It comes round again about every 90 minutes. Passes come in runs for some days, then stop for a
   while; the time of day slowly shifts over the following weeks before they come back.

You need no telescope. To photograph it, put a camera on a tripod and take an exposure of several seconds or more: the
station draws a bright line across the stars.

??? note "Sources"
    - The Spot the Station app: notifications, real-time tracking and flyover schedules: NASA,
      [Spot the Station](https://www.nasa.gov/spot-the-station/).
    - A pass taking up to 10 minutes, often visible for only part of that time because of the Earth's shadow, a return
      about every 90 minutes, and the time of day shifting over some weeks: Wikipedia,
      [International Space Station](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/International_Space_Station) (weaker).

## 16.5 Crowded skies { #crowded }

**Flares.** The first Iridium phone satellites had flat, shiny antennas. When one caught the Sun at just the right
angle, people on the ground saw a flash lasting a few seconds. The brightest reached magnitude −8, far brighter than
Venus, and could be seen several times a month. The new Iridium satellites do not flare, and the last of the old ones
were brought down by 27 December 2019.

**Mega-constellations.** The first batch of 60 Starlink satellites was launched on **24 May 2019**. By October 2026
there were about 11,150, flying low, at around 550 km. Astronomers have warned that so many bright satellites spoil
their pictures with streaks and interfere with radio telescopes, and that it is getting harder to work around them.
SpaceX tried a dark coating on one satellite, which astronomers found still too bright, then sunshades, and later
other ways to dim them.

For a stargazer this is light pollution of a new kind, which even a dark-sky site cannot escape (chapter 20).

??? note "Sources"
    - Iridium flares lasting a few seconds, magnitude −5 three or four times a week and −8 three to five times a month,
      the new generation that does not flare, and the old ones deorbited by 27 December 2019: Wikipedia,
      [Satellite flare](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satellite_flare) (weaker).
    - The first 60 Starlink satellites on 24 May 2019, about 11,150 in October 2026, 550 km, criticism from the IAU and
      radio astronomers, observations "getting more difficult", DarkSat's coating found still too bright, visors from
      August 2020 (removed from September 2021) and later dimming methods: Wikipedia,
      [Starlink](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Starlink) (weaker).

Higher orbits take longer to go round. At **35,786 km** above the equator, an orbit takes exactly as long as the Earth
takes to turn once, so a satellite there stays over the same spot on the ground. This is a **geostationary orbit**.
From the ground the satellite seems to hang still in the sky, which is why a satellite-TV dish can be bolted in place,
pointing at one spot, without having to track it. The writer Arthur C. Clarke made the idea famous in 1945, and the
first satellite in this kind of orbit was launched in 1963.

The Moon, 384,400 km away, is about nine times further out than that.

## 16.7 How it began { #history }

- In **1687** Isaac Newton's *Principia* showed that the Moon is falling towards the Earth all the time, which is just
  what it must do to curve round its orbit instead of flying off in a straight line (history book,
  [chapter 16, section 16.2](../history/16-Newton-Halley-and-Romer.md#principia)). Every satellite works the same way.
- On **4 October 1957** the Soviet Union launched **Sputnik 1**, a polished ball 58 cm across that sent radio beeps
  for three weeks. Sputnik itself was barely visible to the naked eye, but the rocket that carried it up followed it
  round as a first-magnitude light. The new dish at Jodrell Bank in England was ready just in time to track Sputnik
  (history book, [chapter 20, section 20.5](../history/20-Radio-Astronomy.md#dishes)).
- In **1960** the United States launched **Echo 1**, a shiny balloon 30 m across that bounced radio signals from one
  side of America to the other. It could be seen with the naked eye over most of the Earth.
- In **1963** the first satellite was placed in a 24-hour orbit.
- In **1998** the first part of the space station went up.

??? note "Sources"
    - Newton's Moon test: this site's history book, chapter 16, and its sources.
    - Sputnik 1: 4 October 1957, a polished metal sphere 58 cm across, radio beeps for three weeks, barely visible
      itself, and the rocket body visible at night as a first-magnitude object: Wikipedia,
      [Sputnik 1](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sputnik_1) (weaker). Jodrell Bank: this site's history book,

## 16.8 Satellites at Mars { #mars }

Mars has two natural satellites, **Phobos** and **Deimos**. Phobos goes round in only 7 hours 39 minutes, faster than
Mars turns, so it **rises in the west and sets in the east**, twice a sol. The space station does the same thing over
the Earth, for the same reason: it goes round faster than the planet turns.

Deimos takes 30.3 hours, a little longer than Mars takes to turn, so it rises in the east and sets in the west but
crawls across the sky. Figure B shows why: Deimos orbits just outside Mars's stationary orbit, **17,032 km** up,
and Phobos far inside it.

Mars has had artificial satellites since **14 November 1971**, when **Mariner 9** became the first spacecraft to orbit
another planet (history book, [chapter 21, section 21.2](../history/21-The-Space-Age-at-Mars.md#orbit)). Today
several orbiters circle it. NASA's **Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter**, there since **10 March 2006**, takes pictures and
relays data from the rovers back to Earth; so many craft now orbit Mars that one big dish on Earth often listens to
several at once (history book, [chapter 20, section 20.10](../history/20-Radio-Astronomy.md#mars)).

The rule of section 16.2 would hold on Mars too: someone standing there could see an orbiter only while it was in
sunlight and their own sky was dark.

## 16.9 Working it out { #working }

**Speed.** For a circular orbit of radius $r$ (measured from the centre of the planet), the speed is
$v = \sqrt{GM/r}$, where $GM$ is the planet's gravity constant. For the Earth, $GM = 398{,}600$ km³/s². The space
station, 400 km up, is $6{,}371 + 400 = 6{,}771$ km from the centre:

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{398{,}600}{6{,}771}} = \sqrt{58.9} = 7.67 \text{ km/s}.$$

**Period.** Once round is $2\pi r$, so

$$T = \frac{2\pi \times 6{,}771}{7.67} = 5{,}547 \text{ s} = 92.4 \text{ minutes},$$

very close to the 93 minutes of section 16.3.

**The stationary orbit.** Turn the formula round. Putting the two together gives $T^2 = 4\pi^2 r^3 / GM$ (Kepler's third
law, chapter 8, section 8.9), so

$$r = \sqrt[3]{\frac{GM\,T^2}{4\pi^2}}.$$

The Earth turns once against the stars in 23.9345 hours, 86,164 s:

$$r = \sqrt[3]{\frac{398{,}600 \times 86{,}164^2}{39.48}} = 42{,}164 \text{ km},$$

and $42{,}164 - 6{,}378 = 35{,}786$ km above the equator, just as in section 16.6.

| | Mars | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| $GM$ (km³/s²) | 42,828 | 398,600 |
| Radius at the equator | 3,396 km | 6,378 km |
| Turns once in | 24.6229 hours | 23.9345 hours |
| Orbit 400 km up: speed | 3.36 km/s | 7.67 km/s |
| Orbit 400 km up: once round | 118 minutes | 92 minutes |
| Stationary orbit: height | 17,032 km | 35,786 km |
| Natural satellites | Phobos (7 h 39 min) and Deimos (30.3 h) | the Moon (27.3 days) |

Mars's weaker gravity means slower orbits: at the same height, an orbiter goes round Mars at less than half the speed.

??? note "Sources"
    - $GM$, equatorial radii, mean radii (3,389.5 and 6,371 km) and sidereal rotation periods: NASA,
      [Earth Fact Sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/earthfact.html) and
      [Mars Fact Sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/marsfact.html), which also give the periods of
      Phobos and Deimos. The Moon's 27.3 days: history book, chapter 15, section 15.9.

## 16.10 How long after sunset? { #shadow }

Take a satellite at height $h$ straight above you, and let the Sun be $d$ degrees below your horizon. The Earth's
shadow is a cylinder of radius $R$, the Earth's radius, pointing away from the Sun. The satellite is a distance
$(R + h)$ from the centre of the Earth, along your vertical, and that line makes an angle of $90° + d$ with the
direction of the Sun. So its distance from the axis of the shadow is $(R + h)\cos d$, and it stays in sunlight while

$$(R + h)\cos d > R, \quad\text{that is}\quad \cos d > \frac{R}{R + h}.$$

For the space station, $h = 400$ km: $\cos d > 6{,}371 / 6{,}771 = 0.941$, so $d < 19.8°$.

So the station can shine straight overhead until the Sun is almost 20° down, after the end of astronomical twilight
(18°, chapter 1). The Sun sinks 15° an hour at most (near the equator), so that is at least about 80 minutes after
sunset; further from the equator, where the Sun goes down at a slant, it is longer. Satellites low in the sky on the
side towards the Sun can stay lit even later.

**Higher satellites.** For a Starlink satellite at 550 km, $\cos d > 6{,}371 / 6{,}921 = 0.921$, so $d < 23.0°$. For
a geostationary satellite, $R/(R + h) = 6{,}378 / 42{,}164 = 0.151$, so $d < 81.3°$: it is nearly always in sunlight.

**When geostationary satellites go dark.** Seen from the Earth's centre, the shadow at the geostationary distance is a
disc of angular radius $\arcsin(6{,}378 / 42{,}164) = 8.7°$, centred on the point opposite the Sun. Geostationary
satellites all sit over the equator. Most of the year the Sun is far enough north or south of the equator that the
shadow passes above or below them. Only when the Sun is within 8.7° of the equator, around the equinoxes, does the
shadow cross the ring. Near the equinox the Sun's position north or south changes by about 0.4° a day, so these
**eclipse seasons** last about 22 days on either side of each equinox. At the equinox itself the satellite crosses the
full width of the shadow, about 12,756 km, at 3.07 km/s, and is dark for about 69 minutes around local midnight.

??? note "Sources"
    - The Earth's radii (6,371 km mean, 6,378 km at the equator): NASA,
      [Earth Fact Sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/earthfact.html).
    - Twilight ending at 18° and the Sun moving 15° an hour: chapter 1, section 1.2.
    - The rate of 0.4° a day comes from the Earth's tilt of 23.4° (chapter 8) and the Sun's motion of 0.986° a day
      along its path (360° in 365.25 days): $\sin 23.4° \times 0.986° = 0.39°$ a day near the equinox. The speed of
      3.07 km/s is $2\pi \times 42{,}164 / 86{,}164$. The geometry and arithmetic are ours.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) How can you tell a satellite from a plane?

??? note "Answer"
    A satellite is a steady point of light that moves smoothly in a straight line. It has no flashing lights and does
    not turn, and it often fades out in mid-sky as it enters the Earth's shadow.

**2.** (beginner) Why are satellites seen only in the hours after sunset and before sunrise?

??? note "Answer"
    They shine by reflected sunlight. In the day the sky is too bright; late at night low satellites are in the
    Earth's shadow. Only around dusk and dawn is your sky dark while the satellite is still in sunlight.

**3.** (beginner) In which part of the sky does the space station appear, and which way does it go?

??? note "Answer"
    It appears in the western half of the sky and travels towards the east.

**4.** (intermediate) What is a geostationary orbit, and why does a satellite-TV dish not need to move?

??? note "Answer"
    An orbit 35,786 km above the equator that takes exactly as long as the Earth takes to turn once. A satellite there
    stays over the same spot on the ground, so the dish can point at one fixed place in the sky.

**5.** (intermediate) Why can nobody see Iridium flares any more?

??? note "Answer"
    The flares came from the flat, shiny antennas of the first Iridium satellites. Their replacements do not flare,
    and the old ones were all brought down by the end of 2019.

**6.** (intermediate) How is Phobos like the space station?

??? note "Answer"
    Both go round their planet faster than the planet turns, so both rise in the west and set in the east.

**7.** (advanced) Use $v = \sqrt{GM/r}$ to find the speed of an orbiter 400 km above Mars, and the time it takes to go
round once.

??? note "Answer"
    $r = 3{,}389.5 + 400 = 3{,}789.5$ km. $v = \sqrt{42{,}828 / 3{,}789.5} = \sqrt{11.30} = 3.36$ km/s. $T = 2\pi
    \times 3{,}789.5 / 3.36 = 7{,}086$ s, about 118 minutes.

**8.** (expert) A satellite at 400 km above Mars is straight above an astronaut. How far below the horizon can the Sun
be while the satellite is still in sunlight?

??? note "Answer"
    $\cos d > 3{,}389.5 / 3{,}789.5 = 0.894$, so $d < 26.6°$. Mars is smaller, so its shadow is narrower for a
    satellite at the same height, and the window after sunset is longer than on the Earth (19.8°).

## Summary

- Satellites are steady, unblinking lights that move smoothly across the sky; there are now more than ten thousand.
- They shine by reflected sunlight, so you see them in the hours after sunset and before sunrise.
- The space station is 109 m long, orbits 400 km up every 93 minutes, can be as bright as Venus, and has had people on
  board since 2000. China's Tiangong is about as bright as Jupiter.
- NASA's Spot the Station tells you when it will pass; it comes from the west and heads east.
- So many bright satellites are a problem for astronomers.
- A geostationary orbit, 35,786 km up, takes a day, so satellites there hang still in the sky.
- Sputnik 1 began the space age in 1957.
- Phobos rises in the west, like the space station; Mars's stationary orbit is 17,032 km up.
- A satellite 400 km up stays sunlit overhead until the Sun is 19.8° below the horizon.
