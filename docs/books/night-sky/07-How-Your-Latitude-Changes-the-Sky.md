# 7. How your latitude changes the sky

*Travel north and the pole star climbs; travel south and it sinks, until it drops below the horizon and stars you
have never seen rise in its place. This chapter takes a trip from the North Pole to the equator and on into the
south, and watches the sky tilt as we go. On the way it meets Canopus, the star that told travellers how far south they
had come, the people who used the stars to show that the Earth is round, and the sailors who read their latitude from
Polaris with a knotted string. It looks at the sky over the two rovers on Mars, and ends with the expeditions that
found the Earth is not quite round after all.*

## 7.1 The pole climbs as you go north { #climb }

In chapter 6 we saw that the height of Polaris above the horizon is about equal to your latitude. Here is why.

Your latitude is the angle between you and the equator, measured at the centre of the Earth. The north celestial pole
is so far away that it lies in the same direction from every point on Earth: straight along the Earth's axis. At the
North Pole that direction is straight up. At the equator it is level, along the ground. In between, it tilts by
exactly as much as your latitude. So:

$$\text{height of the celestial pole above the horizon} = \text{your latitude}.$$

Walk north and the pole climbs; walk south and it sinks. On the Earth, one degree of latitude is about **111 km**, so
for every 111 km you go north, Polaris rises by about 1°, two widths of the full Moon. Two people looking at the same
star at the same moment from different latitudes see it at different heights.

## 7.2 At the North Pole { #pole }

Stand at the North Pole and the north celestial pole is straight overhead, with Polaris less than a degree from it.

- Every star circles **level with the horizon**, all the way round the sky (figure A, right). No star rises and none
  sets.
- You only ever see **half the sky**: the northern half. The Southern Cross never appears at all.
- The Sun, the Moon and the planets are different, because they move against the stars. At the North Pole the Sun
  stays up for about six months, from about 18 March to 24 September, and stays down for most of the rest of the year.

You do not need to go all the way to the pole to see the Sun at midnight. In summer it stays up at midnight anywhere
north of about **65.7°**, near the Arctic Circle. Chapter 8 explains why.

## 7.3 At the equator { #equator }

Now go to the equator. Your latitude is 0°, so the pole has sunk right down to the horizon.

- Both celestial poles lie **on the horizon**, the north one due north and the south one due south. Polaris, if the
  air is clear enough, sits on the northern horizon.
- Every star rises straight up out of the east and sets straight down into the west (figure A, left).
- Every star is above the horizon for **half of each day**. No star is circumpolar, and none is hidden for ever.

So over a year, from the equator, you can see every star in the sky, north and south.

- **Canopus**, the second-brightest star in the night sky, first rises south of about **37.3°N**, just south of
  Athens, San Francisco and Seoul.
- The whole **Southern Cross** rises at some time of year south of about **25°N**.
- The **Large Magellanic Cloud**, a small galaxy that goes round our own, can be seen from as far north as **20°N**.

Travellers noticed. In 1592 the English explorer Robert Hues counted three first-magnitude stars that he had seen in
the far south but which are never seen in England: Canopus, Achernar and Alpha Centauri. Arab writers knew the same
thing much earlier: in 964 the astronomer al-Sufi repeated a report of bright white stars below Canopus, "unseen in
Iraq" and called **al-Baqar**, "the cows", by the people of Tihama. These were the
Magellanic Clouds.

Familiar stars change too. From Australia or South Africa you must face north to see Orion, so it appears upside down
compared with the view from Europe or North America.

## 7.5 Canopus, the star of the south { #canopus }

For people in the northern half of the old world, Canopus was the star that marked the edge of the sky: always low,
seen only part of the year, and hidden altogether from further north. Its names show it.

- **Arabic:** *Suhayl*, a name that came to suggest rareness, since Canopus appeared so seldom in the Middle East. The
  Bedouin of the Negev and Sinai used Suhayl and Polaris as their two main stars for steering at night. Because Suhayl
  sank below their horizon, it stood for a changeable nature, while ever-present Polaris stood for steadfastness. The
  south-eastern wall of the Kaaba in Mecca is lined up with the rising point of Canopus.
- **China:** the **Old Man of the South Pole**.
- **India:** the Vedic sage **Agastya**, whose star's rising was said to coincide with the calming of the waters of
  the Indian Ocean.
- **Aotearoa:** among several Māori names, *Atutahi*, "stand alone", a solitary star.
- **Hawaiʻi:** *Ke Aliʻi-o-kona-i-ka-lewa*, "the chief of the southern expanse".
- **Navajo:** *Maʼii Bizòʼ*, the "Coyote Star". In the story, Coyote helped place the stars, and put this one directly
  south, naming it after himself.
- **The ǃKung of the Kalahari:** Canopus and Capella were the horns of the Pleiades, and their appearance marked the end
  of the dry season.

??? note "Sources"
    - Suhayl and rareness, the Bedouin of the Negev and Sinai, Suhayl against steadfast Polaris, the Kaaba's

## 7.6 The stars show the Earth is round { #round }

If the Earth were flat, everyone would see the same stars. They do not, and people worked out what that meant.

- **Aristotle**, around 330 BC, noted that some stars seen in Egypt and near Cyprus are not seen further north, so
  the Earth must be "a sphere of no great size" (history book,
  [chapter 7, section 7.2](../history/07-Greece-and-Alexandria.md#round)).
- In 1153 the philosopher **Ibn Rushd** travelled from Córdoba in al-Andalus to Marrakesh in Morocco to see Canopus,
  which never rises at Córdoba, and used it to make the same argument.
- Around 90 to 120 BC the Greek **Posidonius** went further and measured. Canopus stood higher at Alexandria than at
  Rhodes. From the difference in its height and the distance between the two cities, he worked out the size of the
  Earth, perhaps to within 4%.
- In 724 the monk **Yi Xing** led a survey for the Tang court, with thirteen stations from about 17°N in what is
  now Vietnam to about 50°N near Lake Baikal. At each one, observers measured the height of the pole. The results gave
  a fairly good length for one degree of latitude (this site's history book,
  [chapter 4, section 4.9](../history/04-China.md#machines)).
- About a century later, in 827, the caliph **al-Maʾmun** had another arc of the Earth measured.

The oldest of these measurements used the Sun's shadow rather than the stars: Eratosthenes compared noon shadows in
Egypt around 240 BC (history book, [chapter 7, section 7.5](../history/07-Greece-and-Alexandria.md#eratosthenes)).
Section 7.9 shows how Posidonius' method works.

## 7.7 Finding latitude at sea { #sea }

If the height of the pole is your latitude, then a sailor who can measure the height of Polaris knows how far north
the ship is. Arab navigators did this with a simple tool, the **kamal**.

A kamal is a small wooden card, about 5 cm by 2.5 cm, with a knotted string through its middle. The navigator holds
the end of the string in their teeth and the card out in front, with the string pulled straight. They slide the card
until its lower edge sits on the horizon and its upper edge just covers Polaris. The number of knots between teeth
and card gives the angle. A navigator heading for a known port could tie a special knot for that port's latitude.

The kamal was used by Arab navigators from the late 9th century and in the Indian Ocean from the 10th. It allowed the
earliest known **latitude sailing**, and was an early step towards measuring, rather than guessing, at sea.

Pacific navigators also judged latitude from the heights of stars, and they too sailed "down the latitude" of a known
island before turning towards it (history book,
[chapter 12, section 12.2](../history/12-Oceania-and-Australia.md#wayfinding)).

??? note "Sources"

      and the upper on Polaris, counting knots or tying a knot for a known latitude; Arab navigators of the late 9th
      century and the Indian Ocean from the 10th; the earliest known latitude sailing: Wikipedia,

    - Polynesian stellar elevation for latitude and "sailing down the latitude": Wikipedia,

## 7.8 Latitude on Mars { #mars }

The rule works on any spinning planet: the height of the celestial pole equals your latitude. Mars is tilted by
**25.2°**, close to the Earth's 23.4°, but its poles point to different stars (chapter 6, section 6.8).

- **Perseverance**, in Jezero crater at **18.4°N**, sees the north celestial pole of Mars 18.4° above its northern
  horizon, in Cygnus. Deneb, less than 10° from that pole, never sets there.
- **Curiosity**, in Gale crater at **4.5°S**, sees the south celestial pole of Mars just 4.5° above its southern
  horizon. Kappa Velorum, about 2.7° from that pole, circles it without setting, though at its lowest it is under 2° up.

Mars is about half the size of the Earth, so a degree of latitude is shorter: about **59 km**, against 111 km. A rover
that drove 59 km north would see the pole climb by 1°.

## 7.9 Working it out { #working }

**Which stars can you see?** At latitude $\varphi$ north, a star of declination $\delta$ rises at some time of the
year only if $\delta > -(90^\circ - \varphi)$ (chapter 6, section 6.9). Turn it round to find the furthest north a star
can be seen:

$$\varphi_{\max} = 90^\circ + \delta.$$

| Sight | Declination | Seen south of |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Canopus | −52.7° | $90 - 52.7 = 37.3^\circ$N |
| Southern Cross, lowest star | −64.7° | $90 - 64.7 = 25.3^\circ$N |
| Large Magellanic Cloud | about −70° | about $20^\circ$N |

All three agree with figure B. (These are simple geometry; near the horizon, refraction lifts stars by about half a
degree, chapter 2, section 2.11, so in practice they can be glimpsed a little further north.)

**How much of the sky?** The part of the sky you can ever see from latitude $\varphi$ is everything except a cap of
radius $\varphi$ round the hidden pole. Working out the area of that cap gives

$$\text{fraction of the whole sky you can ever see} = \frac{1 + \sin \varphi}{2}, \qquad \text{with } \varphi \text{ taken as positive.}$$

At the equator, $\varphi = 0$: all of it. At 45°: $(1 + 0.707)/2 \approx 85\%$. At the pole: half.

**Posidonius' method.** If a star's height changes by $\Delta h$ between two places a distance $d$ apart along a
north-south line, the Earth's circumference is

$$C = d \times \frac{360^\circ}{\Delta h}.$$

With today's figures, 1° of latitude on the Earth is 111.2 km, so $C = 111.2 \times 360 \approx 40{,}000$ km.

| | Mars | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Mean radius | 3,389.5 km | 6,371 km |
| One degree of latitude, $2\pi R / 360$ | 59.2 km | 111.2 km |
| Tilt of the axis | 25.2° | 23.4° |
| Midnight Sun north of about | $90 - 25.2 = 64.8^\circ$ | $90 - 23.4 = 66.6^\circ$ (65.7° with refraction) |
| Rover or city example | Perseverance, 18.4°N: pole 18.4° up | 45°N: Polaris 45° up |

**Kappa Velorum from Gale.** Mars's south celestial pole is at right ascension 9 h 10 min 42 s, declination
−52° 53.0′; Kappa Velorum is at 9 h 22 min 07 s, −55° 00.6′. The difference in right ascension is 11.4 minutes of
time, or $11.4 \times 0.25 = 2.85^\circ$ of arc along the equator, which at declination about −54° shrinks to
$2.85 \times \cos 54^\circ \approx 1.68^\circ$. The difference in declination is 2.13°. So the distance is
$\sqrt{1.68^2 + 2.13^2} \approx 2.7^\circ$, less than Curiosity's latitude of 4.5°: the star never sets there.

## 7.10 The Earth is not quite round { #shape }

Measure the length of one degree of latitude carefully, using the stars to fix each end, and you find it is **not the
same everywhere**:

| Latitude | Length of 1° of latitude |
| --- | --- |
| 0° (equator) | 110.574 km |
| 45° | 111.132 km |
| 90° (pole) | 111.694 km |

A longer degree means the ground curves more gently there. So the Earth is **flatter near the poles** and bulges at
the equator. If the Earth were a perfect sphere, every degree would be the same length.

This was a real argument in the early 1700s. Isaac Newton and Christiaan Huygens had argued from theory that a
spinning Earth should be flattened at the poles. The French astronomer Jacques Cassini, after earlier measurements in
France, held the opposite: that the Earth is stretched towards the poles. King Louis XV and the French Academy of
Sciences sent two expeditions to settle it:

- one to the **Torne Valley in Lapland**, near the Arctic Circle, led by Pierre Louis Maupertuis with the Swedish
  physicist Anders Celsius;
- one to the **equator** in what is now **Ecuador**, led by Charles Marie de La Condamine, Pierre Bouguer and Louis
  Godin with the Spanish geographers Jorge Juan and Antonio de Ulloa. It left France in May 1735, reached Quito in
  June 1736, and had measured three degrees of the meridian by 1739.

The Lapland team finished first, and their result showed the Earth is flattened at the poles. Newton was right. The
Ecuador results were later used, with a French survey, in defining the **metre** as one ten-millionth of the distance
from the pole to the equator.

**How flat?** Turn each length of a degree into the radius of a circle that curves that much:
$R = (\text{length of } 1^\circ) \times 180/\pi$.

$$R_{\text{equator}} = 110.574 \times 57.30 \approx 6{,}335 \text{ km}, \qquad R_{\text{pole}} = 111.694 \times 57.30 \approx 6{,}400 \text{ km}.$$

The Earth curves like a smaller ball, 6,335 km in radius, along the meridian at the equator, and like a bigger one,
6,400 km, at the poles. The modern **flattening**, (equatorial radius − polar radius) ÷ equatorial radius, is 0.00335,
about 1 part in 300. Mars is
flatter still: its flattening is 0.00589, about 1 part in 170, with an equatorial radius of 3,396.2 km and a polar
radius of 3,376.2 km.

So the stars do more than show that the Earth is round. Measured carefully enough, they show its exact shape.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) You see Polaris 30° above the horizon. What is your latitude?

??? note "Answer"
    About 30° north.

**2.** (beginner) How do the stars move as seen from the North Pole?

??? note "Answer"
    They circle level with the horizon. None rises or sets, and only the northern half of the sky is ever seen.

**3.** (beginner) Why can you see every star in the sky from the equator over a year?

??? note "Answer"
    Both celestial poles are on the horizon, so no part of the sky is hidden for ever. Every star is up for half of
    each day, and over the year each one rises at night at some time.

**4.** (intermediate) Why did the Arabic name *Suhayl* come to suggest rareness?

??? note "Answer"
    Canopus was low and seen only part of the year in the Middle East, and further north it never rose at all.

**5.** (intermediate) How did Ibn Rushd's trip to Marrakesh help show that the Earth is round?

??? note "Answer"
    Canopus never rises at Córdoba but could be seen from Marrakesh, further south. On a flat Earth everyone would see
    the same stars; on a round Earth, going south brings new southern stars into view.

**6.** (intermediate) How does a kamal measure latitude?

??? note "Answer"
    With the string held in the teeth, the card is slid along it until its lower edge is on the horizon and its upper
    edge on Polaris. The knots show the angle, which is the height of Polaris and so the latitude.

**7.** (advanced) How far north can a star at declination −60° be seen? What fraction of the sky can you ever see from
30°N?

??? note "Answer"
    Up to $90 - 60 = 30^\circ$N. From 30°N: $(1 + \sin 30^\circ)/2 = (1 + 0.5)/2 = 75\%$.

**8.** (expert) One degree of latitude is 110.574 km at the equator and 111.694 km at the pole. What does that tell you
about the Earth's shape, and how?

??? note "Answer"
    A longer degree means the meridian curves more gently. Turning each into a radius of curvature gives about
    6,335 km at the equator and 6,400 km at the pole, so the Earth is flatter at the poles and bulges at the equator:
    an oblate shape, as Newton predicted and the Lapland expedition confirmed.

## Summary

- The height of the celestial pole above your horizon equals your latitude.
- At the North Pole the stars circle level with the horizon and only half the sky is seen; at the equator every star
  rises and sets straight up and down, and the whole sky can be seen over a year.
- Going south, Canopus rises south of 37.3°N, the whole Southern Cross south of 25°N, the Large Magellanic Cloud south
  of 20°N.
- Canopus had names across the world, from Suhayl to the Old Man of the South Pole and the Coyote Star.
- Aristotle, Ibn Rushd, Posidonius, Yi Xing and al-Maʾmun used the stars at different latitudes to show the Earth is
  round and to measure it.
- The kamal measured the height of Polaris, and so latitude, with a card and a knotted string.
- On Mars a degree of latitude is 59 km; from Perseverance's site the pole is 18.4° up, and from Curiosity's Kappa
  Velorum never sets.
- Degrees of latitude are longer near the poles, so the Earth is flattened; the Lapland and Ecuador expeditions
  proved it in the 1730s.
