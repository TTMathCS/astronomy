# 19. Binoculars and a first telescope

*Your eyes alone can see a few thousand stars. A pair of binoculars shows far more, and the moons of Jupiter and the
craters of the Moon; a small telescope shows Saturn's rings and the polar cap of Mars. This chapter explains what the
numbers on binoculars mean, the three main kinds of telescope, why the width of the opening matters more than the
magnification, which mount to choose, how to spend a first night with a telescope, how telescopes grew from a Dutch
spyglass to the giants of today, what telescopes do at Mars, and ends by working out why no telescope on the ground
can show detail finer than the air allows.*

## 19.1 Start with binoculars { #first }

Before buying a telescope, learn the sky with your eyes and a chart (chapter 18), then add a pair of binoculars. They
are the best first instrument:

- you use both eyes, so the view is comfortable and natural;
- the view is wide, so it is easy to find things;
- they show the sky the right way up, as you see it;
- they are cheap, and useful by day too.

Even ordinary binoculars show the four large moons of Jupiter (chapter 13), craters on the Moon, many more stars in
the Pleiades than your eyes can count, and the Andromeda Galaxy (chapter 1) more easily than with the eye alone.

    - Jupiter's four large moons in ordinary binoculars: chapter 13, section 13.4, and its sources.
    - The rest is common practical advice.

## 19.2 What the numbers mean { #numbers }

Binoculars are labelled with two numbers, such as **7×50** or **10×50**:

- the first is the **magnification**: 7×50 binoculars make things look 7 times closer;
- the second is the width of each front lens, the **objective**, in millimetres.

The bigger the objective, the more light it collects and the fainter the things you can see. The higher the
magnification, the narrower the view and the more any shaking of your hands shows up. Above about 10×, binoculars
are hard to hold steady, and the big 15× to 25× binoculars made for astronomy need a tripod.

**Exit pupil.** Divide the objective by the magnification and you get the width of the beam of light that leaves the
eyepiece, the **exit pupil**. For 7×50 it is $50 / 7 = 7.1$ mm, about the width of the pupil of a young eye in the
dark, so all the light gets in. Older eyes do not open as wide, so for them 10×50 binoculars, with a 5 mm exit pupil,
waste less light.

??? note "Sources"
    - Binoculars described as magnification × objective diameter, such as 7×50, magnification as the ratio of the
      focal lengths of objective and eyepiece, a larger magnification giving a smaller field and perhaps needing a
      tripod, and astronomical binoculars of 15× to 25×: Wikipedia,
      [Binoculars](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Binoculars) (weaker).
    - 7×50 binoculars with an exit pupil just over 7.14 mm, the average pupil size of a youthful dark-adapted eye:
      Wikipedia, [Exit pupil](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Exit_pupil) (weaker).
    - "Above about 10×" is common advice; the 5 mm exit pupil of 10×50 is our arithmetic.

- A **refractor** has a lens at the front. Refractors are sealed and need little care. Simple lenses bring different
  colours to slightly different focuses, which gives bright objects a coloured fringe; better lenses made of two or
  more pieces of glass correct most of it. A good refractor costs more for its size than a reflector.
- A **reflector** has a curved mirror at the back. In Isaac Newton's design, a small flat mirror near the front sends
  the light out to an eyepiece at the side. Mirrors have no colour fringes and are cheap to make large, so a reflector
  gives the most aperture for the money.
- A **Schmidt–Cassegrain** folds the light path with two mirrors and a thin glass plate at the front. The tube is short
  and easy to carry for its size. This design is very popular with makers.

## 19.4 Aperture matters most { #aperture }

The most important number for any telescope is its **aperture**, the width of its main lens or mirror. A bigger
aperture does two things:

- it **collects more light**, in proportion to its area, so it shows fainter things;
- it shows **finer detail**, because the sharpness of the image is limited by the aperture (section 19.10).

**Magnification** is the least important number. It depends only on which eyepiece you use, and you can change it.
Too much magnification just makes a dim, blurred image bigger. A rule of thumb is that the most a telescope can use is
about **twice its aperture in millimetres**: about 120× for a 60 mm telescope, 400× for 200 mm. Beware of a small
telescope sold as "600 power"; it cannot give a sharp image at that.

      Wikipedia, [Magnification](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Magnification) (weaker).
    - Light collected going with the area, and the numbers in Figure B: section 19.9. Resolution: section 19.10.

## 19.5 Mounts { #mounts }

A telescope is only as good as the stand it sits on. A shaky mount makes every star dance.

- An **altazimuth** mount moves up and down and round, like a camera tripod head. It is simple and quick. The
  **Dobsonian**, made popular by John Dobson in 1965, is a Newtonian reflector on a simple altazimuth box, often of
  plywood. It gives the most aperture for the money and is a popular first telescope.
- An **equatorial** mount has one axis set parallel to the Earth's axis, pointing at the celestial pole (chapter 6). To
  follow a star as the sky turns, you need to turn only that one axis, at a steady rate, by hand or with a motor.
- A **GoTo** mount has motors on both axes, run by a small computer. After you align it, you choose an object from its
  list, or type in a right ascension and declination (chapter 17), and it points there.

??? note "Sources"
    - The Dobsonian, an altazimuth-mounted Newtonian popularised by John Dobson in 1965, often built of plywood and
      other cheap materials, and credited with vastly increasing the size of amateur telescopes: Wikipedia,
      [Dobsonian telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Dobsonian_telescope) (weaker).
    - The equatorial mount with its polar axis parallel to the Earth's axis, tracking by driving one axis at a constant
      speed: Wikipedia, [Equatorial mount](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Equatorial_mount) (weaker).
    - GoTo mounts with both axes driven by motors under computer control, aligned before use, pointing at coordinates
      or at objects from a built-in list: Wikipedia,
      [GoTo (telescopes)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/GoTo_(telescopes)) (weaker).

## 19.6 Your first night { #night }

- **Never point it at the Sun.** Not for a moment, not at sunset, not with sunglasses. A telescope or binoculars
  aimed at the Sun can blind you instantly. Only a proper solar filter fixed over the front makes it safe
  (chapter 12, section 12.4). Keep a cap on the finder too.
- **Line up the finder by day.** Point the telescope at a distant treetop or chimney, then adjust the small finder
  until it points at the same place.
- **Let it cool.** Take the telescope outside half an hour early. A warm mirror stirs the air in the tube and blurs the
  view.
- **Start with low power**, the eyepiece with the longest focal length. Find the object, centre it, then try more
  power.
- **Start with the Moon**, then a bright planet. Look near the line between light and dark on the Moon, where shadows
  are long.
- **Be patient.** The air shimmers; wait for the moments when it steadies, and details snap into view.

??? note "Sources"
    - Sun safety: chapter 12, section 12.4, and its sources.
    - The rest is common practical advice.

## 19.7 From spyglass to giant { #history }

- On **2 October 1608** Hans Lipperhey, a spectacle maker in Middelburg, asked for a patent on a telescope (history
  book, [chapter 15, section 15.1](../history/15-Galileo-and-the-Telescope.md#invention)).
- In **1609** Galileo built his own and turned it to the sky; his telescopes magnified up to about 30 times (history
  book, [chapter 15, section 15.2](../history/15-Galileo-and-the-Telescope.md#galileo)).
- In **1668** Isaac Newton built the first working reflecting telescope (history book,
  [chapter 16, section 16.1](../history/16-Newton-Halley-and-Romer.md#newton)).
- In **1831** in Japan the gunsmith Kunitomo Ikkansai built a reflector that magnified 60 times (history book,
  [chapter 15, section 15.7](../history/15-Galileo-and-the-Telescope.md#world)).
- In **1965** John Dobson's simple mounts put big reflectors within reach of ordinary people.
- Today's giant telescopes have mirrors many metres across (history book,
  [chapter 22, section 22.1](../history/22-Astronomy-Today.md#giants)).

## 19.8 Telescopes and Mars { #mars }

**Your telescope and Mars.** A small telescope at a good opposition shows Mars as a small reddish disc, with perhaps
a white polar cap and dark markings (chapter 14, section 14.4). At the opposition of February 2027 Mars will be only
about 14″ across, so you will need a magnification of 150× or more, and steady air.

**A telescope at Mars.** The biggest telescope ever sent into deep space is at Mars. **HiRISE**, on NASA's Mars
Reconnaissance Orbiter, is a reflector with a mirror 0.5 m across, in orbit since 2006. From about 300 km up, each
pixel covers 0.3 m of ground, so it can see things smaller than a metre. It has even photographed the Earth and the
Moon as crescents (history book, [chapter 15, section 15.8](../history/15-Galileo-and-the-Telescope.md#onmars)).

??? note "Sources"
    - Mars in a small telescope and its size of about 14″ in February 2027: chapter 14, section 14.4, and its sources.
      The 150× is our arithmetic: at 150×, 14″ looks like 35′, a little more than the full Moon looks to the eye.
    - HiRISE: a 0.5 m reflector, the largest of any deep-space mission, 0.3 m per pixel from 300 km, resolving objects
      below a metre: Wikipedia, [HiRISE](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/HiRISE) (weaker), and this site's history book,
      chapter 15, section 15.8.

## 19.9 Working it out { #working }

**Magnification.** Divide the focal length of the telescope by the focal length of the eyepiece. A telescope of
1,200 mm focal length with a 25 mm eyepiece magnifies $1200 / 25 = 48$ times; with a 6 mm eyepiece, 200 times.

**Exit pupil.** Aperture ÷ magnification. A 200 mm telescope at 48× gives $200 / 48 = 4.2$ mm; at 200× it gives 1 mm.

**Light grasp.** Light collected goes with the area, so with the square of the width. Compared with a 7 mm pupil:

$$\left(\frac{50}{7}\right)^2 = 51, \qquad \left(\frac{114}{7}\right)^2 = 265, \qquad
\left(\frac{200}{7}\right)^2 = 816.$$

**The faintest star.** Five magnitudes is a factor of 100 in brightness (chapter 4), so a gain in light of $G$ is
$2.5 \log_{10} G$ magnitudes. For the 200 mm telescope, $2.5 \log_{10} 816 = 7.3$ magnitudes. If your eye alone reaches
magnitude 6 under a dark sky, the telescope should reach about $6 + 7.3 = 13$, in theory; in practice it depends on
the sky and on your eye.

**Finest detail.** Dawes' limit gives the smallest angle a telescope can split, in seconds of arc, as $116 / D$, with
$D$ the aperture in millimetres: 2.3″ for 50 mm, 1.0″ for 114 mm, 0.58″ for 200 mm.

**HiRISE and your telescope compared.** An angle in seconds of arc, times the distance, divided by 206,265, gives the
size of the smallest detail.

| | Mars | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Telescope | HiRISE, in orbit round Mars | a 200 mm telescope in a garden |
| Aperture | 0.5 m | 0.2 m |
| Distance to the surface of Mars | about 300 km | about 101 million km in February 2027 |
| Finest angle | $116 / 500 = 0.23″$ | $116 / 200 = 0.58″$, if the air is steady |
| Smallest detail on Mars | under 1 m (0.3 m pixels) | $101{,}000{,}000 \times 0.58 / 206{,}265 \approx 280$ km |

## 19.10 Why the air sets the limit { #seeing }

**Diffraction.** Light is a wave, and a wave passing through a round opening spreads a little. Even a perfect
telescope turns a star into a tiny disc with faint rings round it. The angle of that disc, in radians, is about

$$\theta \approx 1.22\,\frac{\lambda}{D},$$

where λ is the wavelength of the light and $D$ the aperture. For green light, λ = 550 nm = $5.5 \times 10^{-7}$ m, and
a 200 mm telescope:

$$\theta = 1.22 \times \frac{5.5 \times 10^{-7}}{0.2}
= 3.4 \times 10^{-6} \text{ rad} = 3.4 \times 10^{-6} \times 206{,}265″
= 0.69″.$$

Two stars closer than about this cannot be told apart. Dawes found by testing that 116/D, here 0.58″, works for
experienced observers; it matches the formula for bluish light of about 460 nm. Either way, the bigger the aperture,
the finer the detail: double the aperture, halve the angle.

**Seeing.** But the light has also come through the Earth's air, which is always stirring. Pockets of warm and cool
air bend the light this way and that, which is what makes stars twinkle (chapter 4). Astronomers call the blurring
**seeing**. A star blurred to less than 0.4″ counts as excellent seeing, and even getting below 1″ is hard. The best
sites, such as Mauna Kea in Hawaii and La Palma in the Canary Islands, are on high mountains on small islands.

So on most nights the air, not the telescope, sets the limit. A 200 mm telescope's 0.6″ is often wasted; a bigger
telescope still collects more light, but it shows no finer detail unless the air is steady. That is why
professional astronomers build on mountaintops, or go above the air altogether, as HiRISE does at Mars.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) What do the numbers 10×50 on a pair of binoculars mean?

??? note "Answer"
    They magnify 10 times, and each front lens is 50 mm across.

**2.** (beginner) Why are binoculars a good first instrument?

??? note "Answer"
    They use both eyes, give a wide, right-way-up view that makes things easy to find, are cheap, and are useful by day
    too.

**3.** (beginner) What is the one rule you must never break with binoculars or a telescope?

??? note "Answer"
    Never point them at the Sun unless a proper solar filter is fixed over the front.

**4.** (intermediate) Why is a telescope advertised as "600 power" with a 60 mm lens a bad sign?

??? note "Answer"
    The most a telescope can usefully magnify is about twice its aperture in millimetres, here about 120×. At 600× the
    image would be dim and blurred.

**5.** (intermediate) What is the advantage of an equatorial mount?

??? note "Answer"
    One axis points at the celestial pole, so you can follow a star as the sky turns by moving only that axis, at a
    steady rate.

**6.** (intermediate) Why is a Dobsonian a popular first telescope?

??? note "Answer"
    It is a Newtonian reflector on a simple, cheap, steady altazimuth mount, so it gives a lot of aperture for the
    money and is quick to use.

**7.** (advanced) A telescope of 900 mm focal length and 114 mm aperture is used with a 10 mm eyepiece. Find the
magnification and the exit pupil. Is it within the useful limit?

??? note "Answer"
    $900 / 10 = 90$×. Exit pupil $114 / 90 = 1.3$ mm. The useful limit is about $2 \times 114 = 228$×, so 90× is fine.

**8.** (expert) What is the diffraction limit of HiRISE's 0.5 m mirror in green light, and how small a thing could it
see from 300 km?

??? note "Answer"
    $\theta = 1.22 \times 5.5 \times 10^{-7} / 0.5 = 1.34 \times 10^{-6}$ rad. From 300 km, that is $300{,}000 \times
    1.34 \times 10^{-6} = 0.40$ m. There is no air to blur it, and its pixels of 0.3 m are matched to that.

## Summary

- Learn the sky by eye, then with binoculars: 7×50 or 10×50 are good choices.
- The numbers mean magnification × aperture in millimetres; aperture ÷ magnification is the exit pupil.
- Refractors use a lens, reflectors a mirror, Schmidt–Cassegrains both.
- Aperture matters most: it sets how faint and how fine you can see. Magnification is set by the eyepiece, and about
  2× the aperture in millimetres is the useful limit.
- Dobsonians are simple and give the most aperture for the money; equatorial mounts follow the stars; GoTo mounts find
  them for you.
- Never look at the Sun without a proper solar filter.
- The telescope was invented in 1608; Newton built the first reflector in 1668.
- HiRISE at Mars sees detail under a metre; a garden telescope sees about 280 km on Mars at best.
- Diffraction limits detail to about $1.22\lambda/D$, but on most nights the air limits it more.
