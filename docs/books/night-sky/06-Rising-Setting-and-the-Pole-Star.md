# 6. Rising, setting and the pole star

*Stay out for an hour and the sky will not be where you left it. Stars climb in the east, sink in the west, and in the
north they wheel round one nearly still point. This chapter follows that turning: which stars never set, where each
star rises and sets, and why every star rises four minutes earlier each night. It visits the peoples who named the
still star and steered by the rising ones, and it watches the sky of Mars, where one moon rises in the west. It ends
with the slowest motion of all, the one that is moving the pole away from Polaris.*

## 6.1 The sky turns { #turning }

Pick a bright star low in the east and note where it is against a tree or a roof. Look again an hour later and it will
have climbed. A star in the west will have sunk lower. The whole sky turns together, like a great bowl, at about
**15° an hour**, a full turn in about a day.

It is not the sky that moves, of course, but the Earth, spinning from west to east. Because we turn towards the east,
everything in the sky seems to go the other way: it **rises in the east and sets in the west**. This is true of the
Sun, the Moon, the planets and the stars alike.

Which way the stars seem to move depends on which way you face. In the northern hemisphere:

- **Facing south**, the stars move from left to right, rising on your left and setting on your right.
- **Facing north**, the stars wheel **anticlockwise** round the pole star: rightwards below it, leftwards above it.

In the southern hemisphere it is the mirror image: facing south, the stars turn **clockwise** round the south
celestial pole, near the faint star Sigma Octantis.

## 6.2 The still point { #still }

Imagine the Earth's axis carried on out into space. Its northern end meets the sky at the **north celestial pole**,
and that point does not move as the Earth spins. Every star in the sky circles round it.

Polaris is almost exactly there. In 2018 it was **0.66°** from the pole, about 1.4 times the width of the full
Moon, so it moved round a circle only **1.3°** across each day. That is too small to notice without care, so Polaris
seems to stand still while everything else wheels round it.

Two more things make Polaris useful:

- **It shows north.** Twice a day it is exactly due north; the rest of the time it is a little east or west of north.
- **It shows your latitude.** The height of Polaris above the horizon is about equal to your latitude. From 45°N it
  stands 45° up. Chapter 7 follows this from the equator to the pole.

??? note "Sources"
    - Polaris nearly in line with the Earth's axis, the northern stars turning round it, 0.66° from the pole in 2018,
      1.4 times the Moon's disc, the 1.3° circle, due north twice each sidereal day, and its height giving the
      observer's approximate latitude: Wikipedia, [Polaris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polaris) (weaker).

## 6.3 Stars that never set { #circumpolar }

Stars close to the pole move in small circles, and if the circle is small enough it never dips below the horizon.
Such a star is **circumpolar**: from where you stand, it never sets. You can see it on every clear night of the year.

How close is close enough? Picture a circle round the pole whose radius, in degrees, equals your **latitude**. Every
star inside that circle is circumpolar.

- **At 45°N**, every star within 45° of the pole never sets. The whole Big Dipper is circumpolar there.
- **At the equator** your latitude is 0°, so the circle shrinks to nothing. No star is circumpolar, and every star is
  above the horizon for half of each day.
- **At the North Pole** your latitude is 90°: every star you can see is circumpolar, and none ever rises or sets.

The same rule works the other way for stars that you can **never** see. From 45°N, any star within 45° of the south
celestial pole never rises; the Southern Cross is one of them.

The **Arctic** is named after these turning stars. Before it meant the region inside the Arctic Circle, it meant the
lands where the Bears, Ursa Major and Ursa Minor, stood high in the sky. The word comes from the Greek *arktos*,
"bear".

## 6.4 Where stars rise and set { #rising }

A star rises at the **same point** on the horizon every night, and sets at the same point. Only the time changes. That
point depends on how far north or south the star is in the sky.

- The **celestial equator** is the circle in the sky straight above the Earth's equator. It meets the horizon **due
  east and due west**, from anywhere on Earth. A star on it rises due east, sets due west, and spends half the day
  above the horizon.
- A star north of the celestial equator rises north of east and sets north of west. Seen from the northern
  hemisphere it stays up **more** than half the day.
- A star south of it rises south of east, sets south of west, and stays up **less** than half the day: it makes only a
  short, low arc across the south.

From the southern hemisphere it is the other way round.

The angle at which stars rise also depends on where you are. Near the equator they rise almost straight up out of the
horizon. Further north or south they rise at a slant, and near the poles they slide along the horizon.

??? note "Sources"
    - The celestial equator meeting the horizon due east and due west everywhere, and tilting as the observer moves
      north or south: Wikipedia, [Celestial equator](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Celestial_equator) (weaker).
    - Stars rising and setting at fixed points, with only the time changing through the year, and rising nearly
      straight up within 20° of the equator: Wikipedia,
      [Polynesian navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesian_navigation) (weaker).
    - Which stars stay up longer follows from figure B and from the celestial equator meeting the horizon east and west;
      chapter 7 treats the slant of rising at different latitudes.

## 6.5 Four minutes earlier each night { #earlier }

Watch the same star rise on two nights in a row and you will find it comes up about **4 minutes earlier** the second
night. Why?

A day by the clock is the time from noon to noon, when the Sun is back in the same place. But while the Earth spins
once, it also moves about 1° along its orbit round the Sun. So to bring the Sun back to the same place, the Earth has
to turn a little more than once. A turn measured against the **stars** is shorter: **23 hours 56 minutes 4 seconds**.
This is the **sidereal day**, from the Latin *sidus*, star.

Four minutes a night adds up:

- after a week, a star rises about half an hour earlier;
- after a month, about **2 hours** earlier;
- after a year, the 4 minutes have added up to a whole day, and the star is back where it began.

That is why the evening sky changes with the seasons: each month the evening stars have moved on by about 2 hours,
and new ones have risen in the east to take their place.

Each star also spends some weeks of the year hidden in the Sun's glare. The morning it can first be seen again, low in
the east just before sunrise, is called its **heliacal rising**. Peoples everywhere used it as a calendar:

- In Egypt, the heliacal rising of **Sirius** was a key date of the year (this site's history book,
  [chapter 3, section 3.3](../history/03-Egypt.md#sirius)).
- In Greece, the rising of the **Pleiades** at dawn marked the start of the sailing season and the farming year, as
  the poet Hesiod wrote in his *Works and Days*.
- In Aotearoa New Zealand, the dawn return of the Pleiades, **Matariki**, marks the Māori new year (history book,
  [chapter 12, section 12.5](../history/12-Oceania-and-Australia.md#matariki)).

??? note "Sources"
    - The sidereal day of 23 h 56 min 4.0905 s, the Earth moving about 1° along its orbit, the solar day being nearly
      4 minutes longer, and *sidus*: Wikipedia, [Sidereal time](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sidereal_time) (weaker).
      The arithmetic of a week, a month and a year is in section 6.9.
    - Heliacal rising, the Sun drifting about 1° a day against the stars, Sirius and the Egyptian calendar, and the

    - Matariki: this site's history book, chapter 12, section 12.5, and its sources.

## 6.6 Watching the sky turn { #trails }

You can see the sky turn for yourself in several ways.

- **Use a fixed edge.** Stand where a star is just beside a chimney, a pole or the edge of a roof, and keep your head
  in the same place. Within a few minutes you will see the star move away from the edge.
- **Use the Dipper as a clock hand.** Note where the Big Dipper is at the start of the evening and look again three
  hours later. It will have turned about 45° round Polaris (figure A). Chapter 9 turns this into a way of telling the
  time.
- **Take a star-trail photograph.** Put a camera on a tripod, point it at the pole and leave the shutter open, using
  the "Bulb" setting, for anything from 15 minutes to several hours. Each star draws an arc. Every arc is centred on
  the pole, and in one hour each arc is 15°, a twenty-fourth of a circle.

??? note "Sources"
    - Star trails as long exposures of diurnal circles, exposures from 15 minutes to many hours, and the "Bulb" setting:
      Wikipedia, [Star trail](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Star_trail) (weaker).
    - 15° an hour: Wikipedia, [Diurnal motion](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diurnal_motion) (weaker). The 45° in three
      hours follows from it.

## 6.7 The star that sits still { #names }

A star that never moves is easy to name, and many peoples named Polaris for exactly that.

- **Lakota:** *Wičháȟpi Owáŋžila*, "the star that sits still".
- **Plains Cree:** *acâhkos êkâ kâ-âhcît*, "the star that does not move".
- **Hindu tradition:** in the Puranas the star is personified as **Dhruva**, "immovable, fixed".
- **Hawaiʻi:** *Hōkūpaʻa*, "fixed star", a name used by the Polynesian Voyaging Society.
- **Inuit:** *Nuutuittuq*. **Mi'kmaq:** *Tatapn*.
- **China:** Polaris was one star of *Gouchen*, the "Curved Array", with three other stars of the Little Dipper and two
  in Cepheus.

The name **Polaris** itself is fairly young. The Latin *stella polaris*, "polar star", was coined in the Renaissance
and first appeared in print in the Alfonsine Tables of 1492. In ancient times Polaris was not yet the nearest bright
star to the pole (section 6.10), and sailors used the whole of the Little Dipper instead. The Greek philosopher Thales
was said to have "measured the stars of the Wagon by which the Phoenicians sail". Its two bright bowl stars, Kochab
and Pherkad, have been called the **guardians of the pole**.

**Steering by rising stars.** Near the equator Polaris is low or hidden, and Pacific navigators used rising and setting
stars instead. Because each star always rises at the same point on the horizon, a navigator could steer for a star as
it rose, and when it climbed too high, switch to the next star rising at the same point. Laid out round the horizon,
these points make a **star compass**, with a few dozen stars and sometimes as many as 150. Navigators also judged
their latitude from how high certain stars climbed. The history book tells the story in
[chapter 12, section 12.2](../history/12-Oceania-and-Australia.md#wayfinding).

??? note "Sources"
    - The Lakota, Plains Cree, Inuit, Mi'kmaq, Hawaiian, Hindu and Chinese names; *stella polaris* and the Alfonsine
      Tables of 1492; Ursa Minor used for navigation in antiquity rather than one star: Wikipedia,
      [Polaris](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polaris) (weaker).
    - Thales and the Phoenicians, and Kochab and Gamma Ursae Minoris (Pherkad) as the guardians of the pole: Wikipedia,
      [Ursa Minor](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ursa_Minor) (weaker).
    - Steering by rising and setting stars, star compasses of a few dozen to 150 stars, and measuring stellar elevation
      for latitude: Wikipedia, [Polynesian navigation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Polynesian_navigation) (weaker).
      Switching to the next star rising at the same point: this site's history book, chapter 12, section 12.2.

## 6.8 The turning sky of Mars { #mars }

Mars spins too, so its sky turns just as ours does, but a little more slowly: once every **24 hours 37 minutes** against
the stars. Its poles point to different parts of the sky from ours (chapter 2, section 2.9).

- **North:** there is no bright pole star. The pole lies in Cygnus, about halfway between Deneb and Alpha Cephei, less
  than 10° from Deneb. Deneb never sets from nearly all of Mars's northern hemisphere, and the line from Sadr to Deneb
  would make a good clock hand. The pole is only a few degrees from the Milky Way, so from the northern hemisphere of
  Mars the Milky Way is always in the sky.
- **South:** Mars does better than we do. Its south pole is a couple of degrees from **Kappa Velorum**, a star of
  magnitude 2.5, far easier to see than our faint Sigma Octantis at 5.5. **Canopus**, the second-brightest star in the
  sky, never sets from most southern latitudes.

**The moons go their own ways.** Mars's inner moon, **Phobos**, goes round Mars in just **7 hours 39 minutes**, much
faster than Mars spins. It overtakes the turning of the sky, so it **rises in the west**, crosses the sky in 4 hours
15 minutes or less, and **sets in the east**, about twice a day. The outer moon, **Deimos**, takes 30.3 hours to go
round, a little slower than Mars spins, so it rises in the east and sets in the west like everything else, but it
drifts across the sky very slowly.

## 6.9 Working it out { #working }

**Which stars never set?** Write $\varphi$ for your latitude and $\delta$ for a star's declination, its angle north (+)
or south (−) of the celestial equator (chapter 17 explains it fully). The pole is $90^\circ - \delta$ from the star,
and the circumpolar circle has radius $\varphi$. So from the northern hemisphere

$$\text{never sets if } \delta \ge 90^\circ - \varphi, \qquad \text{never rises if } \delta \le -(90^\circ - \varphi).$$

When a star crosses the meridian south of the zenith, it is at its highest, at altitude

$$h_{\max} = 90^\circ - \varphi + \delta.$$

| Star | Declination | From 45°N |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Capella | +46.0° | $46.0 \ge 45$: never sets, just |
| Vega | +38.8° | sets; never sets only north of $90 - 38.8 = 51.2^\circ$N |
| Antares | −26.4° | highest $90 - 45 - 26.4 = 18.6^\circ$; never rises north of $63.6^\circ$N |

The last line agrees with chapter 5: Antares never rises north of about 64°N.

**The sidereal day.** In one year of $Y$ days by the Sun, the Earth turns once more against the stars than against the
Sun, because going round the Sun adds one extra turn. So

$$\frac{1}{T_{\text{star}}} - \frac{1}{T_{\text{Sun}}} = \frac{1}{Y}.$$

For the Earth, $T_{\text{Sun}} = 24$ h and $Y = 365.25$ days, so the difference is $24 \text{ h} / 365.25 \approx 3$
min 56 s a day: the sidereal day is 23 h 56 min 4 s. Over 30 days that is $30 \times 3.93 \approx 118$ minutes, about
2 hours.

| | Mars | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Day against the stars | 24 h 37 min 23 s | 23 h 56 min 4 s |
| Day against the Sun | 24 h 39 min 35 s | 24 h 0 min 0 s |
| A star rises earlier each night by | 2 min 13 s | 3 min 56 s |
| Sky turns, per hour | 14.6° | 15.0° |
| North pole star | none bright; Deneb under 10° away | Polaris, 0.66° away |
| South pole star | Kappa Velorum, mag 2.5 | Sigma Octantis, mag 5.5 |

Check Mars with the same formula. Its year is 686.98 Earth days, which is $686.98 \times 86{,}400 / 88{,}775 \approx
668.6$ sols. So the difference is $88{,}775 \text{ s} / 668.6 \approx 133$ s, or 2 min 13 s, just what the table
says.

**Phobos and Deimos.** A moon going round in time $P$, seen from a planet spinning in time $T$, comes back to the same
place in the sky after

$$\frac{1}{\left| \dfrac{1}{P} - \dfrac{1}{T} \right|}.$$

For Phobos, $P = 7.65$ h and $T = 24.62$ h: $1/(0.1307 - 0.0406) = 1/0.0901 \approx 11.1$ h, or 11 h 6 min, so it
rises (in the west) about twice a day. For Deimos, $P = 30.3$ h: $1/(0.0406 - 0.0330) = 1/0.0076 \approx 131$ h. That is
about 5.3 sols, so Deimos takes more than five days to go once round the sky of Mars.

## 6.10 The pole star changes { #precession }

Chapter 2 promised to explain why Polaris will not be the pole star for ever. The reason is that the Earth's axis is
not fixed in space. Like a spinning top that leans, it slowly swings round, tracing a cone whose half-width is about
**23.4°**, the tilt of the Earth. One swing takes about **26,000 years**. This is **precession**. Its discovery is
usually credited to the Greek astronomer **Hipparchus**, in the 2nd century BC (history book,
[chapter 7, section 7.6](../history/07-Greece-and-Alexandria.md#hipparchus)).

As the axis swings, the celestial pole moves round a circle in the sky, 23.4° in radius, centred on the pole of the
Sun's path, and it passes one star after another.

| Date | Nearest bright star to the north pole |
| --- | --- |
| 2830 BC | **Thuban** in Draco, less than 10′ from the pole |
| Classical Greece and Rome | none close: Kochab and Polaris both about 10° away |
| 1547 | Polaris, 3° 8′ away |
| 2018 | Polaris, 0.66° away |
| about 2100 | Polaris at its closest, about 0.45° |
| AD 4000 | Errai (Gamma Cephei) |
| AD 10,000 | Deneb, but still 7° away |
| AD 14,500 | Vega, 5° away |
| about AD 28,000 | Polaris again |

Thuban was the pole star when the Egyptians built the pyramids, and they may have used stars near the pole to line
them up with north (history book, [chapter 3, section 3.7](../history/03-Egypt.md#pyramids)). In the 11th century the
Chinese astronomer Shen Kuo (1031–1095) watched the pole star of his day for three months and found the true pole was
a little over 3° from it (history book, [chapter 4, section 4.9](../history/04-China.md#machines)).

**Can we check the speed?** Precession moves the equinoxes along the Sun's path by about **50″ a year**, one degree
in 72 years. The celestial pole goes round a circle of radius 23.4°, so it moves across the sky only
$\sin 23.4^\circ \approx 0.40$ times as fast:

$$50'' \times 0.40 \approx 20'' \text{ a year}, \qquad \text{so } 1^\circ \text{ takes about } 3600 / 20 = 180 \text{ years}.$$

Between 1547 and 2018, 471 years, the pole could therefore have moved at most $471 \times 20'' \approx 9{,}400'' \approx
2.6^\circ$. The measured change in Polaris's distance is $3^\circ 8' - 0.66^\circ \approx 2.5^\circ$. That is a little
less, as it should be, because the pole is not heading straight at Polaris but passing it to one side. Two
observations nearly five centuries apart agree with the same slow wobble.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) You face south from the northern hemisphere. Which way do the stars move?

??? note "Answer"
    From left to right: they rise on your left, in the east, and set on your right, in the west.

**2.** (beginner) What is a circumpolar star?

??? note "Answer"
    A star close enough to the celestial pole that, from where you are, it never sets. It circles the pole all night
    and can be seen on every clear night of the year.

**3.** (beginner) Why does Polaris hardly move while the other stars wheel round?

??? note "Answer"
    It is less than 1° from the north celestial pole, the point the Earth's axis points to. Everything circles that
    point, and Polaris's own circle is only about 1.3° across.

**4.** (intermediate) A star rises at 10 pm tonight. At about what time will it rise a month from now?

??? note "Answer"
    About 8 pm. It rises about 4 minutes earlier each night, and 30 nights × 4 minutes is about 2 hours.

**5.** (intermediate) Where do stars on the celestial equator rise and set, and how long are they up?

??? note "Answer"
    They rise due east and set due west, from anywhere on Earth, and they are above the horizon for half of each day.

**6.** (intermediate) Why does Phobos rise in the west?

??? note "Answer"
    Phobos goes round Mars in 7 hours 39 minutes, much faster than Mars spins (24 hours 37 minutes). It overtakes the
    turning of the sky, so it moves from west to east.

**7.** (advanced) From 50°N, is Vega (declination +38.8°) circumpolar? How high does Antares (−26.4°) get?

??? note "Answer"
    Vega would need $\delta \ge 90 - 50 = 40^\circ$, so at +38.8° it just sets. Antares reaches
    $90 - 50 - 26.4 = 13.6^\circ$, low in the south.

**8.** (expert) In 1547 Polaris was 3° 8′ from the pole; in 2018 it was 0.66°. Could precession alone explain this?
Precession moves the equinoxes 50″ a year and the Earth's tilt is 23.4°.

??? note "Answer"
    The pole moves across the sky at about $50'' \times \sin 23.4^\circ \approx 20''$ a year. In 471 years that is
    about 9,400″, or 2.6°. Polaris got 2.5° closer, slightly less than the maximum because the pole is passing to one
    side of it, not heading straight for it. So yes.

## Summary

- The Earth spins west to east, so the whole sky turns east to west at about 15° an hour.
- In the north the stars circle anticlockwise round the north celestial pole, within 1° of Polaris; in the south they
  circle clockwise round a point near faint Sigma Octantis.
- Stars within a circle round the pole whose radius equals your latitude never set; at the equator none do.
- Every star rises and sets at the same points on the horizon; stars on the celestial equator rise due east and set due
  west.
- The day against the stars is 23 h 56 min 4 s, so stars rise about 4 minutes earlier each night, 2 hours a month.
- Peoples around the world named Polaris for standing still, and Pacific navigators steered by rising stars.
- Mars's sky turns in 24 h 37 min; its south pole has a fair pole star, Kappa Velorum, and Phobos rises in the west.
- Precession swings the Earth's axis round once in about 26,000 years, so the pole star changes: Thuban, then Polaris,
  later Vega.
