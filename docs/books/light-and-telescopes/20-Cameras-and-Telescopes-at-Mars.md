# 20. Cameras and telescopes at Mars

*This book began with what light is, and has followed it through mirrors, lenses, cameras and spectrographs, out to
the invisible sky and up to telescopes in space. This last chapter brings it all to one planet. Since 1965, spacecraft
from the United States, the Soviet Union, Europe, India, the United Arab Emirates and China have carried cameras and
telescopes to Mars. They have flown past it, circled it, landed on it, driven across it, and even flown in its air.
The chapter looks at how those cameras build their pictures, how two pictures from orbit give the height of a hill, how
a camera helped a rover steer itself to a safe landing, and what the cameras on Mars see when they look up.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    **You will learn:**

    - how the first cameras at Mars worked, from Mariner 4 to Pathfinder
    - which countries have cameras orbiting Mars, and how much each can see
    - how a pushbroom camera builds a picture one line at a time
    - how two pictures taken from different angles give the height of the ground
    - what cameras the rovers, a lander's wireless camera and a helicopter have carried
    - how cameras choose landing sites, guide landings and steer rovers
    - what the cameras on Mars have seen in its sky
    - why a camera orbiting Mars has more time for each line but less light, and how the two nearly cancel

    **You need:** chapter 13 on telescope designs, chapter 14 on cameras and detectors, and chapter 19 on orbits.

    **Levels:** 20.1 and 20.2 are beginner; 20.3 to 20.7 are intermediate; 20.8 is advanced; 20.9 is expert.

## 20.1 The first eyes { #first }

- **Mariner 4, 1965.** The first close-up pictures of Mars, 21 of them and part of a 22nd, came home at 8⅓ bits a
  second, and showed craters like the Moon's (chapter 14, [section 14.8](14-From-Eye-to-Camera.md#mars)).
- **Mariner 9, 1971.** On 14 November 1971 Mariner 9 became the first spacecraft to orbit another planet, just ahead of
  the Soviet Mars 2 and Mars 3. A dust storm hid the ground for months. When it cleared, Mariner 9 photographed 85% of
  Mars in 7,329 pictures. They showed old river beds, the giant volcano Olympus Mons, and a system of canyons over
  4,000 km long, now called **Valles Marineris** after the spacecraft.
- **Mars 3, 1971.** On 2 December 1971 the Soviet Mars 3 lander made the first soft landing on Mars. It carried two
  television cameras to look all round it, but it fell silent within two minutes of landing, having sent only a grey
  picture with no detail.
- **Viking, 1976.** The two Viking landers' cameras did not take a picture all at once. Each scanned the scene all
  the way round, with a moving mirror throwing the light onto just 12 small light sensors. Three of them, behind blue,
  green and red filters, gave colour, and three more saw infrared.
- **Mars Pathfinder, 1997.** Pathfinder landed in Ares Vallis on 4 July 1997 with a stereo camera on a pole and a
  10.6 kg rover, **Sojourner**, the first rover to work beyond the Earth and the Moon. It sent 16,500 pictures.

Some of these cameras look for the finest detail. HiRISE (chapter 13, [section 13.9](13-Telescope-Designs.md#mars))
picks out things the size of a basketball. Others step back to see the whole planet:

- **MARCI**, on the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter, photographs all of Mars in five colours of visible light and two of
  ultraviolet, to make a weekly weather report.
- **India's Mars Orbiter Mission** reached Mars on 24 September 2014, making India the first Asian country to orbit
  Mars, on its first try. From its high, stretched orbit its camera took colour pictures of the whole disc. It was
  lost in 2022, after a seven-hour eclipse it was not designed to survive.
- **Hope** circles between 20,000 and 43,000 km up, once every 55 hours, an orbit chosen so that it can follow the
  weather every day, through the seasons, and compare it from place to place over the planet.

The long-lived ones have mapped almost everything. The Mars Orbiter Camera sent home over 240,000 pictures over 4.8
Martian years. By 2015 Germany's stereo camera on Mars Express had covered 70% of Mars at better than 20 m per pixel,
and 97% at better than 60 m. China's Tianwen-1 orbiter even let loose a small camera, on 31 December 2021, to take
pictures of the orbiter itself.

## 20.3 Building a picture one line at a time { #pushbroom }

A phone camera takes a whole picture at once, on a grid of pixels (chapter 14,
[section 14.5](14-From-Eye-to-Camera.md#ccd)). Many cameras in orbit work differently. They have one long row of
detectors, set across the direction of flight. At each moment the row sees one thin line of ground. As the orbiter
moves on, the next line slides into view, and line by line the picture builds up. This is called a **pushbroom**
camera, after the way a broom sweeps a floor. The moving bar in a photocopier works the same way.

HiRISE's red pictures are 20,048 pixels wide, a strip 6 km wide from 300 km up, and as long as the camera keeps
recording, at least twice the width.

There is a catch. Each line of ground passes the row in about a ten-thousandth of a second (section 20.8), too short
to gather much light. The answer is **time delay integration**. Several rows of detectors sit side by side. The charge
collected by the first row is passed to the next just as the same patch of ground moves under it, and so on, so each
patch is photographed again and again and the exposures added up (chapter 14,
[section 14.10](14-From-Eye-to-Camera.md#noise)). With it, HiRISE's red pictures typically have a signal 200 times the
noise.

Germany's camera on Mars Express has nine channels: some looking straight down, some slanting forward or back by up to
18.9°, some behind colour filters. In one pass it gathers colour and depth. Because its channels see the same place
a moment apart, its team can even make short films of Mars from a single pass.

## 20.4 Seeing in depth { #stereo }

Our two eyes see from slightly different places, and the brain joins the two views into one with depth. Cameras at
Mars do the same, with two pictures taken from different directions.

- **HiRISE** photographs the same place again on a later orbit, looking from a different angle. From the pair, the
  height of the ground can be measured to 0.25 m. These height maps were made to check possible landing sites.
- **Mars Express's stereo camera** and Europe's **CaSSIS** were both built to make maps of height. India's team used
  stereo pictures of the Valles Marineris canyons, with a height map, to measure the haze in the air over the canyon
  walls.
- **Lasers.** Mars Global Surveyor measured heights another way, timing laser pulses bounced off the ground, to map
  the whole planet.
- **On the ground.** Rover cameras come in pairs, like eyes. Curiosity has four pairs of hazard cameras and two pairs
  of navigation cameras. Pathfinder's camera was a stereo camera, and China's rover Zhurong uses its navigation and
  topography cameras to map the slopes and roughness of the ground around it.

## 20.5 Cameras on wheels and wings { #rovers }

- **Spirit and Opportunity, 2004.** Each carried a panoramic camera, Pancam, with colour filters, a navigation camera
  and wide-angle hazard cameras. Only Pancam could take colour pictures, so the panoramas were built from it.
- **Curiosity, 2012.** It carries 17 cameras: eight hazard cameras, four navigation cameras, two mast cameras, a hand
  lens camera on its arm (MAHLI), a descent camera that filmed the landing (MARDI), and the camera of ChemCam (chapter
  13). The mast, hand lens and descent cameras, from Malin Space Science Systems in San Diego, share 1600 × 1200 pixel
  CCDs with Bayer colour filters (chapter 14, [section 14.6](14-From-Eye-to-Camera.md#colour)).
- **Perseverance, 2021.** It carries 19 cameras and two microphones. Its mast cameras can zoom (chapter 10,
  [section 10.9](10-Lenses-and-Refraction.md#mars)), and SuperCam both takes pictures and reads the make-up of rocks
  from a distance, as ChemCam does (chapter 7, [section 7.7](07-Spectral-Lines.md#mars)).
- **Ingenuity, 2021.** Perseverance brought a small helicopter. On 19 April 2021 it made the first powered, controlled
  flight on another planet. Planned for five flights, it made 72 over nearly three years, until its rotor blades broke
  on landing in January 2024. It carried two off-the-shelf cameras: a black-and-white one, 640 by 480 pixels, looking
  down to steer by, and a colour one of 4,208 by 3,120 pixels for pictures.
- **Zhurong, 2021.** China's Tianwen-1 lander touched down on 14 May 2021, making China the third country, after the
  Soviet Union and the United States, to land softly on Mars and talk from its surface. Its rover, Zhurong, rolled off
  on 22 May. Its first panorama was stitched from 24 pictures, and the lander set a small wireless camera on the ground
  that took a group photograph of rover and lander together. Zhurong went to sleep on 18 May 2022 for the winter and
  the dust storms, and never woke: too much dust had settled on it.

## 20.6 Cameras that steer { #steer }

**Choosing where to land.** Before each landing, pictures from orbit are searched for safe ground: not too steep, not
too rocky. The Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter played a key part in choosing the landing sites of Phoenix in 2008,
Curiosity in 2012, InSight in 2018 and Perseverance in 2021.

**Watching a landing.** On 25 May 2008 HiRISE photographed the Phoenix lander as it came down under its parachute, a
spacecraft photographing another spacecraft at another planet.

**Landing by sight.** As Perseverance came down into Jezero Crater on 18 February 2021, its camera took pictures of
the ground below and compared them with maps it carried, to work out where it was. It could then change course at the
last minute, away from danger. This **terrain relative navigation** had been tested on Earth in 2016, on a small
rocket called Xombie.

**Driving by sight.** Rovers use their hazard cameras to find their own way round rocks (chapter 2,
[section 2.9](02-The-Speed-of-Light.md#mars)). Curiosity's wheels have a pattern of holes in them, which spells "JPL"
in Morse code; its cameras watch the tracks the pattern leaves to tell how far it has gone.

**Flying by sight.** Ingenuity's downward camera followed the texture of the ground below to keep track of where it
was going.

## 20.7 Looking up from Mars { #sky }

The cameras on Mars also look up. The history book tells of the eclipses they have filmed when Phobos crosses the Sun,
and of HiRISE photographing the Earth and the Moon (history book,
[chapter 22, section 22.8](../history/22-Astronomy-Today.md#mars)). A few more:

- **The Earth from the ground.** In early March 2004 Spirit took the first photograph of the Earth from the surface
  of another planet. In late 2005, with energy to spare, it photographed Phobos and Deimos at night.
- **Measuring the dust.** How much dust hangs in the air can be found from how much it dims the Sun. Astronomers call
  the number **tau**, the optical depth. On a clear day on Mars it is usually below 1. Opportunity's last message, on
  10 June 2018, reported tau 10.8, the highest ever measured on Mars, as a dust storm blotted out the Sun over its
  solar panels (chapter 5, [section 5.7](05-The-Inverse-Square-Law.md#mars)).
- **A comet.** On 19 October 2014 Comet Siding Spring passed 140,000 km from Mars, a third of the distance from the
  Earth to the Moon. The Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter, Mars Odyssey and MAVEN all watched it. Its nucleus turned out to
  be only 400 to 700 m across, much smaller than expected.

## 20.8 Working it out { #working }

**A ten-thousandth of a second.** An orbiter 300 km above Mars circles at distance $a = 3{,}389.5 + 300 = 3{,}689.5$
km from the centre, at speed

$$v = \sqrt{\frac{GM}{a}} = \sqrt{\frac{42{,}828}{3{,}689.5}} = 3.41 \text{ km/s}.$$

Its view slides over the ground a little more slowly, $v \times 3{,}389.5 / 3{,}689.5 = 3.13$ km/s. A line of ground
0.3 m deep therefore passes in $0.3 / 3{,}130 = 96$ millionths of a second: about 10,400 lines every second.

**A flood of numbers.** At 20,048 pixels a line, that is $2.1\times10^8$ pixels a second. HiRISE measures each pixel
with 14 bits and squeezes it to 8 as it goes, so it makes about 1.7 billion bits a second. Its memory holds 28 billion
bits, full in about 17 seconds. So HiRISE takes its pictures in short bursts: a picture 6 km wide and 12 km long takes
under 4 seconds and fills 6.4 billion bits.

**Mars and Earth.** For a camera 300 km up:

| | Mars | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Orbit speed | 3.41 km/s | 7.73 km/s |
| Speed of the view over the ground | 3.13 km/s | 7.38 km/s |
| Time for one 0.3 m line | 96 µs | 41 µs |
| One orbit | 113 minutes | 90 minutes |
| Sunlight on each square metre | 586 W | 1,361 W |
| Time for the picture to reach the Earth | 3 to 22 minutes | a fraction of a second |

Because Mars is lighter, its orbits are slower, and each line has $96 / 41 = 2.4$ times as long to collect light. But
the sunlight is only 0.43 times as strong. The two nearly cancel: $2.4 \times 0.43 \approx 1.0$. A camera at Mars
collects about as much light in each line as the same camera would at the Earth.

      398,600 km³/s² and 1,361 W/m²: NASA,
      [Earth Fact Sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/earthfact.html).

## 20.9 Height from two pictures, and light from many rows { #depth }

**Height.** Take one picture looking forward at an angle $\theta_1$ from straight down, and another looking back at
$\theta_2$. A hilltop at height $h$ above the level ground is seen along a slanting line. In the first picture that line
meets the level ground $h\tan\theta_1$ beyond the hilltop, and in the second $h\tan\theta_2$ on the other side. So,
against the ground around it, the hilltop seems to shift between the two pictures by

$$p = h\,(\tan\theta_1 + \tan\theta_2) \quad\Rightarrow\quad h = \frac{p}{\tan\theta_1 + \tan\theta_2}.$$

The bigger the angle between the views, the bigger the shift for the same height, and the finer the measurement. If a
shift can be measured to a fraction $f$ of a pixel of size $s$, the smallest height step that can be seen is

$$\Delta h = \frac{f s}{\tan\theta_1 + \tan\theta_2}.$$

With pixels of 0.3 m, views 10° forward and back ($\tan 10° + \tan 10° = 0.35$), and shifts measured to a fifth of a
pixel, $\Delta h = 0.2 \times 0.3 / 0.35 \approx 0.17$ m, the same size as the 0.25 m HiRISE achieves.

**Light from many rows.** In time delay integration with $N$ rows, each patch of ground is exposed $N$ times, so the
signal grows $N$ times. The random noise of counting photons grows only as $\sqrt{N}$ (chapter 14,
[section 14.10](14-From-Eye-to-Camera.md#noise)). The signal-to-noise ratio therefore improves by
$N / \sqrt{N} = \sqrt{N}$: 16 rows make the picture 4 times cleaner, 64 rows 8 times.

??? note "Sources"
    - HiRISE's 0.25 m: section 20.4. The views of 10° and the fifth of a pixel are examples, not HiRISE's actual
      settings.
    - The derivations are ours. Photon noise growing as the square root of the count: chapter 14.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Which spacecraft first orbited another planet, and what did it find at Mars once the dust cleared?

??? note "Answer"
    Mariner 9, in November 1971. It photographed 85% of Mars and found old river beds, the volcano Olympus Mons and the
    canyons of Valles Marineris.

**2.** (beginner) Besides the United States, name three countries or groups whose cameras have orbited Mars.

??? note "Answer"
    Any three of: Europe (Mars Express and, with Russia, the Trace Gas Orbiter), India (Mars Orbiter Mission), the
    United Arab Emirates (Hope) and China (Tianwen-1).

**3.** (beginner) Why do rover cameras often come in pairs?

??? note "Answer"
    Like our two eyes, two cameras a little apart see the scene from slightly different places, and the difference
    gives the distance and shape of the ground.

**4.** (intermediate) How does a pushbroom camera build a picture?

??? note "Answer"
    It has a single row of detectors across the direction of flight. At each moment it records one line of ground, and
    as the orbiter moves forward, line after line is added to make the picture.

**5.** (intermediate) What problem does time delay integration solve, and how?

??? note "Answer"
    Each line of ground passes so quickly, about a ten-thousandth of a second, that one row of detectors gathers too
    little light. With several rows, the charge is passed from row to row in step with the ground, so the same patch is
    exposed many times and the light adds up.

**6.** (intermediate) How did Perseverance use a camera while landing?

??? note "Answer"
    It photographed the ground as it came down and compared the pictures with maps it carried, to find where it was and
    steer away from danger at the last minute: terrain relative navigation.

**7.** (advanced) HiRISE's pixels each cover 1 microradian. At 250 km above Mars, how big is a pixel on the ground, and
how long does one line of pixels take to pass?

??? note "Answer"
    A pixel covers $250{,}000 \times 10^{-6} = 0.25$ m. With $a = 3{,}639.5$ km, $v = \sqrt{42{,}828/3{,}639.5} = 3.43$
    km/s, and over the ground $3.43 \times 3{,}389.5/3{,}639.5 = 3.20$ km/s. One line takes $0.25/3{,}200 \approx 78$
    µs, close to the 76 µs that HiRISE's design allows.

**8.** (expert) A stereo pair is taken 12° forward and 12° back from straight down. A hilltop shifts by 40 m against the
ground around it. How tall is the hill?

??? note "Answer"
    $h = 40 / (\tan 12° + \tan 12°) = 40 / 0.425 \approx 94$ m.

## Summary

- Mariner 4 took the first close-ups of Mars in 1965; Mariner 9 was the first orbiter, in 1971; Mars 3 made the first
  soft landing, and Viking the first good pictures from the ground.
- Cameras from the United States, Europe, India, the United Arab Emirates and China have orbited Mars, from HiRISE's
  0.3 m detail to whole-planet weather views from MARCI and Hope.
- Pushbroom cameras build pictures one line at a time as the orbiter moves; time delay integration adds up the light
  of several rows.
- Two pictures from different angles give the height of the ground, to 0.25 m with HiRISE; rover cameras come in
  pairs for the same reason.
- Rovers carry many cameras: 17 on Curiosity, 19 on Perseverance; Ingenuity flew with two, and Zhurong's lander set
  one down to photograph the rover.
- Cameras choose landing sites, guided Perseverance's landing, steer rovers and flew a helicopter.
- From Mars, cameras have photographed the Earth, Phobos and Deimos, a passing comet, and the dust that ended
  Opportunity.
- A camera orbiting Mars has 2.4 times as long for each line as at the Earth but 0.43 times the sunlight, so it
  collects about the same light.
