# 3. Colours, prisms and rainbows

*White light is not one colour but all of them together. This chapter shows how a prism spreads sunlight into its
colours and why, how your eye turns three kinds of signal into every colour you see, and how raindrops paint a
rainbow at the same angle every time. It follows the rainbow from Aristotle and Song China to Persia, Germany and
Descartes, then looks at halos round the Sun, the blue of our sky, and the butterscotch sky and blue sunsets of
Mars. It ends by tracing rays through a raindrop to find where the 42° comes from.*

## 3.1 White light is a mix { #white }

In the 1660s Isaac Newton passed a beam of sunlight through a glass prism and showed that white light is a mixture
of colours, each bent by its own amount (history book,
[chapter 18, section 18.1](../history/18-Light-Split-into-Colours.md#prism)). He reported his experiments to the
Royal Society of London in 1672 and set them out in full in his book *Opticks* in 1704.

In his "critical experiment" Newton showed that each colour has its own amount of bending, and that more reflections,
more prisms or a coloured filter cannot change it. A prism does not make the colours; it only sorts out the colours
that were already in the sunlight.

At first, in 1672, Newton named five main colours: red, yellow, green, blue and violet. Later he added orange and
indigo, to make seven, like the seven notes of a musical scale. He also said clearly that colour is a sensation in
the mind, not something that light or objects have in themselves.

??? note "Sources"
    - Newton's prism experiments, reported to the Royal Society in 1672, *Opticks* in 1704, the critical experiment,
      and colour as a sensation in the mind: Wikipedia, [Opticks](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Opticks) (weaker).
    - Newton's five colours in 1672 and the two he added by analogy with the musical scale: Wikipedia,
      [Rainbow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainbow) (weaker).

## 3.2 Why a prism spreads the colours { #prism }

Light slows down in glass (chapter 2, section 2.6). When it enters glass at a slant, the slowing bends it, a change
of direction called **refraction** (chapter 10). The amount of slowing is the **refractive index**, $n$, and it is not
quite the same for every colour: it is a little larger for violet than for red. So violet light is bent the most and
red the least, and the colours fan out. This spreading is called **dispersion**.

The differences are small. In water, for example, $n$ is 1.330 for red light and 1.343 at the blue end. But that is
enough to spread the colours across a screen, or across the sky in a rainbow.

Dispersion is useful and annoying. Astronomers use prisms to spread starlight and read it (chapter 15). But a glass
lens bends colours by different amounts too, so a simple lens gives bright objects coloured fringes
(chapter 10).

## 3.3 Colours and wavelengths { #wavelengths }

Each colour of light is a wave of a certain **wavelength** (chapter 1). Our eyes see from about **380 to 740
nanometres**, where a nanometre is a millionth of a millimetre.

| Colour | Wavelength (nm) |
| --- | --- |
| Red | 625 to 740 |
| Orange | 590 to 625 |
| Yellow | 565 to 590 |
| Green | 500 to 565 |
| Cyan | 485 to 500 |
| Blue | 450 to 485 |
| Violet | 380 to 450 |

The edges are not sharp: the colours blend into each other. Other animals see a different range. Bees see
ultraviolet, which helps them find nectar, but their range ends at about 590 nm, before orange begins. What lies
beyond both ends of our range is the subject of chapter 4.

??? note "Sources"
    - The visible range of about 380 to 740 nm, the wavelength ranges of the colours, and bees: Wikipedia,
      [Color vision](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Color_vision) (weaker).

## 3.4 How your eye sees colour { #eye }

At the back of your eye, colour is sensed by **cone cells**. Most people have three kinds:

- **S cones**, most sensitive at about 420 to 440 nm, in the blue. They are only about 2 per cent of the cones.
- **M cones**, most sensitive at about 534 to 545 nm, in the green.
- **L cones**, most sensitive at about 564 to 580 nm, towards the yellow and red.

Each colour sets off the three kinds by different amounts, and the brain compares them. Thomas Young proposed that
colour vision works with three kinds of receptor. In the 19th century Hermann von Helmholtz tested the idea with
colour-matching experiments: people with normal vision needed three wavelengths, mixed in the right amounts, to match
the range of colours they could see.

That is why three colours are enough for a camera. Each pixel sits under a red, green or blue filter, and the colours
are mixed back together afterwards (the physics lesson
[Colour cameras on Mars](https://physics.akadian.ca/topics/waves/level-6/06-colour-cameras-on-mars.html)).

??? note "Sources"
    - The three types of cone, their peak wavelengths, and S cones at about 2 per cent: Wikipedia,

    - Young's three receptors and Helmholtz's colour-matching experiments: Wikipedia,
      [Trichromacy](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Trichromacy) (weaker).

## 3.5 How a rainbow forms { #rainbow }

To see a rainbow you need the Sun behind you and rain in front of you. Each raindrop does three things to the
sunlight that enters it: it **bends** the light going in, **reflects** it off the back of the drop, and **bends** it
again coming out (Figure B). The light comes back out at about 42° to the direction the sunlight came from, and the
colours separate a little on the way.

So the rainbow is a circle about 42° in radius, centred on the point exactly opposite the Sun, where the shadow of
your head falls. Red is on the outside, at about 42.5°, and blue towards the inside, at about 40.6°. From the ground,
at least half of the circle is below the horizon. From an aeroplane, with enough mist below, you can sometimes see
the whole circle.

Because your rainbow is centred on your own shadow, the person next to you sees light from different drops. Everyone
sees their own rainbow.

**The second bow.** Outside the main bow there is often a larger, fainter one. Its light has been reflected twice
inside each drop, and its colours are the other way round, with red on the inside. The dark band of sky between the
two bows is called **Alexander's band**, after Alexander of Aphrodisias, who first described it.

## 3.6 Explaining the rainbow, around the world { #history }

- **Greece.** Aristotle was the first to give the rainbow serious attention, though his explanation had many flaws.
- **China.** In the Song dynasty (960 to 1279) the scholar-official **Shen Kuo** suggested, as **Sun Sikong** had
  before him, that rainbows form when sunlight meets raindrops in the air.
- **The Islamic world.** **Ibn al-Haytham** tried to give a scientific explanation. In Persia, **Qutb al-Din
  al-Shirazi** (1236 to 1311) gave a fairly accurate one, and his student **Kamal al-Din al-Farisi** (1267 to 1319)
  made it more mathematical: light is bent going into a drop, reflected inside, and bent again coming out. He tested
  this with a glass sphere full of water (history book,
  [chapter 18, section 18.1](../history/18-Light-Split-into-Colours.md#prism)).
- **Europe.** Ibn al-Haytham's *Book of Optics* was translated into Latin and studied by Robert Grosseteste, whose
  work on light was carried on by **Roger Bacon**. Bacon wrote in 1268 about light shining through crystals and water
  drops, and was the first to work out the size of the rainbow: its top can never be more than 42° above the horizon.
  **Theodoric of Freiberg** in Germany explained both the first and second bows in 1307, independently of al-Farisi.
- **Descartes.** In 1637 René Descartes passed light through a large glass sphere full of water, measured the angles,
  and showed that the main bow comes from one reflection inside each drop and the second bow from two. He calculated
  the angles of both. Newton then explained the colours.

??? note "Sources"
    - Aristotle, Shen Kuo and Sun Sikong, Ibn al-Haytham, al-Shirazi, al-Farisi's dates, Grosseteste and the Latin
      translation, Roger Bacon's *Opus Majus* of 1268 and the 42° limit, Theodoric in 1307, al-Farisi in 1309, and
      Descartes in 1637: Wikipedia, [Rainbow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainbow) (weaker).
    - Al-Farisi's glass sphere of water: this site's history book, chapter 18, section 18.1, and its sources.

## 3.7 Halos and sun dogs { #halos }

Rainbows come from raindrops. **Halos** come from tiny ice crystals high in the air, which act like little
six-sided prisms.

- **The 22° halo** is a ring about 22° in radius round the Sun or the Moon, roughly the width of your outstretched
  hand at arm's length. Light passes through two faces of each crystal that meet at 60°, and is bent twice. Halos
  appear when the sky is covered with thin, high cirrus or cirrostratus cloud, which often comes a few days before a
  big storm front.
- **Sun dogs** are two bright, faintly coloured patches about 22° to the left and right of the Sun, at the same height
  as the Sun. They come from flat, plate-shaped ice crystals that drift down with their large faces almost level.

Halos are on the same side of the sky as the Sun, so hide the Sun behind your hand when you look for them. Never
look straight at the Sun.

??? note "Sources"
    - The 22° halo round the Sun or Moon, hexagonal ice crystals, the 60° angle, the hand at arm's length, and
      cirrus or cirrostratus clouds before a storm front: Wikipedia,
      [22° halo](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/22%C2%B0_halo) (weaker).
    - Sun dogs about 22° left and right of the Sun at the same altitude, from plate-shaped crystals: Wikipedia,
      [Sun dog](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sun_dog) (weaker).

## 3.8 Why the sky is blue { #blue }

As sunlight passes through the air, the molecules of the air scatter some of it in all directions. Blue light, with
its shorter waves, is scattered much more strongly than red or green. So wherever you look in the sky away from the
Sun, you see scattered blue light. Lord Rayleigh explained this in 1871, and it is called **Rayleigh scattering**.
The strength of the scattering goes as one over the fourth power of the wavelength (section 3.10).

At sunrise and sunset, sunlight reaches you almost sideways, through a long path of air. Most of the blue is scattered
out of the beam on the way, and the Sun and the clouds it lights look orange to red.

??? note "Sources"
    - Rayleigh and Mie scattering, blue scattered more strongly, Rayleigh's explanation of 1871, the fourth power of

      [Diffuse sky radiation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Diffuse_sky_radiation) (weaker).
    - The physics lesson [Scattering](https://physics.akadian.ca/topics/waves/level-6/04-scattering.html).

## 3.9 Colours on Mars { #mars }

- **Red ground.** The red-orange colour of the Martian surface comes from iron(III) oxide, a kind of rust, and the
  iron mineral goethite. In places the ground looks like butterscotch, or golden, brown, tan or even greenish.
- **A butterscotch sky.** Mars has about 60 times less air above each square metre than the Earth (section 3.10), so
  Rayleigh scattering is weak there. Instead the sky's colour comes from the fine reddish dust in the air, with iron
  oxide in it. By day the Martian sky is butterscotch.
- **Blue sunsets.** On 15 April 2015, its 956th Martian day, Curiosity took its first colour picture of a sunset, in
  Gale Crater. The sky round the setting Sun was blue. The fine dust lets blue light through more easily than the
  longer wavelengths, so the blue stays close to the Sun, while yellow and red light spreads across the sky.
- **No rainbows.** Mars has no rain, so there are no rainbows on its surface. Of all the worlds we know, only Saturn's
  moon Titan, with its rain of methane, could have rainbows at the ground besides the Earth.
- **True colours.** Perseverance's colour camera **Mastcam-Z** is a pair of zoom cameras on the rover's mast.
  Besides ordinary red, green and blue pictures, it takes pictures through narrow colour filters. To get the colours
  right, it often photographs calibration targets carried on the rover, patches of known colour.

## 3.10 Working it out { #working }

**How high is the rainbow?** The rainbow is a circle of radius 42° centred on the point opposite the Sun. That point
is as far below the horizon as the Sun is above it. So the top of the rainbow is at

$$\text{height of the top} = 42^\circ - \text{height of the Sun}.$$

With the Sun at 20°, the top of the bow is 22° up. With the Sun on the horizon, the bow is a full half circle, 42°
high. Once the Sun is higher than 42°, the whole bow is below the horizon, which is Roger Bacon's limit. That is why
rainbows are seen in the morning and late afternoon, and not near midday in summer, when the Sun is high.

**How much bluer?** Scattering goes as $1/\lambda^4$. Compare blue at 450 nm with red at 700 nm:

$$\left(\frac{700}{450}\right)^4 = 5.9,$$

so blue light is scattered almost six times as strongly.

**How much air?** The weight of the air above each square metre is the surface pressure, so its mass is pressure
divided by gravity. Mars: $636 / 3.73 = 171$ kg. Earth: $101{,}400 / 9.82 = 10{,}300$ kg. That is about 60 times
more on the Earth.

| | Mars | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Air above each square metre | about 171 kg | about 10,300 kg |
| What colours the sky | reddish dust | air molecules (Rayleigh scattering) |
| Daytime sky | butterscotch | blue |
| Sky round the setting Sun | blue | orange to red |
| Rainbows | none: no rain | 42° from the point opposite the Sun |

## 3.11 Tracing rays through a raindrop { #descartes }

This is the kind of calculation Descartes did. Take a ray that hits a round drop at an angle of incidence $i$. Going
in, it bends to a smaller angle $r$, given by Snell's law (chapter 10):

$$\sin i = n \sin r.$$

Follow the ray round. Going in, it turns by $i - r$. At the back, it reflects, turning by $180^\circ - 2r$. Coming
out, it turns by $i - r$ again. The total turn is $180^\circ + 2i - 4r$, so the ray comes back at an angle

$$\theta = 4r - 2i$$

to the direction the sunlight came from. Here it is for water, $n = 4/3$:

| Angle of incidence $i$ | 20° | 40° | 50° | 60° | 70° | 80° | 90° |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Angle back $\theta$ | 19.5° | 35.3° | 40.3° | 42.0° | 39.2° | 30.5° | 14.4° |

The angle rises to a largest value of about **42°** and then falls again. Near the top, a wide range of rays all come
back at nearly the same angle, so the light piles up there: that is the bright bow. No ray with one reflection comes
back at more than 42°, so the sky just outside the bow gets none of this light.

With calculus, $\theta$ is largest when $d\theta/di = 0$, which works out as

$$\cos^2 i = \frac{n^2 - 1}{3}.$$

For $n = 4/3$: $\cos^2 i = 7/27$, $i = 59.4^\circ$, and $\theta = 42.0^\circ$. For red light, $n = 1.330$, the largest
angle is 42.5°; for blue, $n = 1.343$, it is 40.6°. So red is on the outside.

??? note "Sources"
    - Descartes's sphere of water, one reflection for the primary bow, his use of the law of refraction, and his
      calculation of the angles: Wikipedia, [Rainbow](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rainbow) (weaker).
    - $n = 1.330$ and $1.343$, giving 42.5° and 40.6°: the same page.
    - The ray tracing, the table and the calculus: our working.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Where must the Sun be for you to see a rainbow?

??? note "Answer"
    Behind you, with rain in front of you.

**2.** (beginner) Which colour is on the outside of the main rainbow?

??? note "Answer"
    Red.

**3.** (beginner) Why is the Earth's sky blue?

??? note "Answer"
    The air scatters blue light much more strongly than red, so scattered blue light comes from every part of the sky.

**4.** (intermediate) Why does a prism spread white light into colours?

??? note "Answer"
    The refractive index of glass is a little larger for violet than for red, so violet is bent more and red less, and
    the colours fan out.

**5.** (intermediate) The Sun is 30° above the horizon. How high is the top of the rainbow?

??? note "Answer"
    $42^\circ - 30^\circ = 12^\circ$.

**6.** (intermediate) Why is the sky round the setting Sun blue on Mars but orange on the Earth?

??? note "Answer"
    On Mars the fine dust lets blue light through more easily and keeps it near the Sun. On the Earth the long path
    through the air scatters the blue out of the sunbeam, leaving orange and red.

**7.** (advanced) How many times more strongly is violet light of 400 nm scattered than red light of 700 nm?

??? note "Answer"
    $(700/400)^4 = 9.4$ times.

**8.** (expert) In a drop with $n = 1.33$, a ray enters at $i = 50^\circ$. Find $r$ and the angle at which the ray
comes back. Does it end up inside or outside the red bow?

??? note "Answer"
    $\sin r = \sin 50^\circ / 1.33 = 0.576$, so $r = 35.2^\circ$, and $\theta = 4 \times 35.2^\circ - 2 \times 50^\circ
    = 40.7^\circ$. That is less than 42.5°, so the ray lands inside the red bow.

## Summary

- White light is a mix of colours; a prism sorts them because each colour bends by its own amount (dispersion).
- Our eyes see from about 380 to 740 nm, using three kinds of cone cell.
- A rainbow is light bent, reflected and bent again in raindrops, in a circle about 42° round the point opposite the
  Sun, red outside. A fainter second bow has two reflections and reversed colours.
- The rainbow was explained step by step by thinkers in Greece, China, Persia and Europe, from Aristotle to Descartes.
- Halos and sun dogs, about 22° from the Sun, come from six-sided ice crystals.
- The Earth's sky is blue because air scatters blue light most; sunsets are red because the blue is scattered away.
- Mars has a red surface, a butterscotch sky, blue sunsets and no rainbows.
