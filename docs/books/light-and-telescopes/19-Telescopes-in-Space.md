# 19. Telescopes in space

*Chapter 12 showed how the air blurs starlight, and chapter 18 how it blocks most kinds of light altogether. The cure
for both is to leave the air behind. This chapter is about what that takes. A telescope in space has to be launched,
pointed with no ground to stand on, kept cold with no air to carry heat away, and told what to do from far away. Its
pictures then have to be sent home by radio. It looks at where space telescopes are put and why, at Hubble, the only
one astronauts were meant to repair, and at Webb, which had to unfold itself on the way out to its post 1.5 million km
from the Earth. It ends with the space telescopes that look back at Mars.*

    **You will learn:**

    - why astronomers put telescopes in space, and what it costs

    - how pictures from space get to astronomers on the ground
    - what Hubble, Chandra and Webb have seen at Mars

    **You need:** chapter 12 on seeing and the limits of telescopes, and chapter 18 on the light the air blocks.

    **Levels:** 19.1 to 19.3 are beginner; 19.4 to 19.8 are intermediate; 19.9 is advanced; 19.10 is expert.

## 19.1 Why go to space? { #why }

In 1946 the American astronomer **Lyman Spitzer** wrote a paper with the plain title "Astronomical advantages of an
extraterrestrial observatory". Above the air, he argued, a telescope would see light that never reaches the ground
(chapter 18), would never be stopped by clouds, and would not suffer from twinkling and blurring (chapter 12,
[section 12.4](12-The-Limits-Diffraction-Resolution-and-Seeing.md#seeing)). His idea led in the end to the Hubble Space
Telescope, and a later infrared telescope was named after him.

The first space telescopes to work were small and looked at ultraviolet light.

- **OAO-2**, the second of NASA's Orbiting Astronomical Observatories, carried 11 ultraviolet telescopes when it was
  launched on 7 December 1968. It was the first successful space telescope. The series was run by **Nancy Grace
  Roman**, NASA's chief of astronomy.
- **Orion 1**, a Soviet ultraviolet telescope, worked on board the space station Salyut 1 in 1971.
- **OAO-3**, named **Copernicus**, launched on 21 August 1972, was the most successful of the series.

There is a price. Space telescopes cost far more to build than telescopes on the ground, and once launched, almost all
of them can never be touched again.

how warm it gets, and whether anyone can reach it.

- **Low orbit.** Hubble circles 540 km up, going round once every 95 minutes (section 19.9). Astronauts on the Space
  Shuttle could reach it. But the Earth is close, and blocks part of its view.
- **Geosynchronous orbit**, 35,786 km up, goes round once a day, in step with the turning Earth, so one ground
  station can stay in touch with a satellite there all the time. The ultraviolet telescope IUE worked
  there (chapter 18), and Hubble's pictures come home through relay satellites there (section 19.7).
- **A long loop.** **TESS** swings on a stretched orbit, from 108,000 km out to about the Moon's distance, and goes
  round twice for each trip of the Moon.
- **Trailing the Earth.** **Spitzer** was the first spacecraft to be put on an orbit round the Sun just behind the
  Earth, slowly drifting away from it, about 15 million km a year. **Kepler** followed.
- **L2.** About 1.5 million km beyond the Earth, on the side away from the Sun, is a point called **L2**. A spacecraft
  there goes round the Sun in a year, keeping pace with the Earth. Webb, Gaia, Euclid and Roman went there, and so did
  the earlier Herschel and Planck, launched together in 2009. Far from the Earth's shadow, they get steady sunlight
  for their solar panels, need little fuel to stay in place, and can always radio home.

## 19.3 Hubble, the telescope that could be fixed { #hubble }

The **Hubble Space Telescope** was built in the 1970s by NASA with help from the European Space Agency, and named after
the astronomer Edwin Hubble. It was meant to be launched in 1983, but delays, money troubles and the loss of the Space
Shuttle *Challenger* in 1986 held it back. The shuttle *Discovery* finally carried it up on 24 April 1990. Its 2.4 m
mirror feeds instruments that see ultraviolet, visible and near-infrared light.

Hubble is the only telescope designed to be looked after in space by astronauts. That turned out to matter at once: its
mirror had been ground to the wrong shape, and in 1993 astronauts fitted corrections (chapter 12,
[section 12.7](12-The-Limits-Diffraction-Resolution-and-Seeing.md#hubble)). Later visits swapped in new cameras and
spare parts. Its main camera was replaced in 1993 and again in the last servicing mission, in 2009. An infrared
instrument whose block of nitrogen ice, its coolant, ran out after a couple of years was brought back to life with a
small refrigerator.

In December 1995 the director of the Space Telescope Science Institute, **Robert Williams**, used his own share of the
observing time to point Hubble at a "typical" patch of sky in Ursa Major, far from the dust of the Milky Way. Over ten
days Hubble took 342 pictures of the same spot. The patch is only about a 24-millionth of the sky, the size of a
tennis ball seen from 100 m away, yet the **Hubble Deep Field** showed about 3,000 objects, almost all of them
galaxies, some among the most distant then known.

Hubble passed 30 years of work in April 2020, and is expected to keep going until some time between 2030 and 2040.

## 19.4 Holding still with nothing to hold on to { #pointing }

On the ground a telescope is turned by motors pushing against its mount. In space there is nothing to push against.
Yet a space telescope must point at a faint galaxy and hold still, sometimes for hours.

The usual answer is a **reaction wheel**: a heavy wheel spun by an electric motor. Spin the wheel faster one way, and
the spacecraft turns slowly the other way, because the total spin, the angular momentum, cannot change. Three wheels,
set at different angles, can turn the telescope any way, with no fuel at all, only electricity from its solar panels.

Hubble's pointing system uses magnetic sensors, optical sensors and six gyroscopes to tell where it is facing, and
reaction wheels and magnetic coils to turn. Fine guidance sensors lock onto stars to hold it steady. It is never
pointed within about 50° of the Sun, so that sunlight cannot fall on any part of its optics.

Wheels wear out. Two servicing missions to Hubble replaced one. Kepler had four reaction wheels and needed three. One
stopped on 14 July 2012, and a second on 11 May 2013, ending its first mission. Engineers found a way to carry on, as
**K2**, with the stars allowed to drift slowly across its view and software to follow them. Kepler went on finding
planets until it ran out of fuel in 2018.

Not every telescope holds still. Gaia, which measured the positions of stars, spun slowly on purpose, sweeping its
view across the sky at 60 seconds of arc each second.

## 19.5 Webb: folded, shaded and cold { #webb }

The **James Webb Space Telescope**, launched on 25 December 2021, was built by NASA with the European and Canadian
space agencies (history book, [chapter 22, section 22.2](../history/22-Astronomy-Today.md#space)). It works mostly in
the infrared, from 0.6 to 28.5 micrometres, so like the telescopes of chapter 18
([section 18.2](18-Infrared-Ultraviolet-X-ray-and-Gamma-ray-Astronomy.md#infrared)) it must be cold.

**Folded to fit.** Its mirror, 6.5 m across, is far too wide for any rocket. It was made instead of 18 hexagonal
segments of beryllium, coated with gold, which unfolded after launch, an idea first tried by the Italian astronomer
**Guido Horn d'Arturo**. Its sunshield has five layers of plastic film, each about a tenth of a millimetre thick and
coated with aluminium on both sides. It was folded twelve times to fit inside the nose of its Ariane 5 rocket, 4.57 m
wide.

**Shaded at L2.** Webb was sent to L2, where it loops round the point on a **halo orbit**.

*Figure B. Why Webb was sent to L2.*

From L2 the Sun, the Earth and the Moon all lie in roughly the same direction. One sunshield, kept facing them, shades
the telescope from all three, and the telescope stays below 50 K. Its mid-infrared instrument, MIRI, must be colder
still, below 6 K, and has its own refrigerator that pumps helium gas. Earlier cold telescopes carried liquid helium and
stopped when it ran out. Europe's **Herschel**, with a 3.5 m mirror, the largest infrared telescope launched before
Webb, carried over 2,300 litres. It ran out on 29 April 2013, after four years.

**Fuel.** L2 is not a stable place to sit (section 19.10), so Webb fires small thrusters to stay there. It was built
with enough fuel for 10 years, but its launch was so accurate that it may now last about 20.

## 19.6 Telescopes that map the whole sky { #surveys }

Hubble and Webb look closely at chosen targets. Other space telescopes do one job, over the whole sky or a big part of
it.

- **Measuring the stars.** The air makes it hard to measure the tiny yearly shifts of stars, their **parallax**, that
  give their distances (history book,
  [chapter 19, section 19.3](../history/19-Galaxies-and-the-Expanding-Universe.md#parallax)). Europe's **Hipparcos**,
  from 1989 to 1993, was the first space telescope built for this, and measured about 118,000 stars. Its successor
  **Gaia**, launched in 2013 and switched off on 27 March 2025, measured the positions, distances and motions of
  objects down to magnitude 20, about 1% of the stars of the Milky Way. Its catalogue of about a billion objects is
  still being prepared.
- **Finding planets.** **Kepler**, launched in 2009, stared at one patch of sky in Cygnus, Lyra and Draco, watching
  for stars that dimmed as planets crossed them. Its 1.4 m mirror was the largest outside Earth orbit when it was
  launched. It found over 2,600 planets (history book,
  [chapter 22, section 22.3](../history/22-Astronomy-Today.md#planets)). **TESS**, launched on 18 April 2018 and led
  by the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, looks at 85% of the sky for planets round the brightest nearby stars.
- **The oldest light.** **COBE** (1989 to 1993) measured the faint microwave glow left from the early universe, and
  two of its scientists, John Mather and George Smoot, shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2006. Europe's **Planck**
  (2009 to 2013) mapped it in much finer detail, helping to find the age of the universe. To do so it cooled its
  detectors to a tenth of a degree above absolute zero, which made it, for over two years, the coldest known object in
  space.
- **The shape of the universe.** Europe's **Euclid**, launched on 1 July 2023, is mapping up to two billion galaxies
  over a third of the sky, to learn about dark matter and dark energy. NASA's **Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope**,
  launched on 30 August 2026 and named after the woman who ran the first OAO telescopes, has a 2.4 m mirror given by
  a US spy-satellite agency. Its camera takes in an area of sky 100 times larger than Hubble's cameras, with Hubble's
  sharpness. China's **Xuntian**, due in 2027, will take in an area 300 to 350 times Hubble's (history book,
  [chapter 22, section 22.2](../history/22-Astronomy-Today.md#space)).

      dark energy: Wikipedia, [Euclid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Euclid_(spacecraft)) (weaker).
    - Roman: launched on 30 August 2026, named after NASA's first chief of astronomy, the 2.4 m mirror donated by the
      National Reconnaissance Office, a field 100 times larger than Hubble's cameras at Hubble's sharpness: Wikipedia,
      Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope (above). That the NRO runs US spy satellites is standard.
    - Xuntian: due in 2027, a field 300 to 350 times Hubble's: Wikipedia,
      [Xuntian](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xuntian) (weaker).

## 19.7 Getting the pictures home { #data }

A space telescope's pictures are numbers, sent home by radio (chapter 17). About twice a day Hubble radios its stored
data up to one of NASA's relay satellites in geosynchronous orbit, which passes it down to one of two 18 m dishes at
White Sands, New Mexico. Webb sends its data to the big dishes of NASA's Deep Space Network.

Both telescopes are run from Baltimore, Maryland, by the **Space Telescope Science Institute**. Astronomers anywhere in
the world send in proposals; the institute chooses which to observe, plans the work, checks and calibrates the data, and
puts it online. After a while the data is opened to everyone, so that pictures taken for one team are used again by
many others.

??? note "Sources"
    - Hubble radioing data about twice daily to the geosynchronous TDRSS, downlinked to one of two 18 m antennas at
      White Sands, and STScI selecting targets and processing data: Wikipedia, Hubble Space Telescope (above).
    - Webb's data sent through the Deep Space Network, processed and calibrated at STScI and distributed online to
      astronomers worldwide, and STScI in Baltimore operating Webb: Wikipedia, James Webb Space Telescope (above).
    - That observing time is given by proposal, and that data becomes public after a period, is standard practice for
      both telescopes.

## 19.8 Space telescopes look at Mars { #mars }

**Hubble.** Hubble has made long studies of Mars and taken the sharpest pictures of it ever made from near the Earth,
with detail about 13 km across when Mars is closest (chapter 12,
[section 12.9](12-The-Limits-Diffraction-Resolution-and-Seeing.md#mars)). It sees a whole side of the planet at once,
so it can follow storms and clouds across it. It can only look when Mars is more than 50° from the Sun in the sky.

**Chandra and XMM-Newton.** In 2001 Chandra (chapter 18,
[section 18.4](18-Infrared-Ultraviolet-X-ray-and-Gamma-ray-Astronomy.md#xray)) caught X-rays from Mars for the first
time. By 2003 astronomers had found two sources: X-rays from the Sun scattered by the top of Mars's air, and X-rays
given off when charged particles from the Sun meet gas escaping from Mars. XMM-Newton has seen this second glow
reaching out to eight times the radius of Mars.

**Webb.** Webb can look at anything in the Solar System that is more than 85° from the Sun in the sky and moves slowly
enough to follow, and that includes Mars.

These telescopes see Mars from tens of millions of kilometres away. The sharpest views of all come from telescopes
that went to Mars itself (chapter 20).

??? note "Sources"
    - Hubble's systematic studies of Mars, the highest-resolution images of Mars from the Earth, useful images only at
      least 50° from the Sun, whole-hemisphere views of weather systems, Chandra's first observation of X-rays from Mars
      in 2001, the two components found in 2003, scattered solar X-rays and charge exchange, and XMM-Newton seeing the
      second out to eight times the radius of Mars: Wikipedia,
      [History of Mars observation](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Mars_observation) (weaker). The 13 km:
      chapter 12. That the charge-exchange ions come from the solar wind and Mars's escaping gas is standard.
    - Webb observing Solar System objects more than 85° from the Sun moving slower than 0.03 arcseconds per second,
      including Mars: Wikipedia, James Webb Space Telescope (above).

## 19.9 Working it out { #working }

**How long is one orbit?** A small body circling a planet at distance $a$ from its centre takes

$$T = 2\pi\sqrt{\frac{a^3}{GM}}.$$

For Hubble, $a = 6{,}371 + 540 = 6{,}911$ km and $GM = 398{,}600$ km³/s², so
$T = 2\pi\sqrt{6{,}911^3 / 398{,}600} \approx 5{,}720$ s, about 95 minutes, or 15 times a day.

**TESS and the Moon.** For a stretched orbit, $a$ is the average of the nearest and farthest distances. Taking
108,000 km and the Moon's distance, 384,400 km, gives $a \approx 246{,}000$ km and $T \approx 14.1$ days. The real
orbit, which turns back a little short of the Moon, takes 13.70 days, half the Moon's 27.3.

**A space telescope near Mars?**

| | Mars | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sunlight on each square metre of a telescope near the planet | 586 W | 1,361 W |
| One orbit 540 km up | 125 minutes | 95 minutes |
| The planet's L2 point, beyond it | 1.08 million km | 1.50 million km |
| How much the planet dims the Sun, seen crossing it from far away | 24 parts per million | 84 parts per million |

A telescope at Mars's L2 would need to shade itself from less than half as much sunlight. And an astronomer on a
distant planet, hunting with a Kepler of their own, would find Mars much harder to spot than the Earth: as it crossed
the Sun it would dim it by less than a third as much (history book,
[chapter 22, section 22.11](../history/22-Astronomy-Today.md#working)).

??? note "Sources"
    - The Earth's mean radius 6,371 km, $GM$ 398,600 km³/s², and solar irradiance 1,361 W/m²: NASA,
      [Earth Fact Sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/earthfact.html). Mars's radius 3,389.5 km,
      $GM$ 42,828 km³/s², and 586.2 W/m²: NASA,
      [Mars Fact Sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/marsfact.html). The Sun's radius 695,700 km:
      NASA, [Sun Fact Sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/sunfact.html).
    - TESS's 13.70 days and near point of 108,000 km: section 19.2. The Moon's distance, 384,400 km, and its orbit of
      27.3 days are standard.
    - The L2 distances: section 19.10. The dimming is $(R_\text{planet}/R_\text{Sun})^2$. The arithmetic is ours.

## 19.10 Where L2 is, and why it will not hold still { #l2 }

Put a spacecraft a distance $r$ beyond a planet of mass $m$, on the line from the Sun, of mass $M$, which the planet
circles at distance $R$ with angular speed $\omega$, where $\omega^2 R = GM/R^2$. To keep pace with the planet, the
spacecraft must go round the Sun with the same $\omega$, at the larger radius $R + r$. The pull it needs is
$\omega^2(R + r)$, and the pull it gets is from the Sun and the planet together:

$$\omega^2 (R + r) = \frac{GM}{(R + r)^2} + \frac{Gm}{r^2}.$$

For $r$ much smaller than $R$, use $1/(1 + x)^2 \approx 1 - 2x$:

$$\frac{GM}{R^2}\left(1 + \frac{r}{R}\right) \approx \frac{GM}{R^2}\left(1 - \frac{2r}{R}\right) + \frac{Gm}{r^2}
\quad\Rightarrow\quad \frac{3GMr}{R^3} = \frac{Gm}{r^2} \quad\Rightarrow\quad r = R\left(\frac{m}{3M}\right)^{1/3}.$$

For the Earth, $m/M = 5.97 / 1{,}988{,}400 = 3.00\times10^{-6}$, so $(m/3M)^{1/3} = 0.0100$ and
$r = 0.0100 \times 149.6$ million km $= 1.50$ million km. For Mars, $m/M = 3.23\times10^{-7}$, the cube root of a
third of it is 0.00475, and $r = 0.00475 \times 228.0$ million km $= 1.08$ million km.

**Why it will not hold still.** Move the spacecraft a little farther out. The pull it needs, $\omega^2(R + r)$, grows,
but the pull of gravity shrinks, so it falls short and drifts farther out. Move it a little inward and gravity wins, so
it drifts farther in. A spacecraft at L2 is like a ball balanced on a hilltop. Webb nudges itself back with about
2.5 m/s of thrust a year, from a total of 93 m/s it can use.

??? note "Sources"
    - $r \approx R(\mu/3)^{1/3}$ for L1 and L2, equal to the radius of the Hill sphere, and L1, L2 and L3 being
      unstable: Wikipedia, Lagrange point (above).
    - The masses of the Sun (1,988,400 × 10²⁴ kg), the Earth (5.9722 × 10²⁴ kg) and Mars (0.64169 × 10²⁴ kg), and the
      orbits of the Earth (149.598 million km) and Mars (227.956 million km): NASA, Sun, Earth and Mars Fact Sheets
      (above).
    - Webb's station-keeping of about 2.5 m/s a year from a total of 93 m/s: Wikipedia, James Webb Space Telescope
      (above).
    - The derivation is ours; the ball on a hilltop is our comparison.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Name three things the air does to starlight that a telescope in space escapes.

??? note "Answer"
    The air absorbs much of the light (most infrared, ultraviolet, X-rays and gamma rays), clouds block it, and the
    moving air makes stars twinkle and blurs their images.

**2.** (beginner) Why could astronauts repair Hubble but not Webb?

??? note "Answer"
    Hubble orbits only 540 km up, where the Space Shuttle could reach it. Webb is near L2, about 1.5 million km away.

**3.** (beginner) What did the Hubble Deep Field show?

??? note "Answer"
    In a patch about a 24-millionth of the sky, where nothing had been known, it found about 3,000 objects, almost all
    of them galaxies.

**4.** (intermediate) How does a reaction wheel turn a spacecraft without fuel?

??? note "Answer"
    A motor spins the wheel faster one way, and the spacecraft turns the other way, because the total angular momentum
    cannot change. The motor runs on electricity from the solar panels.

**5.** (intermediate) Why is L2 a good place for an infrared telescope like Webb?

??? note "Answer"
    From L2, the Sun, the Earth and the Moon are all on one side, so one sunshield can keep all their light and warmth
    off the telescope, which stays very cold. It is also far from the Earth's shadow and heat and can always radio home.

**6.** (intermediate) What did Gaia measure, and why from space?

??? note "Answer"
    The positions, distances (from parallax) and motions of about a billion stars and other objects. The air makes such
    tiny measurements much less precise from the ground.

**7.** (advanced) How long does one orbit take 1,000 km above the Earth?

??? note "Answer"
    $a = 6{,}371 + 1{,}000 = 7{,}371$ km, so $T = 2\pi\sqrt{7{,}371^3 / 398{,}600} \approx 6{,}300$ s, about 105
    minutes.

**8.** (expert) How far beyond Jupiter is its L2 point? Take Jupiter's mass as 1/1,048 of the Sun's, and its distance
from the Sun as 778.5 million km.

??? note "Answer"
    $m/3M = 1/3{,}144 = 3.18\times10^{-4}$, whose cube root is 0.0683. So
    $r = 0.0683 \times 778.5$ million km $\approx 53$ million km, about 35 times as far as the Earth's L2.

## Summary

- Above the air a telescope sees every kind of light, never clouded and never blurred; but it costs far more and can
  rarely be repaired.
- OAO-2 in 1968 was the first successful space telescope; Lyman Spitzer had proposed the idea in 1946.
- Space telescopes orbit low (Hubble), on long loops (TESS), behind the Earth (Kepler, Spitzer), or near L2, 1.5
  million km away (Webb, Gaia, Euclid, Roman).
- Hubble, launched in 1990, was repaired and improved by astronauts until 2009; its Deep Field found about 3,000
  galaxies in a tiny patch of sky.
- Space telescopes are pointed with gyroscopes, star sensors and reaction wheels; when Kepler's wheels failed, it kept
  working as K2.
- Webb's mirror and sunshield unfolded in space; at L2 one shield shades it from the Sun, Earth and Moon, keeping it
  below 50 K.
- Survey telescopes map star positions (Hipparcos, Gaia), find planets (Kepler, TESS), map the oldest light (COBE,
  Planck) and map galaxies (Euclid, Roman, Xuntian).
- Hubble, Chandra, XMM-Newton and Webb have all studied Mars from near the Earth.
- L2 lies at $R(m/3M)^{1/3}$ from a planet: 1.50 million km for the Earth, 1.08 million km for Mars. It is unstable, so
  spacecraft there need fuel to stay.
