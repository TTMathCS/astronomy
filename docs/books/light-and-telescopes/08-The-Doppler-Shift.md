# 8. The Doppler shift

*A siren drops in pitch as the ambulance passes. The same thing happens to light: a star moving away from us looks a
little redder, and one coming towards us a little bluer. The change is far too small to see by eye, but the sharp
spectral lines of chapter 7 let astronomers measure it, and so measure speeds across the universe. This chapter
explains why the shift happens, follows the idea from a lecture in Prague and a train full of musicians in the
Netherlands to the first planets found round other stars, and shows how radio signals from landers on Mars help to
weigh the planet's core. It ends with the huge redshifts of the most distant galaxies.*

## 8.1 The sound of a passing siren { #pitch }

Stand by a road as an ambulance goes past. As it comes towards you, its siren sounds higher than it really is. As it
passes, the pitch slides down, and as it drives away it sounds lower. The driver hears the same note all the time.

This is the **Doppler effect**, named after Christian Doppler, who described it in 1842. It happens with any kind of
wave, including light, whenever the source and the observer move towards or away from each other.

??? note "Sources"
    - The siren, starting higher, sliding down as it passes and continuing lower, and Doppler in 1842: Wikipedia,
      [Doppler effect](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Doppler_effect) (weaker).

## 8.2 Why the pitch changes { #why }

source was when it sent that crest. If the source is moving, each new circle starts a little further along.

## 8.3 Redshift and blueshift { #light }

For light, longer waves mean redder light and shorter waves mean bluer light (chapter 3).

- A source moving **away** from us has its light shifted to longer wavelengths: a **redshift**.
- A source moving **towards** us has its light shifted to shorter wavelengths: a **blueshift**.

For a star moving at an ordinary speed $v$, the fraction by which each wavelength changes is simply $v/c$, its speed
divided by the speed of light. A star moving away at 30 km/s, for example, has its light stretched by only 1 part in
10,000. The star does not look any different, but its spectral lines (chapter 7) are sharp enough that their tiny
shift can be measured.

Only motion **along our line of sight**, straight towards or away from us, makes this shift. Astronomers call it the
**radial velocity**. A star moving across the sky does not show a shift (at ordinary speeds).

## 8.4 A lecture in Prague and a train of musicians { #history }

- **Doppler, 1842.** Christian Doppler, an Austrian mathematician born in Salzburg, was a professor in Prague when he
  gave a lecture to the Royal Bohemian Society of Sciences in 1842. His paper was called "On the coloured light of
  the binary stars and some other stars of the heavens". He hoped his idea would explain why some stars look coloured.
  That part was wrong: the colours of stars come from their temperature (chapter 6). He also believed, wrongly, that
  a star moving faster than 136,000 km/s would become invisible.
- **Buys Ballot, 1845.** The Dutch scientist **Christophorus Buys Ballot** tested the idea with sound. He put a group
  of musicians, playing a known note, on a train on the line between Utrecht and Amsterdam. As the train came towards
  him the note sounded higher, and as it went away, lower.
- **Fizeau, 1848.** In France, **Hippolyte Fizeau**, who did not know of Doppler's work, suggested that a shift in the
  spectral lines of stars could measure how fast they move. In France the effect is still sometimes called the
  Doppler–Fizeau effect. The same year **John Scott Russell** studied the effect with experiments in Britain.
- **Huggins, 1868.** In London, **William Huggins** found that the lines of Sirius were shifted to the red, and used
  the shift to work out the star's speed away from us (history book,
  [chapter 18, section 18.6](../history/18-Light-Split-into-Colours.md#stars)).
- **Checking it.** In 1887 **Hermann Carl Vogel** and **Julius Scheiner** found the yearly change in the shifts of
  stars caused by the Earth's own motion round the Sun. In 1901 the Russian astronomer **Aristarkh Belopolsky**
  showed the shift of light in the laboratory, using rotating mirrors.

    - Buys Ballot's musicians on the Utrecht to Amsterdam line in 1845: Wikipedia,

## 8.5 Spinning suns, rings and galaxies { #spin }

When something spins, one side comes towards us and the other side goes away. The Doppler shift shows both.

- **The Sun.** In 1871 the spin of the Sun was seen in its dark lines: light from the edge turning away from us was
  shifted to the red by about 0.01 nm.
- **Saturn's rings.** In 1895 **James Keeler** found that different parts of Saturn's rings have different Doppler
  shifts, because they go round Saturn at different speeds. So the rings do not turn as one solid body: each part
  keeps to its own orbit.
- **Hidden pairs.** Some stars that look single in any telescope show lines that split and move back and forth. They
  are pairs of stars going round each other too closely to be seen apart. Edward Pickering and Hermann Vogel found the
  first of these **spectroscopic binaries**.
- **Galaxies.** At Lowell Observatory in Flagstaff, **Vesto Slipher** began in 1912 to measure the spectra of spiral
  nebulae. The Andromeda Galaxy was coming towards us at about 300 km/s, but most were moving away. In 1914 he also
  found that spiral galaxies spin. In 1929 Edwin Hubble put Slipher's speeds together with distances and found that
  the farther a galaxy is, the faster it moves away: the universe is expanding (history book,
  [chapter 19, section 19.7](../history/19-Galaxies-and-the-Expanding-Universe.md#expanding)).

## 8.6 Planets that make their star wobble { #planets }

A planet does not simply go round its star. Both go round their shared centre, so the star moves in a small circle of
its own. Seen from the side, the star comes towards us and goes away again, over and over, and its lines shift back
and forth.

The wobble is tiny. Jupiter makes the Sun move at about 13 m/s (47 km/h), and the Earth makes it move at only about
9 cm/s. The best spectrographs, such as HARPS at La Silla in Chile, can measure changes
of 3 m/s or even less.

- **1988, Canada.** Bruce Campbell, G. A. H. Walker and Stephenson Yang found that the star Gamma Cephei wobbled as if
  a planet pulled it. It was confirmed only in 2003.
- **1995, Switzerland and France.** **Michel Mayor** and **Didier Queloz** of the University of Geneva used the
  ELODIE spectrograph at the Haute-Provence Observatory in France to find a planet round **51 Pegasi**, a star like
  the Sun. The planet, about half the mass of Jupiter, goes round its star in about four days, much closer than
  Mercury is to the Sun. They shared the Nobel Prize in Physics in 2019 (history book,
  [chapter 22, section 22.3](../history/22-Astronomy-Today.md#planets)).
- **2018, India.** Scientists of the Physical Research Laboratory in Ahmedabad used the PARAS spectrograph to
  confirm K2-236b, a planet between Neptune and Saturn in size about 600 light-years away. It was the first planet
  found by scientists based in India.

Until about 2012, this Doppler method found more planets than any other.

## 8.7 Doppler at work on Earth and in the sky { #everyday }

- **Speed guns.** A police radar gun sends out radio waves and measures the change in frequency of the echo from a
  moving car. Connecticut State Police tested one in 1947. The same kind of gun times baseball pitches, tennis serves
  and cricket bowling.
- **Weather radar.** Doppler weather radars find not only where rain, snow and hail are, but how fast they are moving
  towards or away from the radar. In May 1973 a Doppler radar followed a tornado at Union City, Oklahoma, through its
  whole life, and showed the cloud spinning before the tornado touched the ground.
- **Millions of stars.** China's **LAMOST** telescope at Xinglong in Hebei, named after the 13th-century astronomer
  Guo Shoujing (history book, [chapter 4, section 4.10](../history/04-China.md#guo)), has 4,000 optical fibres at its
  focus, each carrying the light of a different object to its spectrographs at the same time. It was planned to survey
  10 million stars in our galaxy. Each spectrum gives a star's speed towards or away from us as well as what it is
  made of.

??? note "Sources"
    - The radar speed gun, the Doppler change of frequency, the Connecticut State Police in 1947, and its use for

    - Doppler weather radar, the motion of precipitation, and the Union City tornado of May 1973: Wikipedia,

## 8.8 Weighing the core of Mars by radio { #mars }

Every lander on Mars talks to Earth by radio. As Mars spins, a lander is carried towards us and then away from us,
so the frequency of its signal shifts up and down by the Doppler effect. Measured carefully over many months, these
shifts show exactly how Mars turns, including the slow wobble of its axis.

- **Viking and Pathfinder.** Radio Doppler measurements from Viking in 1976, and twenty years later from
  Mars Pathfinder, each gave the direction of Mars's axis. The change between them showed how fast the axis is
  slowly turning, and from that how the planet's mass is spread out inside. This set limits on the size of the core.
- **InSight.** NASA's InSight lander, which reached Mars in 2018, carried RISE, the Rotation and Interior Structure
  Experiment. It used the lander's radio to measure Mars's turning even more precisely, to learn the size and density
  of its core.

The core of Mars is now thought to be about 1,830 km in radius, a little more than half the radius of the planet.

??? note "Sources"
    - The landing of 26 November 2018, radio Doppler measurements from Viking and Mars Pathfinder, the precession over
      20 years, the moment of inertia and the core size, and RISE using the X-band radio to measure rotation:
      Wikipedia, [InSight](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/InSight) (weaker).
    - Core radius 1,830 km and mean radius 3,389.5 km: NASA,
      [Mars Fact Sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/marsfact.html).

## 8.9 Working it out { #working }

**Speed from a shift.** For speeds much less than the speed of light,

$$\frac{\Delta \lambda}{\lambda} = \frac{v}{c}, \qquad c = 299{,}792 \text{ km/s}.$$

For hydrogen's red line H-alpha, at 656.3 nm (chapter 7):

| Motion | Speed | Shift of H-alpha |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Earth round the Sun | 29.78 km/s | 0.065 nm |
| Andromeda towards us | 300 km/s | 0.66 nm to the blue |
| The Sun, pulled by Jupiter | 13 m/s | 0.000028 nm |
| The Sun, pulled by the Earth | 9 cm/s | 0.0000002 nm |

**The Sun's spin.** The Sun's radius is 695,700 km and it turns once in 609.12 hours at its equator, so the edge moves
at $2\pi \times 695{,}700 / (609.12 \times 3{,}600) = 1.99$ km/s. One edge comes towards us and the other goes away, so
at 600 nm the two edges differ by $600 \times 2 \times 1.99 / 299{,}792 = 0.008$ nm, close to the 0.01 nm seen in 1871.

**How hard a planet pulls.** The star and planet balance like a seesaw round their centre, so the star's speed is the
planet's speed times the planet's mass divided by the star's. For Jupiter,
$13.06 \text{ km/s} \times 1{,}898 / 1{,}988{,}400 = 12.5$ m/s, which is the 13 m/s above.

| | Mars | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Speed round the Sun | 24.08 km/s | 29.78 km/s |
| Speed of the equator as the planet spins | 241 m/s | 465 m/s |
| Wobble it gives the Sun | 0.8 cm/s | 8.9 cm/s |
| Core radius | 1,830 km | 3,485 km |

An astronomer on another star trying to find Mars by the Sun's wobble would have to measure a speed of less than a
centimetre per second.

## 8.10 When the shift is huge { #redshift }

Astronomers write the shift as $z$:

$$z = \frac{\Delta \lambda}{\lambda}.$$

For small speeds $z = v/c$. But for a source moving close to the speed of light, Einstein's relativity changes the
formula to

$$1 + z = \sqrt{\frac{1 + v/c}{1 - v/c}}.$$

A source moving away at 60% of the speed of light has $1 + z = \sqrt{1.6/0.4} = 2$, so $z = 1$: every wavelength is
doubled. Turning it round, $v/c = \dfrac{(1+z)^2 - 1}{(1+z)^2 + 1}$, which never reaches 1 however large $z$ gets.

**The most distant galaxies.** The biggest redshifts of all are not caused by galaxies racing through space. They
come from the **expansion of the universe**, which stretches light on its long journey (history book, chapter 19).
This is called **cosmological redshift**, and the formula above does not give a real speed for it. Some of the most
distant galaxies known, such as JADES-GS-z14-0, have $z \approx 14.3$: we see them as they were less than 300 million
years after the Big Bang.

At $z = 14.32$ every wavelength is $15.32$ times longer:

- hydrogen's ultraviolet line at 121.6 nm (chapter 7) arrives at $121.6 \times 15.32 = 1{,}863$ nm, in the infrared;
- H-alpha, at 656.3 nm, arrives at about $10{,}050$ nm, or 10 µm, in the far infrared.

This is why the most distant galaxies are studied with infrared telescopes, above all in space (chapter 19).

??? note "Answer"
    $v = 0.2 / 656.3 \times 299{,}792 = 91$ km/s, away from us (the line moved to the red).

**8.** (expert) In a quasar with $z = 3$, where does hydrogen's 121.6 nm ultraviolet line appear? If the redshift were
a Doppler shift, how fast would the quasar be moving?

??? note "Answer"
    $121.6 \times 4 = 486.4$ nm, blue-green visible light. With $1 + z = 4$,
    $v/c = (16 - 1)/(16 + 1) = 15/17 = 0.88$, about 265,000 km/s. (In fact a redshift this large comes mostly from the
    expansion of the universe.)

## Summary

- A source moving towards you gives shorter waves (higher pitch, bluer light); one moving away gives longer waves.
- For light, $\Delta\lambda/\lambda = v/c$; only motion along the line of sight counts.
- Doppler proposed the idea in Prague in 1842; Buys Ballot tested it with musicians on a train in 1845; Fizeau and
  Huggins applied it to the stars.
- Doppler shifts show the spin of the Sun, the many pieces of Saturn's rings, hidden pairs of stars and the motion of
  galaxies.
- Planets are found from the tiny wobble they give their star: 51 Pegasi b in 1995, K2-236b from India in 2018.
- Speed guns, weather radar and surveys of millions of stars all use the effect.
- Doppler tracking of Viking, Pathfinder and InSight measured how Mars turns, and so the size of its core.
- The largest redshifts come from the expansion of the universe and push light from the first galaxies into the
  infrared.
