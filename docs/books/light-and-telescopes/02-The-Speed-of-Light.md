# 2. The speed of light

*Light is fast, but it is not instant. This chapter tells how people around the world argued about whether light
takes time to travel, how Galileo tried and failed to time it with lanterns, how Rømer and Bradley timed it with the
sky, and how Fizeau finally measured it on the ground with a spinning wheel. It explains why the speed of light now
defines the metre, why looking out into space means looking back in time, and why nobody can drive a rover on Mars
with a joystick. It ends by working out the speed of light from Fizeau's wheel and from the tilt of starlight.*

## 2.1 How fast? { #fast }

In empty space light travels at exactly **299,792,458 metres per second**, about 300,000 km every second, or about a
billion kilometres an hour. Physicists call it $c$. In one second light could go about seven and a half times round
the Earth.

Even so, the distances in space are so large that light takes time to cross them:

- from the Moon to the Earth, about **1.3 seconds**;
- from the Sun to the Earth, about **8 minutes 19 seconds**.

When Apollo 8 became the first crewed spacecraft to go round the Moon, the controllers on Earth had to wait
at least three seconds after each question for the answer.

## 2.2 Instant or not? { #instant }

For two thousand years people argued about whether light takes any time at all.

- **Greece.** Empedocles, in the 5th century BC, thought light moves and so must take time. Aristotle disagreed:
  light, he said, is a presence, not a movement. Heron of Alexandria argued that light must be infinitely fast,
  because the stars appear the moment you open your eyes.
- **The Islamic world.** In his *Book of Optics* of 1021, **Ibn al-Haytham** argued that light is something real
  that moves, so it must take time, even if too little to notice, and that it goes more slowly in denser materials.
  In the same century **al-Biruni** agreed that light takes time, and noted that it is much faster than sound.
- **Europe.** In the 17th century Johannes Kepler and René Descartes both held that light is instant. Descartes
  thought that if light took time, the Sun, the Earth and the Moon would be visibly out of line during an eclipse
  of the Moon. Pierre de Fermat disagreed and thought light's speed was finite.

??? note "Sources"
    - Empedocles, Aristotle, Heron, Ibn al-Haytham's finite and variable speed in 1021, al-Biruni, Kepler, Descartes's
      eclipse argument, and Fermat: Wikipedia, [Speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light)
      (weaker).
    - Ibn al-Haytham: chapter 1, section 1.2.

## 2.3 Galileo's lanterns { #galileo }

In 1638 Galileo described a test. Two people stand far apart, each with a covered lantern. The first uncovers his
lantern; as soon as the second sees it, he uncovers his. The first times how long it takes for the light to come
back. Galileo said he had tried it with the lanterns "at a short distance, less than a mile", and could not tell
whether light was instant or not, only that if it took time, it must be extraordinarily fast.

In 1667 the Accademia del Cimento in Florence tried again with lanterns about a mile apart and saw no delay. No
wonder: the real round trip takes about 11 millionths of a second, far shorter than anyone can react.

??? note "Sources"
    - Galileo's proposal of 1638, his lanterns "less than a mile" apart, his conclusion, the Accademia del Cimento in
      1667, and the true delay of about 11 microseconds: Wikipedia,
      [Speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light) (weaker).

## 2.4 Timing light with the sky { #sky }

The answer came from the sky, where the distances are big enough.

- **Rømer, 1676.** In Paris the Danish astronomer **Ole Rømer** timed the eclipses of Io, Jupiter's innermost large
  moon, as it went into Jupiter's shadow. They came early when the Earth was approaching Jupiter and late when it was
  moving away. He concluded that light takes about **22 minutes** to cross the Earth's orbit (history book,
  [chapter 16, section 16.3](../history/16-Newton-Halley-and-Romer.md#light)). Christiaan Huygens combined this with
  the size of the orbit and got about **220,000 km/s**, 27 per cent too low, but the first number for the speed of
  light. In 1704 Newton wrote that sunlight takes "seven or eight minutes" to reach us.
- **Bradley, 1729.** From 1725 the English astronomer **James Bradley** watched the star Eltanin, in Draco, and found
  it shifting back and forth through the year. He explained it in 1729: because the Earth is moving, starlight seems
  to come in at a slight slant, just as rain falling straight down seems to slant towards you when you walk, and you
  tilt your umbrella forward. This is **aberration**. From its size he found that light goes about 10,210 times
  faster than the Earth goes round the Sun; the modern figure is 10,066.

??? note "Sources"
    - Rømer's 22 minutes in 1676, Huygens's 220,000 km/s, 27 per cent low, Newton's "seven or eight minutes" in
      *Opticks*, and Bradley's 10,210 times, against the modern 10,066: Wikipedia,
      [Speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light) (weaker).
    - Bradley and Molyneux's observations of Eltanin from 1725, the explanation of 1729, and the rain and umbrella
      picture: Wikipedia, [Aberration (astronomy)](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Aberration_(astronomy)) (weaker).
    - Rømer: this site's history book, chapter 16, section 16.3, and its sources.

## 2.5 Fizeau's wheel { #fizeau }

The first measurement on the ground was made in 1848 and 1849 by the French physicist **Hippolyte Fizeau**. He sent a
bright beam from the bell tower of his father's house in Suresnes, near Paris, to a mirror on the hill of Montmartre,
**8,633 metres** away, and back. On its way out and on its way back, the beam passed the rim of a toothed wheel with
**720** notches.

With the wheel still, the returning light came back through the same gap. As Fizeau spun it faster, there came a
speed, **12.6 turns a second**, when the light vanished: in the time the light took to go to Montmartre and back, a
tooth had moved into its path. From the speed of the wheel and the distance, he could work out the speed of light
(section 2.11). He reported about 315,000 km/s.

In 1862 **Léon Foucault**, using a fast-spinning mirror instead of a wheel, got **298,000 km/s**, within 0.6 per
cent of the true value.

## 2.6 The speed that defines the metre { #metre }

Measurements kept getting better, until the speed of light was known more precisely than the metre itself. So in
**1983** the world turned it round: the speed of light was fixed at exactly 299,792,458 m/s, and the **metre** became
the distance light travels in empty space in 1/299,792,458 of a second. The second itself has been defined since 1967
by the light of caesium atoms: 9,192,631,770 of its vibrations.

**Slower in water and glass.** Light goes at $c$ only in empty space. In water or glass it goes more slowly. How
much more slowly is given by the **refractive index**, $n$: light goes at $c / n$. For water, Newton found a ratio of
"nearly 4 to 3", so $n \approx 1.33$ and light goes at about 225,000 km/s. For ordinary glass $n \approx 1.5$, about
200,000 km/s. This slowing is what makes lenses bend light (chapter 10).

??? note "Sources"
    - The metre defined since 1983 by the distance light travels in 1/299,792,458 s, and the second defined in 1967
      by 9,192,631,770 cycles of radiation from caesium-133: Wikipedia,
      [Speed of light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Speed_of_light) (weaker).
    - Newton's ratio for water, "529 to 396" or "nearly 4 to 3", and $n = 1.5$ for common glass: Wikipedia,
      [Refractive index](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Refractive_index) (weaker). The speeds are our arithmetic.

## 2.7 Light-years and looking back { #lookback }

Because light takes time, we always see things as they were when the light left them. You see the Sun as it was
8 minutes ago.

For the stars, astronomers measure distance by how long light takes. A **light-year** is the distance light travels
in a year of 365.25 days: about **9.46 trillion km**. The nearest star to the Sun, **Proxima Centauri**, is about
**4.25 light-years** away, so we see it as it was over four years ago.

## 2.8 Light and electricity { #maxwell }

In 1856, in Germany, Wilhelm Weber and Rudolf Kohlrausch measured a number from experiments with electric charge, by
discharging a Leyden jar. It came out very close to the speed of light that Fizeau had measured. A few years later
James Clerk Maxwell showed that electric and magnetic waves travel at exactly that speed, and concluded that light
is such a wave (chapter 1, section 1.6). The speed of light turned out to be built into electricity and magnetism
themselves.

Later, Einstein's theory of relativity made $c$ even more basic: it is the same for everyone, however they move, and
nothing can carry a message faster.

## 2.9 Talking to Mars { #mars }

The Earth and Mars are never closer than about 54.6 million km, and can be as far as 401.4 million km apart. So a radio
message, which travels at the speed of light, takes between about **3 and 22 minutes** to get there; on average about
14 minutes. Sunlight itself takes about 13 minutes to reach Mars.

- **No joystick.** A question to Mars and its answer take at least 6 minutes and up to 45. Nobody can steer a rover
  in real time. Instead, engineers send a day's plan, and the rovers use their own hazard cameras to steer round
  rocks by themselves.
- **Three stations round the world.** NASA talks to its spacecraft through the **Deep Space Network**, with big
  dishes, up to 70 m across, near Goldstone in California, near Madrid in Spain and near Canberra in Australia. As the
  Earth turns, at least one of them can always see Mars.
- **The Sun in the way.** About every 26 months Mars passes behind the Sun as seen from the Earth, a **solar
  conjunction**. The Sun's radio noise drowns the signals, so the rovers are left on their own. In one conjunction
  Curiosity was put in a self-run mode for 25 days, stopping its drives and doing only experiments that need no
  commands; Perseverance had one in October 2021.

## 2.10 Working it out { #working }

**Time = distance ÷ speed.** Use $c = 299{,}792$ km/s.

- The Moon, 384,400 km away: $384{,}400 / 299{,}792 = 1.28$ s.
- Mars at its closest: $54{,}600{,}000 / 299{,}792 = 182$ s, about 3.0 minutes.
- Mars at its farthest: $401{,}400{,}000 / 299{,}792 = 1{,}339$ s, about 22.3 minutes.

**How far is a light-year?** $299{,}792 \text{ km/s} \times 365.25 \times 86{,}400 \text{ s} = 9.46 \times 10^{12}$ km.

**Laser to the Moon.** Since 21 July 1969, when the crew of Apollo 11 left a set of reflectors on the Moon,
observatories have fired laser pulses at them and timed the echoes. The round trip takes about 2.5 seconds. Half the
time, times $c$, gives the distance, now measured to within a millimetre. It shows that the Moon is moving away from
us by 3.8 cm a year.

| | Mars | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Average distance from the Sun | 1.524 AU | 1 AU |
| Sunlight takes | $1.524 \times 499 = 760$ s, about 12.7 minutes | 499 s, 8 minutes 19 seconds |
| A signal to the other planet takes | 3.0 to 22.3 minutes | 3.0 to 22.3 minutes |
| A question and its answer take at least | 6.1 to 44.6 minutes | 6.1 to 44.6 minutes |

## 2.11 Two old measurements, done again { #measure }

**Fizeau's wheel.** The wheel had 720 teeth and 720 gaps, so going from a gap to the next tooth takes half of one
720th of a turn. At 12.6 turns a second, that takes

$$t = \frac{1}{2 \times 720 \times 12.6} = 5.51 \times 10^{-5} \text{ s}.$$

In that time the light went to Montmartre and back, $2 \times 8{,}633 = 17{,}266$ m. So

$$c = \frac{17{,}266}{5.51 \times 10^{-5}} = 4 \times 8{,}633 \times 720 \times 12.6 = 313{,}274{,}304 \text{ m/s},$$

about 313,000 km/s, 4.5 per cent too high. Fizeau's own reported value was a little different.

**Bradley's aberration.** If you walk at speed $v$ through rain falling at speed $c$, the rain seems to slant by an
angle whose tangent is $v / c$. For starlight and the moving Earth, the angle is tiny, so in radians it is just
$v / c$. The Earth goes round the Sun at 29.78 km/s, and stars swing back and forth by up to

$$\theta = \frac{v}{c} = \frac{1}{10{,}066} = 9.93 \times 10^{-5} \text{ rad}
= 9.93 \times 10^{-5} \times 206{,}265 = 20.5''.$$

Turned round, Bradley's measured angle gives the speed of light: $c = v / \theta = 29.78 \times 10{,}066 = 299{,}770$
km/s.

**How precise is laser ranging?** A millimetre more distance adds 2 mm to the round trip, which takes light
$0.002 / 299{,}792{,}458 = 6.7 \times 10^{-12}$ s: the clocks must tell apart times a few millionths of a millionth of a
second.

    - The constant of aberration, 20.49552″, the rain picture, and $\theta \approx v/c$: Wikipedia,

**3.** (beginner) What is a light-year?

??? note "Answer"
    The distance light travels in a year, about 9.46 trillion km. It is a distance, not a time.

**4.** (intermediate) How did Rømer know that light takes time?

??? note "Answer"
    The eclipses of Io came early when the Earth was approaching Jupiter and late when it was moving away, because the
    light had less or more distance to travel.

**5.** (intermediate) Why can't engineers drive a Mars rover with a joystick?

??? note "Answer"
    A signal takes 3 to 22 minutes each way, so a command and the picture of what happened take 6 to 45 minutes. The
    rover would have crashed long before they saw it.

**6.** (intermediate) How fast does light go in glass with a refractive index of 1.5?

??? note "Answer"
    $c / n = 299{,}792 / 1.5 \approx 200{,}000$ km/s.

**7.** (advanced) On 27 August 2003 Mars was 55.76 million km from the Earth. How long did its light take to reach
us?

??? note "Answer"
    $55{,}760{,}000 / 299{,}792 = 186$ s, about 3.1 minutes.

**8.** (expert) Suppose Fizeau had used a wheel with 500 teeth and the same 8,633 m distance. At how many turns a
second would the light first vanish?

??? note "Answer"
    The time for half a tooth step must equal the round trip:
    $1 / (2 \times 500 \times f) = 17{,}266 / 299{,}792{,}458$, so
    $f = 299{,}792{,}458 / (1000 \times 17{,}266) = 17.4$ turns a second.

## Summary

- Light travels at exactly 299,792,458 m/s in empty space: 1.3 s from the Moon, 8 min 19 s from the Sun.
- Thinkers from Empedocles to Ibn al-Haytham and al-Biruni argued it takes time; Kepler and Descartes thought it
  instant.
- Galileo's lanterns were far too slow a test.
- Rømer (1676) timed light with Io's eclipses; Bradley (1729) found it from the aberration of starlight.
- Fizeau (1849) measured it on the ground with a toothed wheel, Foucault (1862) with a spinning mirror.
- Since 1983 the speed of light defines the metre. Light is slower in water and glass, by the refractive index.
- A light-year is 9.46 trillion km; we see distant things as they were.
- Messages to Mars take 3 to 22 minutes, so rovers must look after themselves, and fall silent at solar conjunction.
