# 9. Mirrors and reflection

*Almost every big telescope in the world gathers light with a curved mirror. This chapter starts with the simple rule
that a mirror follows and the 8,000-year story of mirrors, from polished stone in Anatolia and the Americas to
bronze in China and silvered glass in Germany. It explains why a curved mirror brings light to a focus, why
telescope mirrors are shaped as parabolas rather than parts of spheres, and how mirrors went from metal to silver to
aluminium, and even to spinning pools of mercury in Canada and India. It ends with the mirrors left on the Moon and
on Mars, and with the shape of a spinning liquid.*

## 9.1 Bouncing light { #law }

Shine a torch at a mirror and the beam bounces off. Draw a line at right angles to the mirror where the beam hits it;
this line is called the **normal**. Then:

- the beam comes off at the **same angle** to the normal as it went in, on the other side of the normal;
- the beam going in, the beam coming out and the normal all lie in one flat plane.

This is the **law of reflection**. The earliest known description of it is by Hero of Alexandria, in the first
century AD. Ibn al-Haytham (Alhazen), working in Cairo about a thousand years later (history book,
[chapter 8, section 8.7](../history/08-The-Islamic-World-and-Persia.md#doubts)), gave the full statement,
including the rule that all three lines lie in one plane.

A mirror works because its surface is very smooth, so all the light bounces off in step. A sheet of white paper
reflects most of the light that falls on it too, but its surface is rough, so the light is scattered in all
directions. That is called **diffuse reflection**, and it is why you can see paper from anywhere in a room but cannot
see your face in it.

## 9.2 Eight thousand years of mirrors { #ancient }

- **Volcanic glass.** The earliest made mirrors were polished stone, such as **obsidian**, a natural volcanic glass.
  Obsidian mirrors from Çatalhöyük in Anatolia, in modern Turkey, date from around 6000 BCE. Polished stone mirrors
  from Central and South America date from around 2000 BCE onwards.
- **Metal.** Mirrors of polished copper were made in Mesopotamia from 4000 BCE and in Egypt from around 3000 BCE. In
  China, bronze mirrors were made from around 2000 BCE, some of the earliest by the Qijia culture.
- **The smoking mirror.** In Mexico, the Aztec god **Tezcatlipoca**'s name is often translated as "smoking mirror",
  after the obsidian from which mirrors were made in Mesoamerica.
- **Magic mirrors.** Some Chinese bronze mirrors, known from at least the 5th century, seem to become transparent:
  when sunlight bounces off the polished face onto a wall, the pattern cast on the back of the mirror appears in the
  patch of light. The scholar Shen Kuo (1031–1095) owned three as family heirlooms and wrote about them.
- **Glass.** The Roman writer Pliny the Elder says that craftsmen at Sidon, in modern Lebanon, made glass mirrors
  backed with lead or gold leaf. A way of making flat glass for mirrors, by blowing a cylinder of glass, slicing it
  open and unrolling it, was developed in Germany and perfected in Venice by the 16th century. In 1835 the German
  chemist **Justus von Liebig** found how to coat glass with a thin layer of silver, the start of the modern mirror.

## 9.3 Curved mirrors { #curved }

A mirror does not have to be flat.

- A **concave** mirror is hollowed in, like the inside of a spoon. Parallel rays of light, such as light from a
  distant star, bounce off it and meet at one point, the **focus**. The distance from the mirror to the focus is the
  **focal length**. For a mirror shaped like part of a sphere, the focal length is half the radius of the sphere.
- A **convex** mirror bulges out, like the back of a spoon. It spreads light out, so it shows a small image of a wide
  view. Shops, car parks, bends in the road and the passenger side of a car use convex mirrors for this.

A telescope's main mirror is concave. It collects light from a star over its whole surface and brings it all
together at the focus, where it makes an image (chapter 11).

    - Concave and convex mirrors, the centre of curvature at twice the focal length, and the uses of convex mirrors:
      Wikipedia,
      [Curved mirror](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Curved_mirror) (weaker).

## 9.4 Burning mirrors { #burning }

A concave mirror pointed at the Sun brings sunlight to a hot spot at its focus. Mathematicians in the ancient and
medieval world wrote whole books on these "burning mirrors".

- **Diocles**, a Greek mathematician, wrote *On Burning Mirrors* and is thought to be the first to prove that a
  mirror shaped as a **parabola** brings parallel rays to a single point. The Greek original is lost; the book
  survives in an Arabic translation and in pieces quoted by later Greek writers.
- **Archimedes** is said to have set the Roman fleet on fire with mirrors during the siege of Syracuse. The story was
  written down by Lucian in the 2nd century AD, centuries later. Modern tests disagree. In 2005 students at MIT aimed
  127 small flat mirrors at a model wooden ship about 30 m away; flames broke out, but only after about ten minutes
  of cloudless sky with the ship standing still. The television programme *MythBusters* judged the story "busted"
  in 2006, because it needed so long and such perfect weather.
- **Ibn Sahl**, at the court of Baghdad, wrote a book on burning instruments around 984. He studied curved mirrors and
  lenses, and has been called the discoverer of the law of refraction (chapter 10).

??? note "Sources"
    - Diocles, *On Burning Mirrors*, the first proof of the focal property of the parabola, the fragments kept by

## 9.5 Sphere or parabola? { #parabola }

A mirror shaped like part of a sphere is the easiest curve to grind and polish. But it does not bring all parallel
rays to the same point. Rays that hit near the edge cross the axis a little closer to the mirror than rays that hit
near the middle, so the image is blurred. This fault is called **spherical aberration**.

A mirror shaped as a **parabola** has no such fault: every ray parallel to the axis is reflected through the same
focus.

In 1663 the mathematician **James Gregory** pointed out that a telescope with a parabolic mirror would avoid
both spherical aberration and the colour fringes of lens telescopes (chapter 10). Isaac Newton knew this, but chose a
spherical mirror for his own telescope because it was easier to make. Large telescope mirrors today are parabolas or
close relatives of them (chapter 13).

## 9.6 Metal, silver and aluminium { #coatings }

- **Speculum metal.** Newton's first reflecting telescope of 1668, the earliest known to work, had a metal mirror only
  33 mm across. Mirrors like his were made of **speculum metal**, about two-thirds copper and one-third tin. It
  reflected only about 66% of the light, tarnished, and had to be polished again and again. William Herschel's
  telescopes and the "Leviathan" built in Ireland in 1845, with a metal mirror 1.8 m across, used it too (history
  book, [chapter 22, section 22.1](../history/22-Astronomy-Today.md#giants)).
- **Silver on glass.** In 1856 and 1857 **Karl August von Steinheil** and **Léon Foucault**
  coated the front of polished glass with a very thin layer of silver. These were the first glass mirrors good enough
  for telescopes, and they replaced speculum metal.
- **Aluminium.** In 1930 **John Strong**, a physicist and astronomer at Caltech, invented a way to coat glass with
  aluminium in a vacuum. Most reflecting telescopes moved to aluminium. The 5.1 m Hale Telescope at Palomar, finished
  in 1948, had an aluminium-coated glass mirror.
- **Segments.** The largest mirrors are now made of many pieces fitted together. Each Keck telescope in Hawaii,
  finished in 1993 and 1996, has 36 hexagonal segments making a 10 m mirror; the James Webb Space Telescope has 18;
  the Extremely Large Telescope in Chile will have 798 (chapters 16 and 19).

## 9.7 Mirrors of spinning mercury { #liquid }

Spin a bowl of liquid and its surface hollows into a curve. That curve is a parabola (section 9.11), exactly the
shape a telescope mirror needs. If the liquid is mercury, a shiny metal, the bowl becomes a mirror.

- **Newton** noticed that a spinning liquid takes this shape, but could not build such a telescope because he could
  not keep the speed steady.
- **Ernesto Capocci** of the Naples Observatory took the idea further in 1850, and in 1872 **Henry Skey** of Dunedin,
  New Zealand, built the first working liquid-mirror telescope, in a laboratory.
- The **Large Zenith Telescope** near Vancouver, Canada, had a spinning mercury mirror 6 m across, the largest
  liquid-metal mirror ever built. It worked from 2003 to 2016.
- The **International Liquid Mirror Telescope**, 4 m across, at the Aryabhatta Research Institute of Observational
  Sciences (ARIES) in Nainital, India, saw its first light on 2 June 2022. It is the first liquid-mirror telescope for
  astronomy in Asia, and now the largest working one in the world.

A liquid mirror is far cheaper than a solid one of the same size. But it can only look **straight up**, because
gravity keeps the liquid level. It watches whatever strip of sky the Earth's turning carries overhead.

## 9.8 Mirrors on the Moon { #moon }

A flat mirror sends light back towards where it came from only if it faces the source exactly. A **corner reflector**
does better. It has three flat mirrors at right angles to each other, like the inside corner of a cube. Light
entering from almost any direction bounces off all three and leaves going straight back the way it came.

- **1962.** Scientists at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, and later the same year a Soviet team at the
  Crimean Astrophysical Observatory, bounced laser pulses off the bare surface of the Moon.
- **1969 onwards.** The crew of Apollo 11 set up an array of corner reflectors on 21 July 1969, and Apollo 14 and 15
  left two more. The Soviet robot rovers Lunokhod 1 and 2 carried reflectors too, the one on Lunokhod 1 made in
  France. India's Chandrayaan-3 lander added another.

Observatories fire laser pulses at these reflectors and time the echoes. The round trip takes about 2.5 seconds. Of
about $3 \times 10^{17}$ photons in each pulse aimed at a reflector, only 1 to 5 come back to Earth. Even so, the
distance to the Moon can be measured to within millimetres, and it shows that the Moon is moving away from the Earth
by about 3.8 cm a year.

## 9.9 Mirrors at Mars { #mars }

**The biggest telescope at another planet.** The **HiRISE** camera on NASA's Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter, which
reached Mars on 10 March 2006, is a reflecting telescope with a mirror 0.5 m across, the largest so far on any
mission into deep space. From a height of 300 km it can pick out details 0.3 m across, about a foot.

**Little mirrors for the future.** Two landers have carried small corner reflectors provided by the Italian Space
Agency, so that one day an orbiter could find them with a laser, as observatories find the reflectors on the Moon.

- **INRRI** rode on Europe's Schiaparelli lander. On 19 October 2016 Schiaparelli tried to land, but its signals,
  followed live by the Giant Metrewave Radio Telescope in India, were lost during the descent. On 21 October NASA
  released a picture from the Mars Reconnaissance Orbiter showing what appeared to be its crash site.
- **LaRRI**, on NASA's InSight lander, is a small aluminium dome 54 mm across, weighing 25 g, with eight corner
  reflectors of fused silica. It sits on the lander's top deck and needs no power, so it can still be used now that

## 9.10 Working it out { #working }

**Tilting a mirror.** If a mirror is tilted by an angle $\theta$, the normal tilts by $\theta$ too, so the angle in
and the angle out each change by $\theta$, and the reflected beam swings by $2\theta$. Tilt a mirror by 10° and the
beam moves by 20°.

**Focal length.** For a spherical mirror of radius $R$, the focal length is $f = R/2$. A mirror ground to a sphere of
radius 2 m has a focal length of 1 m.

**Light-gathering power.** A mirror collects light in proportion to its area, and so to its diameter squared.

| Mirror | Diameter | Light compared with Newton's |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Newton, 1668 | 33 mm | 1 |
| Hale, 1948 | 5.1 m | $(5{,}100/33)^2 \approx 23{,}900$ |
| Keck, 1993 | 10 m | $(10{,}000/33)^2 \approx 91{,}800$ |

**Timing the Moon.** Distance is half the round trip times the speed of light. A round trip of 2.56 s gives
$299{,}792 \times 2.56 / 2 = 383{,}700$ km.

| | Mars | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sunlight on 1 m² of mirror, above the air | 586 W | 1,361 W |
| Mirror area to collect 1 kW of sunlight | 1.7 m² | 0.73 m² |
| Gravity | 3.73 m/s² | 9.82 m/s² |
| Spin for a liquid mirror with an 8 m focal length (section 9.11) | 4.6 turns a minute | 7.5 turns a minute |

A burning mirror on Mars would need well over twice the area to gather the same power, and a liquid mirror there
could spin more slowly.

## 9.11 The shape of a spinning liquid { #spin }

Take a bowl of liquid spinning at $\omega$ radians per second about a vertical axis. A small piece of the surface at
distance $r$ from the axis needs a pull of $\omega^2 r$ (per kilogram) towards the axis to keep going round, and
gravity pulls it down with $g$. The surface settles at right angles to the combined pull, so its slope is

$$\frac{dz}{dr} = \frac{\omega^2 r}{g}, \qquad \text{so} \qquad z = \frac{\omega^2 r^2}{2g}.$$

That is a parabola. A parabola $z = r^2/(4f)$ has its focus at height $f$, so matching the two,

$$f = \frac{g}{2\omega^2}.$$

For an 8 m focal length on Earth, $\omega = \sqrt{9.82 / 16} = 0.783$ radians per second, or 7.5 turns a minute; on
Mars, $\omega = \sqrt{3.73 / 16} = 0.483$, or 4.6 turns a minute.

**How far is a sphere from a parabola?** A sphere of radius $R = 2f$ has the same focal length near its centre as the
parabola, but at distance $r$ from the axis the two surfaces differ by about $r^4 / (64 f^3)$.

- Newton's mirror: 33 mm across at f/5, so $f = 165$ mm and $r = 16.5$ mm. The difference is
  $16.5^4 / (64 \times 165^3) = 0.00026$ mm, about half the wavelength of green light (550 nm). A sphere was good
  enough.
- The Hale mirror: 5.1 m across at f/3.3, so $f = 16.8$ m and $r = 2.55$ m. The difference is
  $2.55^4 / (64 \times 16.8^3) = 0.00014$ m, or 0.14 mm, about 250 wavelengths. Only a true parabola will do.

Big, short-focus mirrors have to be parabolas; small, long-focus ones can get away with spheres.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) State the law of reflection.

??? note "Answer"
    Light leaves a mirror at the same angle to the normal as it arrived, on the other side of the normal, and both
    rays and the normal lie in one plane.

**2.** (beginner) What were the earliest made mirrors made of, and where have some of the oldest been found?

??? note "Answer"
    Polished stone such as obsidian; some from Çatalhöyük in Anatolia date from around 6000 BCE.

**3.** (beginner) Why can you not see your face in a sheet of white paper, even though it reflects most of the light?

??? note "Answer"
    Its surface is rough, so it scatters the light in all directions (diffuse reflection) instead of sending it off
    in step.

**4.** (intermediate) Why are large telescope mirrors made as parabolas rather than parts of spheres?

??? note "Answer"
    A spherical mirror brings edge rays to a focus closer than central rays (spherical aberration). A parabola brings
    all parallel rays to one focus.

**5.** (intermediate) What replaced speculum metal, and why?

??? note "Answer"
    Glass coated with silver, from 1856 to 1857, and later with aluminium, from 1930. Speculum reflected only about 66%
    of the light and tarnished.

**6.** (intermediate) Why are corner reflectors, not flat mirrors, left on the Moon?

??? note "Answer"
    A corner reflector sends light straight back the way it came from almost any direction, so it does not have to
    be aimed at the Earth.

**7.** (advanced) The Keck mirror is 10 m across and the Hale mirror 5.1 m. How many times more light does Keck
collect?

??? note "Answer"
    $(10 / 5.1)^2 = 3.8$ times.

**8.** (expert) A liquid mirror on Earth spins at 6 turns a minute. What is its focal length? What would it be on Mars
at the same spin?

??? note "Answer"
    $\omega = 6 \times 2\pi / 60 = 0.628$ radians per second. On Earth $f = 9.82 / (2 \times 0.395) = 12.4$ m; on Mars
    $f = 3.73 / (2 \times 0.395) = 4.7$ m.

## Summary

- Light leaves a mirror at the same angle to the normal as it arrives; rough surfaces scatter light instead.
- Mirrors began as polished obsidian about 8,000 years ago, then copper and bronze, then glass; silvered glass came
  in 1835.
- A concave mirror brings parallel light to a focus; for a sphere the focal length is half the radius.
- Diocles proved that a parabola focuses parallel light to a point; Ibn Sahl wrote on burning instruments in Baghdad.
- Spherical mirrors blur the image; telescope mirrors are parabolas.
- Telescope mirrors went from speculum metal to silver on glass (1856–1857) to aluminium (1930) and to segments.
- Spinning mercury makes a cheap parabolic mirror that can only look straight up, as in India's 4 m telescope.
- Corner reflectors on the Moon let us time laser echoes to millimetres; small ones now sit on Mars too.
- A spinning liquid has focal length $f = g / (2\omega^2)$.
