# 18. Infrared, ultraviolet, X-ray and gamma-ray astronomy

*Chapter 4 showed that visible light is a narrow slice of a much wider spectrum, and that the Earth's air blocks most
of the rest. This chapter is about how astronomers catch the blocked parts. It explains why infrared telescopes must
be kept colder than a winter night, why ultraviolet telescopes ride on rockets, why an X-ray mirror only works if the
X-rays barely touch it, and why gamma rays cannot be focused at all, though their flashes in the air can be caught
from the ground in Namibia and on the edge of Tibet. It ends with gamma rays that found ice under the ground of Mars,
and an Emirati spacecraft that watches the top of Mars's air in ultraviolet.*

!!! abstract "In this chapter"
    **You will learn:**

    - how high a telescope must go to catch each kind of light
    - why infrared telescopes and cameras are cooled, and where they are flown
    - how ultraviolet astronomy works from space
    - how X-rays are focused by mirrors at glancing angles
    - how gamma rays are detected without any mirror or lens
    - how telescopes on the ground catch gamma rays by the light they make in the air
    - what gamma rays, neutrons and ultraviolet light have shown about Mars
    - why a third of some gamma rays from space would reach the ground on Mars

    **You need:** chapter 4 on the bands of the spectrum, chapter 6 on glowing bodies, and chapter 14 on detectors.

    **Levels:** 18.1 and 18.2 are beginner; 18.3 to 18.7 are intermediate; 18.8 is advanced; 18.9 is expert.

## 18.2 Infrared: telescopes that must be cold { #infrared }

Everything warm glows in the infrared (chapter 6, [section 6.1](06-Hot-Things-Glow.md#glow)). That includes the
telescope, its dome, the air above, and the detector itself. If infrared detectors were not cooled, their own glow
would swamp the light from the sky. So infrared cameras are chilled with liquid nitrogen, at 77 K, and some with liquid
helium, at 4 K, and the telescope is shielded from warm things around it. A common detector is an array of
mercury-cadmium-telluride (HgCdTe), working much like the CCD of chapter 14.

**On mountains.** Infrared astronomy began in the 1830s. In 1856 Charles Piazzi Smyth, Astronomer Royal for Scotland,
took instruments up Tenerife in the Canary Islands to test mountain-top astronomy, and detected the infrared warmth of
the Moon. In October 1979 the 3.8 m **United Kingdom Infrared Telescope** began work on Mauna Kea; NASA's **Infrared
Telescope Facility** was built there at the same time. With adaptive optics (chapter 12), infrared light also sees
through the dust to the centre of the Milky Way. By following stars orbiting there, using adaptive optics on the Keck
telescopes, **Andrea Ghez** showed that a giant, compact mass lies at the centre. She shared the Nobel Prize in Physics
in 2020 with **Reinhard Genzel**.

**In the air.** NASA's **Kuiper Airborne Observatory**, a 91.5 cm telescope in a jet transport aircraft, flew from 1974
to 1995 at up to 14 km. It found the rings of Uranus in 1977 and confirmed that Pluto has an atmosphere in 1988. Its
successor, **SOFIA**, built by NASA with the German Aerospace Center, carried a 2.5 m telescope in a Boeing 747 with a
door that opened in flight. It made 921 flights from 2010 to 2022.

**In space.** The first survey of the whole sky in infrared was made by **IRAS**, built by the United States, the
Netherlands and the United Kingdom. Launched on 25 January 1983, it mapped 96% of the sky and found over 250,000
sources, until its liquid helium ran out ten months later. **Spitzer** worked from 2003 to 2020; in 2016 it helped to
find five of the seven Earth-sized planets of the star TRAPPIST-1. The James Webb Space Telescope (chapter 19) works
mostly in the infrared.

## 18.3 Ultraviolet: above the ozone { #uv }

Ultraviolet astronomy works between about 10 and 320 nm, light that the ozone high in the air stops (chapter 4). Its
telescopes are mirror telescopes much like those for visible light, but they must be above the air. Ultraviolet shows
hot young stars, and the gas between the stars (chapter 4, [section 4.6](04-Beyond-the-Rainbow.md#sky)).

- **The International Ultraviolet Explorer**, proposed by British scientists in 1964 and built by NASA, the United
  Kingdom and ESA, was launched on 26 January 1978. Its mirror was only 45 cm across. It was placed in an orbit that
  kept it in constant touch with ground stations in Maryland and in Spain. Planned for 3 years, it lasted 18, and made
  over 104,000 observations.
- **GALEX**, a 50 cm NASA telescope, surveyed the whole sky in ultraviolet from 2003 to 2012.
- **AstroSat**, India's first space observatory for many bands at once, launched in 2015, carries an Ultra Violet
  Imaging Telescope alongside its X-ray instruments.

## 18.4 X-rays: mirrors at a glancing angle { #xray }

Aim X-rays at an ordinary mirror and they go into it and are absorbed, without turning. They bounce off only if they
hit at a very shallow angle, between about 10 minutes of arc and 2°, like a stone skipping on water. In 1952 **Hans
Wolter** worked out how to build a telescope from such grazing mirrors. The X-rays glance first off a gently tapering
tube shaped as a paraboloid, then off a second tube shaped as a hyperboloid, and come to a focus.

Each tube catches only a thin ring of X-rays, so the tubes are **nested** one inside another.

- **Uhuru**, the first satellite built for X-ray astronomy, was launched on 12 December 1970 from an Italian platform
  off the coast of Kenya. Its name means "freedom" in Swahili, in thanks to Kenya. Its catalogue, the first of the
  whole X-ray sky, listed 339 sources. **Riccardo Giacconi** received the Nobel Prize in 2002 for the discoveries
  that began with Scorpius X-1 in 1962 (chapter 4).
- **ROSAT**, led by Germany, mapped the whole X-ray sky from 1990 to 1999.
- **Chandra**, NASA's X-ray telescope, launched in 1999, has four nested pairs of mirrors up to 1.23 m across, coated
  with iridium. It sees detail of half a second of arc, more than 1,000 times sharper than the first X-ray telescope
  in orbit, and sources 100 times fainter than any X-ray telescope before it. It is named after the Indian-American
  astrophysicist Subrahmanyan Chandrasekhar.
- **XMM-Newton**, Europe's X-ray telescope, launched the same year, has three telescopes, each of 58 nested mirrors,
  to collect as many X-rays as possible.
- **Einstein Probe**, from the Chinese Academy of Sciences with ESA and Germany's Max Planck Institute, launched on
  9 January 2024. Its "lobster-eye" optics, named after the eyes of lobsters, watch 3,600 square degrees of sky at once,
  almost a tenth of the whole sky, looking for sudden flashes.

## 18.5 Gamma rays: no mirrors at all { #gamma }

Gamma rays carry so much energy that they are very hard to focus. They are also rare: even a bright source
may take minutes to show up. The first gamma-ray telescope in orbit, on Explorer 11 in 1961, caught fewer than 100
gamma rays from space.

Instead of focusing, a gamma-ray telescope lets the gamma ray turn into particles, and tracks them. In the **Large
Area Telescope** on NASA's **Fermi** satellite, launched on 11 June 2008, a gamma ray turns into an electron and its
antiparticle, a positron. Layers of detectors follow their paths, which point back to where the gamma ray came from.
Fermi sees about a fifth of the sky at once and sweeps the whole sky every three hours. Before it, the **Compton Gamma
Ray Observatory** (from 1991) found many gamma-ray sources. **INTEGRAL** and **AGILE** are among the other gamma-ray
observatories in orbit.

??? note "Sources"
    - Gamma rays rare and hard to focus, minutes to detect a bright source, Explorer 11 in 1961 with fewer than 100
      photons, the Compton Gamma Ray Observatory of 1991, and INTEGRAL, Fermi and AGILE: Wikipedia, Gamma-ray astronomy
      (above).
    - Fermi: launched on 11 June 2008, the LAT as a pair-conversion instrument with a tracker, about 20% of the sky in
      view, the whole sky every three hours: Wikipedia,
      [Fermi Gamma-ray Space Telescope](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Fermi_Gamma-ray_Space_Telescope) (weaker). That
      the pair is an electron and a positron is standard physics.

## 18.6 Catching gamma rays from the ground { #cherenkov }

The most energetic gamma rays are too rare even for Fermi to catch many. But they can be caught from the ground.

When such a gamma ray hits the top of the air, 10 to 20 km up, it sets off a shower of fast charged particles. They
move faster than light itself travels in air, and give off a faint flash of blue light, **Cherenkov light**, lasting
only a few billionths of a second. A large segmented mirror collects the flash onto a camera of very fast light
sensors. Several telescopes together see the flash from different sides and work out where the gamma ray came from.

- **Arizona, 1989.** The 10 m Whipple telescope pioneered the method and found very high-energy gamma rays from the
  Crab Nebula, the remains of the star of 1054 (history book,
  [chapter 4, section 4.7](../history/04-China.md#guests)).
- **Namibia, 2002 to 2004.** **H.E.S.S.**, named after Victor Hess, who discovered cosmic rays, has four telescopes
  of about 12 m
  on a square with 120 m sides in the Khomas highlands, and a fifth with a 28 m mirror in the middle.
- **Elsewhere.** **MAGIC** and **VERITAS** work the same way. The **Cherenkov Telescope Array** is
  being built to be ten times more sensitive.
- **Sichuan, 2021.** **LHAASO**, 4,410 m up in Daocheng, in a Tibetan area of Sichuan, catches the particles of the
  showers themselves, with detectors that include huge pools of water. By 2021 it had caught gamma rays of up to 1.4
  PeV, about 560 million million times the energy of visible light.

## 18.7 Mars in invisible light { #mars }

Chapter 4 showed Mars in radio, radar, infrared and ultraviolet (chapter 4,
[section 4.7](04-Beyond-the-Rainbow.md#mars)), and chapter 6 how it glows in the infrared. Here are two more.

**Gamma rays and ice.** **Mars Odyssey**, in orbit since 24 October 2001, carries a **Gamma Ray Spectrometer**, built by
the University of Arizona, the Los Alamos National Laboratory and Russia's Space Research Institute, which provided its
neutron detector, HEND. The ground of Mars, struck by fast particles from space, gives off gamma rays and neutrons
whose energies reveal which elements are present. Hydrogen slows neutrons down very well. On 28 May 2002 NASA reported
that Odyssey had found large amounts of hydrogen: ice lying within a metre of the surface. Odyssey is
now the longest-working spacecraft at any planet other than Earth.

**Ultraviolet from the top of the air.** The United Arab Emirates' **Hope** probe, in orbit since 9 February 2021
(history book, [chapter 8, section 8.11](../history/08-The-Islamic-World-and-Persia.md#mars)), carries an ultraviolet
spectrograph, **EMUS**, working from 100 to 170 nm. It measures the thin outermost air of Mars, and the clouds of
hydrogen and oxygen atoms that surround the planet, as they slowly escape into space.

??? note "Sources"

## 18.8 Working it out { #working }

**Cold enough?** By Wien's law (chapter 6), a body at temperature $T$ glows most strongly at
$\lambda = 2.9\text{ mm} / T$.

| Body | Temperature | Strongest glow |
| --- | --- | --- |
| The Sun's surface | 5,772 K | 0.50 µm, visible |
| A telescope on a freezing night | 273 K | 10.6 µm, infrared |
| Liquid nitrogen | 77 K | 38 µm, infrared |
| Liquid helium | 4 K | 0.72 mm, far infrared, near the edge of radio |

A telescope at 0 °C glows brightest right in the middle of the infrared, which is why infrared detectors are cooled
far below it.

**Energy per photon.** A photon's energy in electronvolts is about $1{,}240 / \lambda$, with $\lambda$ in nanometres.
Visible light at 500 nm carries 2.5 eV, ultraviolet at 150 nm 8.3 eV, an X-ray at 1 nm 1,240 eV, and a 1 MeV gamma ray
a million. LHAASO's 1.4 PeV is $1.4\times10^{15} / 2.5 = 5.6\times10^{14}$ times a photon of visible light.

**Would gamma rays reach the ground on Mars?** Each square centimetre of the ground lies under a column of air of mass
$p/g$. A beam of gamma rays falls off as $e^{-\mu m}$, where $m$ is that mass per square centimetre and $\mu$ is
measured for each energy.

| Straight down through the air | Mars | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Air above each square centimetre | 17 g | 1,033 g |
| X-rays of 10 keV getting through | none ($e^{-87}$) | none |
| Gamma rays of 100 keV getting through | 7% | none ($e^{-159}$) |
| Gamma rays of 1 MeV getting through | 34% | none ($e^{-66}$) |
| Gamma rays of 10 MeV getting through | 71% | none ($e^{-21}$) |

On Mars, a gamma-ray telescope for the higher energies could work on the ground, where on Earth it must go to space.

## 18.9 Why X-rays glance and showers glow { #theory }

**Glancing X-rays.** For X-rays, the refractive index of a metal is slightly *less* than 1: $n = 1 - \delta$, with
$\delta$ very small. Light going from vacuum, where $n = 1$, into the metal is bending into a medium of lower index,
so at shallow enough angles it cannot get in, and is totally reflected, just as light is trapped inside glass (chapter
10, [section 10.3](10-Lenses-and-Refraction.md#trapped)), except that here it happens on the outside of the metal.
Measure angles from the surface. Snell's law (chapter 10, [section 10.2](10-Lenses-and-Refraction.md#law)) becomes
$\cos\theta_1 = n\cos\theta_2$, and total reflection happens when $\cos\theta < n$, below the critical angle

$$\cos\theta_c = 1 - \delta \quad\Rightarrow\quad \theta_c \approx \sqrt{2\delta},$$

using $\cos\theta \approx 1 - \theta^2/2$. Turning it round, the grazing angles of real X-ray mirrors, 10 minutes of
arc to 2°, mean $\delta = \theta_c^2/2$ of about $4\times10^{-6}$ to $6\times10^{-4}$: the index differs from 1 by only
a few parts in a million to a few in ten thousand.

**Glowing showers.** In air, light travels at $c/n$, where $n \approx 1.00029$ at sea level. A particle moving at speed
$v$ faster than that leaves a cone of light behind it, like the bow wave of a boat, at an angle

$$\cos\theta = \frac{c}{nv} \approx \frac{1}{n} \quad\Rightarrow\quad \theta \approx \sqrt{2(n-1)}$$

for $v \approx c$. At 10 km up, where the air is a third as dense, $n - 1 \approx 0.0001$, so $\theta \approx 0.8°$.
Light leaving at 0.8° from 10 km up spreads over a circle about $10{,}000 \times 0.014 \approx 140$ m in radius on the
ground. Any telescope inside that pool of light can see the flash, which is why the telescopes of an array stand about
100 m apart. All the light arrives within a few billionths of a second, since it and the particles travel at nearly the
same speed.

      X-ray optics (above). The angles: Wikipedia, Wolter telescope (above).
    - The refractive index of air, about 1.00029 at sea level, and air at 10 km being about a third as dense as at sea
      level, are standard values.
    - The derivations are ours. H.E.S.S.'s 120 m square: section 18.6.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Why must many infrared, ultraviolet, X-ray and gamma-ray telescopes go high up?

??? note "Answer"
    The Earth's air absorbs most of this light: water vapour blocks much of the infrared, ozone the ultraviolet, and the
    whole air the X-rays and gamma rays. Only radio and visible light pass freely.

**2.** (beginner) Why are infrared detectors cooled?

??? note "Answer"
    Everything warm glows in the infrared, including the detector and telescope. Without cooling, their own glow would
    swamp the faint light from the sky.

**3.** (beginner) What did the Kuiper Airborne Observatory discover in 1977?

??? note "Answer"
    The rings of Uranus.

**4.** (intermediate) Why do X-ray telescopes use nested tubes instead of a dish-shaped mirror?

??? note "Answer"
    X-rays reflect only at glancing angles, under about 2°. So the mirrors are long, nearly straight tubes that the
    X-rays skim along; each tube catches only a thin ring of X-rays, so many are nested to collect more.

**5.** (intermediate) How does Fermi's Large Area Telescope tell where a gamma ray came from?

??? note "Answer"
    The gamma ray turns into an electron and a positron in a thin metal sheet. Layers of detectors track their paths,
    which point back towards the source.

**6.** (intermediate) How did Mars Odyssey find ice under the ground?

??? note "Answer"
    Its Gamma Ray Spectrometer and neutron detector measured gamma rays and neutrons coming from the ground. Their
    pattern showed large amounts of hydrogen, which means ice within a metre of the surface.

**7.** (advanced) At what wavelength does a body at 30 K glow most strongly?

??? note "Answer"
    $2.9\text{ mm} / 30 \approx 0.097$ mm $= 97$ µm, in the far infrared.

**8.** (expert) For an X-ray mirror with $\delta = 2\times10^{-5}$, what is the critical grazing angle in degrees?

??? note "Answer"
    $\theta_c \approx \sqrt{2 \times 2\times10^{-5}} = \sqrt{4\times10^{-5}} = 0.0063$ radians $\approx 0.36°$.

## Summary

- Only radio and visible light pass freely through the air; other light needs mountains, aircraft, balloons, rockets or
  satellites.
- Infrared telescopes and detectors must be cooled; they have worked on Mauna Kea, in the KAO and SOFIA aircraft, and in
  space with IRAS and Spitzer.
- Ultraviolet telescopes such as IUE, GALEX and AstroSat's UVIT work above the ozone.
- X-rays are focused by nested mirrors at glancing angles, as Wolter showed in 1952: Uhuru, ROSAT, Chandra, XMM-Newton
  and the Einstein Probe.
- Gamma rays cannot be focused; Fermi tracks the particles they make.
- The most energetic gamma rays are caught from the ground by their Cherenkov flashes or air showers: H.E.S.S., MAGIC,
  VERITAS and LHAASO.
- Odyssey's gamma rays found ice under Mars; Hope's EMUS watches Mars's air escaping in ultraviolet.
- On Mars, with so little air above, a third of 1 MeV gamma rays would reach the ground.
