# 16. Giant telescopes and the observatories of the world

*Chapters 11 to 15 took a telescope apart: lenses and mirrors, the limits set by waves and air, the designs, the
cameras and the spectrographs. This chapter puts them together at the largest size people have built. It explains why
bigger is better, how a mirror 8 or 39 metres across can be made at all, and why the great telescopes stand on a few
high, dry mountains in Chile, Hawaiʻi, the Canary Islands, the Karoo, the Himalayas, the Caucasus and Iran. It also
looks at the arguments over who the mountains belong to, at the fight to keep the sky dark, and at how three
telescopes on Earth worked out how much water Mars has lost.*

## 16.1 Why bigger is better { #bigger }

A telescope's main mirror is a bucket for light (chapter 11). Two things grow with its width $D$:

- **The light it collects** grows with its area, so with $D^2$. Double the width and you collect four times the light,
  and see fainter things.
- **The detail it can show** grows with $D$ (chapter 12). Double the width and the finest detail is half the size, as
  long as the blurring of the air can be beaten.

## 16.2 Observatories before telescopes { #before }

Long before telescopes, astronomers knew that a bigger instrument measures more accurately, and rulers paid for
observatories built on a grand scale.

- **Maragha, Iran, 1259.** Building began on an observatory for the Mongol ruler Hulagu, with Nasir al-Din al-Tusi as
  its director (history book, [chapter 8, section 8.8](../history/08-The-Islamic-World-and-Persia.md#maragha)).
- **Samarkand, 1420s.** The prince **Ulugh Beg** built an observatory whose great sextant, a curved track set into a
  trench, had a radius of 40.04 m (history book,
  [section 8.10](../history/08-The-Islamic-World-and-Persia.md#samarkand)).
- **Beijing, 1442.** An observatory was finished on a platform on the city wall, and rebuilt in 1673 with new bronze
  instruments (history book, [chapter 4, section 4.11](../history/04-China.md#jesuits)).
- **Hven, between Denmark and Sweden, 1576.** Tycho Brahe built Uraniborg, and then Stjerneborg, partly underground so
  that his instruments stayed firm and out of the wind (history book,
  [chapter 14, section 14.3](../history/14-Tycho-and-Kepler.md#hven)).
- **Jaipur, India, 1734.** Jai Singh's observatory was finished, with a sundial 27 m high (history book,
  [chapter 5, section 5.8](../history/05-India.md#jantar)).

These were built to measure positions. Today's giants are built to collect light, but the idea is the same: the
biggest questions need the biggest instruments, and the money of whole nations.

??? note "Sources"
    - Maragha, Samarkand, Beijing, Hven and Jaipur: this site's history book, sections 8.8, 8.10, 4.11, 14.3 and 5.8.

## 16.3 Up the mountains { #mountains }

In 1917 the 2.5 m Hooker Telescope began work on **Mount Wilson**, above Los Angeles. As the city grew, its lights
limited the observatory's study of faint, distant objects, though it is still busy with work on stars. Big telescopes
have been moving to higher, darker and drier places ever since.

**Mauna Kea.** In the 1960s Gerard Kuiper looked for a dry site for infrared astronomy. Haleakalā, on Maui, looked
promising, but it was too low, often under cloud. Mauna Kea, on the island of Hawaiʻi, 4,207 m high in the middle of
the Pacific, was nearly ideal. Its Astronomy Precinct was set up in 1967, and by 2012 it held 13 telescopes, some
funded by as many as 11 countries.

A good site has:

- **height**, to be above much of the air and most of its water vapour
- **dry, clear air**, with little cloud
- **steady air**, for good seeing (chapter 12, [section 12.5](12-The-Limits-Diffraction-Resolution-and-Seeing.md#sites))
- **darkness**, far from city lights, and quiet, far from radio signals

**The Atacama.** In northern **Chile** the Atacama is the driest desert in the world outside the polar regions. Two
mountain ranges, the Andes and the coastal range, block moist air from both oceans. With its height, nearly cloudless
skies, dry air and few towns, it is one of the best places on Earth for astronomy, and Europe's ESO runs three
observatories there and is building a fourth.

## 16.4 Three ways to make a giant mirror { #mirrors }

A big mirror must hold its shape to a small fraction of a wavelength of light, while the telescope tips and turns and
the night cools. There are three answers, and an unusual fourth.

**1. One thick disc, made lighter.** The 5.1 m mirror of the **Hale Telescope** was cast in 1934 at the Corning Glass
Works in New York State, in Pyrex, a glass that changes size little with temperature, with a "honeycomb" back. It
cooled for several months before it went by rail to California. The telescope was dedicated in 1948 and saw first
light on 26 January 1949, under the direction of Edwin Hubble. A huge mass of glass has a cost, though. The Russian
**BTA-6**, 6 m, the largest telescope in the world from 1975 until 1990, has had serious problems with heat because of
the great mass of its mirror. But it pioneered what is now standard for large telescopes: the simple up-down and round
mount of chapter 13 ([section 13.8](13-Telescope-Designs.md#mounts)), with a computer-controlled turntable that keeps
the picture from spinning.

**2. One piece, pushed into shape.** Japan's **Subaru Telescope** has a single mirror 8.2 m across. Underneath it,
261 computer-controlled pushers correct its shape as the telescope tilts. Pushing on the back of a mirror to undo the
effects of gravity and temperature is called **active optics**.

**3. Many pieces.** Make the mirror from smaller pieces, each held and moved by computer so that together they act as
one surface. The **Keck** telescopes each have 36 pieces making 10 m. The **Gran Telescopio Canarias** has 36
six-sided pieces, the **Southern African Large Telescope** 91, each 1 m across, and the **ELT** will have 798.

**4. Liquid.** Spin a pool of mercury and its surface curves into the shape a telescope mirror needs. The
**International Liquid Mirror Telescope** at Devasthal in India, built with Belgium and Canada, has a 4 m spinning
mercury mirror. It can only look straight up, and it saw first light on 29 April 2022.

The **Giant Magellan Telescope** goes back to big single discs, seven of them. Each of its mirrors, 8.4 m across, is
cast from 20 tons of glass from Japan at the University of Arizona's mirror laboratory, and the casting
alone takes about 12 to 13 weeks.

## 16.6 The next giants { #next }

**The Extremely Large Telescope (ELT).** Europe's ESO chose Cerro Armazones in the Atacama, 23 km from Paranal, in
2010, and the first stone was laid on 26 May 2017.

- Its main mirror, 39.3 m across, is made of 798 six-sided pieces. It will collect about 250 times as much light as
  Hubble, and is meant to give pictures 15 times sharper.
- Light bounces off **five mirrors** in turn. The fourth bends its shape to undo the blurring of the air, guided by
  six laser beams that make artificial stars (chapter 12,
  [section 12.6](12-The-Limits-Diffraction-Resolution-and-Seeing.md#tricks)).
- The moving parts weigh about 3,700 tonnes. The dome, 80 m high and 93 m wide, is the largest ever built for a
  telescope.
- First light is planned for March 2029, and the first science for December 2030. It will look for planets as light as
  the Earth by the wobble of their stars (chapter 8), and try to take pictures of larger planets directly.

**The Giant Magellan Telescope (GMT)**, at Las Campanas in Chile, 2,516 m up, joins seven 8.4 m mirrors, collecting
light over 368 square metres. It should start work in the early 2030s.

**The Thirty Meter Telescope (TMT)**, with a mirror of 492 pieces, is backed by Canada, Japan and India. It was planned
for Mauna Kea but has been held up for years (section 16.7). A site on La Palma has been put forward, though
astronomers think it less good. In June 2025 the United States' National Science Foundation chose to support the GMT
instead.

## 16.7 Whose mountain? { #whose }

The best mountains for telescopes are often sacred to the people who lived there first.

- **Mauna Kea.** For many Native Hawaiians the whole mountain is sacred, and building on it, even for science, spoils
  it. The Astronomy Precinct lies on land protected for its importance to Hawaiian culture. Protests against the TMT
  began in 2014 and grew in 2019, with thousands camped on the road, while others rallied for the telescope (history
  book, [chapter 12, section 12.8](../history/12-Oceania-and-Australia.md#telescopes)). In April 2024 the TMT's project
  manager apologised for adding to the division in the community.
- **Mount Graham, Arizona.** The observatory stands on land sacred to the Western Apache, and the San Carlos Apache
  Tribal Council formally opposed the telescopes. Environmentalists also worried about the Mount Graham red squirrel,
  which lives nowhere else.

Astronomers answer that sites this good are rare; La Palma, offered for the TMT instead, is thought less good for
science. How to
weigh these things is not a question telescopes can answer.

## 16.8 Keeping the sky dark { #dark }

A telescope cannot see a faint galaxy through the glow of a city.

- **Flagstaff, Arizona**, made one of the world's first organised efforts, by law, to
  reduce light pollution.
- In **2001** a programme began to name International Dark Sky Places, rewarding communities and parks that light
  responsibly.
- In **2007** the area round the **Mont Mégantic** Observatory in Quebec became the first International Dark Sky
  Reserve: a dark core, with towns around it that control their lighting.

The giant telescopes are mostly far from cities, but satellites cross every sky (history book,
[chapter 22, section 22.9](../history/22-Astronomy-Today.md#dark)). Book 1,
[chapter 20, section 20.7](../night-sky/20-Light-Pollution-and-Dark-Sky-Places.md#places), lists dark-sky places
around the world.

## 16.9 How much water has Mars lost? { #mars }

The giants on Earth still study Mars. In March 2015 a team published maps of the water in Mars's air made over six
years with ESO's **VLT**, the **Keck** Observatory and NASA's Infrared Telescope Facility, all on mountaintops on Earth.

They measured two kinds of water. Ordinary water is H₂O. In "heavy" water, HDO, one hydrogen atom is **deuterium**,
which weighs twice as much. Light hydrogen escapes into space more easily, so as a planet loses water, the heavy kind
is left behind. Each kind absorbs its own wavelengths, so a spectrograph (chapter 15) can tell them apart.

- The polar cap ice turned out to be about **eight times** as rich in deuterium as the water in Earth's oceans.
- So Mars has lost a volume of water about **6.5 times** that in its polar caps today.
- That is enough for an ocean at least **137 m** deep over the whole planet. Gathered in the low northern plains, it
  would have covered a fifth of Mars, in places almost 1.6 km deep.

Mars has helped the telescopes too. The dry Atacama, home of so many observatories, is so like Mars that it is used
for practice runs of Mars expeditions.

??? note "Sources"
    - Deuterium less easily lost to space than ordinary hydrogen, the HDO to H₂O ratio, the results of March 2015, the
      eightfold enrichment of the polar cap ice, the loss of 6.5 times today's polar cap water, the ocean at least 137 m
      deep, 20% of the planet and almost a mile deep in places, and the VLT, Keck and IRTF over six years: Wikipedia,
      [Martian polar ice caps](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Martian_polar_ice_caps) (weaker).
    - The Atacama used for Mars simulations: Wikipedia, Atacama Desert (above).

## 16.10 Working it out { #working }

**Light.** The light collected goes as $D^2$.

| Compared with the ELT, 39.3 m | Width | ELT collects |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Your eye, dark-adapted | 7 mm | about 31 million times more |
| Hale | 5.1 m | 59 times more |
| One VLT telescope | 8.2 m | 23 times more |
| Gran Telescopio Canarias | 10.4 m | 14 times more |
| GMT | 25.4 m | 2.4 times more |

31 million times the light is 18.7 magnitudes fainter ($2.5\log_{10}$ of 31 million; book 1,
[chapter 4, section 4.5](../night-sky/04-How-Bright-Is-a-Star.md#pogson)).

**Detail.** The smallest detail, from chapter 12, is $1.22\lambda/D$. For the ELT in the near infrared, at
$\lambda = 1{,}600$ nm, that is $1.22 \times 1.6\times10^{-6} / 39.3 = 5.0\times10^{-8}$ radians, about 0.01 seconds of
arc. With Mars at its closest, 54.6 million km away, that is a detail of about 2.7 km on the planet.

**A giant telescope on Mars?** The thin air and lower gravity would make some things easier.

| | Mars | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Surface gravity | 3.73 m/s² | 9.82 m/s² |
| Weight of the ELT's 3,700 tonnes of moving parts | 13.8 MN | 36.3 MN |
| Air pressure at the surface | 6.36 mb | 1,014 mb |
| Mass of air above each square metre ($p/g$) | about 170 kg | about 10,300 kg |

There is about 60 times less air above each square metre on Mars. But every tonne would have to be flown there. And
Mars's fine reddish dust hangs in the air and colours the sky (chapter 3,
[section 3.9](03-Colours-Prisms-and-Rainbows.md#mars)).

## 16.11 Why big telescopes win twice { #speed }

How long must a telescope look at a faint star to see it clearly? Suppose the star is so faint that the main noise is
the glow of the night sky itself, as it usually is.

**Without adaptive optics.** The star's light collected in time $t$ is $S \propto D^2 t$. Its image is blurred by the
air to a fixed size $\theta_s$ (chapter 12), so the sky glow inside it is $B \propto D^2\theta_s^2 t$. The noise is
$\sqrt{B}$ (chapter 14, section 14.10), so the signal-to-noise ratio is

$$\frac{S}{\sqrt{B}} \propto \frac{D^2 t}{D\theta_s\sqrt{t}} = \frac{D\sqrt{t}}{\theta_s}.$$

To reach a given ratio needs $t \propto \theta_s^2/D^2$: twice the width, a quarter of the time.

**With adaptive optics.** Now the image shrinks to the diffraction size $\theta \approx \lambda/D$. The sky glow inside
it is $B \propto D^2(\lambda/D)^2 t = \lambda^2 t$, which no longer depends on $D$ at all. Then

$$\frac{S}{\sqrt{B}} \propto \frac{D^2\sqrt{t}}{\lambda}, \qquad t \propto \frac{1}{D^4}.$$

A bigger mirror wins twice: it collects more starlight and lets in less sky with it. Compared with one 8.2 m VLT
telescope, the ELT would need $(39.3/8.2)^2 = 23$ times less time without adaptive optics, but $(39.3/8.2)^4 = 528$
times less with perfect adaptive optics. Real adaptive optics are never perfect, so the real gain lies in between. This
is the main reason the ELT, GMT and TMT are all built around adaptive optics.

??? note "Sources"
    - The derivation is ours, using counting statistics (chapter 14) and the diffraction limit (chapter 12). Adaptive
      optics as a central part of the ELT: Wikipedia, Extremely Large Telescope (above).

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) Give two reasons why astronomers build bigger telescopes.

??? note "Answer"
    A bigger mirror collects more light, in proportion to its area, so it sees fainter things; and it can show finer
    detail, in proportion to its width, if the blurring of the air is overcome.

**2.** (beginner) Name three things that make a good site for a telescope.

??? note "Answer"
    Any three of: height, above most of the water vapour; dry, clear air with little cloud; steady air for good
    seeing; darkness far from city lights; quiet, far from radio signals.

**3.** (beginner) What was the great instrument of Ulugh Beg's observatory at Samarkand?

??? note "Answer"
    A sextant, a curved track set into a trench, with a radius of 40.04 m, built in the 1420s.

**4.** (intermediate) Name three ways of making a mirror 8 m or more across.

??? note "Answer"
    A single big disc (Hale, BTA-6, GMT); one disc pushed into shape by computer-controlled supports (Subaru); or many
    smaller pieces moved together (Keck, GTC, SALT, ELT). A spinning liquid mirror is a fourth, for a telescope that
    only looks straight up.

**5.** (intermediate) Why can SALT reach only 70% of the sky?

??? note "Answer"
    Its mirror stays at a fixed tilt; the telescope turns only round, not up and down.

**6.** (intermediate) How did telescopes on Earth show that Mars has lost a lot of water?

??? note "Answer"
    They measured heavy water (HDO) against ordinary water (H₂O). Light hydrogen escapes to space more easily, so lost
    water leaves deuterium behind. The polar ice is about eight times as rich in deuterium as Earth's oceans, which
    means Mars has lost about 6.5 times the water now in its polar caps.

**7.** (advanced) How many times more light does the ELT, 39.3 m, collect than the GTC, 10.4 m?

??? note "Answer"
    $(39.3/10.4)^2 = 3.78^2 \approx 14$ times.

**8.** (expert) With perfect adaptive optics, and the sky glow as the main noise, how many times faster would the ELT
reach the same signal-to-noise ratio on a faint star than the GTC?

??? note "Answer"
    The time goes as $1/D^4$, so $(39.3/10.4)^4 = 3.78^4 \approx 204$ times faster.

## Summary

- A bigger mirror collects light in proportion to $D^2$ and shows detail in proportion to $D$.
- Observatories have been built big for centuries: Maragha, Samarkand, Beijing, Hven and Jaipur.
- The best sites are high, dry, clear, steady and dark: Mauna Kea, the Atacama, La Palma, the Karoo, the Himalayas.
- Giant mirrors are made as honeycomb discs, thin discs pushed into shape, many pieces, or spinning liquid.
- The VLT, Keck, Subaru, GTC, SALT, BTA-6, India's DOT, Iran's INO340 and others are spread around the world.
- The 39.3 m ELT and the GMT are being built in Chile; the TMT is held up.
- Some sites, such as Mauna Kea and Mount Graham, are sacred to Indigenous peoples, and building there is disputed.
- Dark-sky laws and reserves protect what remains of the dark.
- The VLT, Keck and IRTF showed from Earth that Mars has lost about 6.5 times the water in its polar caps.
- With adaptive optics, the time to see a faint star falls as $1/D^4$.
