# 1. Waves and particles: what light is

*Everything we know about the stars has reached us as light. This chapter asks what that light is. It starts with how
we see, follows the old ideas from China, Greece, India and the Islamic world, then the long argument between waves
and particles: Newton against Huygens, Young's two slits, Maxwell's electric waves and Einstein's packets of light. It
ends with the strange answer that light is both, with the sunlight that powered rovers on Mars, and with the
experiment that won Einstein his Nobel Prize.*

## 1.1 How we see { #see }

Some things make their own light: the Sun, a candle, a lamp, the stars. Most things do not. We see a tree, a book or
the Moon because light from a source bounces off them and comes into our eyes. The Moon and the planets shine only
by reflected sunlight.

Light goes in straight lines. That is why shadows have sharp edges, and why you cannot see round a corner.

It seems obvious that light goes into the eye. But for a long time many thinkers believed the opposite: that the eye
sends out rays that touch what it sees. The question was settled in the 11th century by **Ibn al-Haytham** (section
1.2), who argued from experiments that we see because light comes into the eye.

## 1.2 Old ideas from around the world { #ideas }

- **China.** The book called the *Mozi*, from the 4th century BC, named after the thinker Mozi, has one of the
  earliest written accounts of a **pinhole image**: light through a small hole into a dark room makes an upside-down
  picture of the scene outside. It explains that the picture is turned over because the rays cross at the hole.
- **Greece.** Around 300 BC **Euclid** wrote that light travels in straight lines, and worked out the rules of
  reflection. He and later **Ptolemy** held that sight comes from rays sent out by the eye. In 55 BC the Roman poet
  **Lucretius** wrote that sunlight is made of tiny atoms shot across the air.
- **India.** From around the early centuries AD, the **Samkhya** school counted light among five basic "subtle"
  elements. Later the Buddhist thinkers **Dignaga**, in the 5th century, and **Dharmakirti**, in the 7th, described
  the world as made of momentary flashes of light or energy.
- **The Islamic world.** **Ibn al-Haytham** (about 965 to about 1040), born in Basra and working in Cairo, wrote a
  seven-volume **Book of Optics**. With careful experiments he argued against rays from the eye, and for light coming
  into it. He studied pinhole images too. His book was put into Latin around 1200. He is sometimes called the father
  of modern optics.
- **China again.** In 1088 the scholar **Shen Kuo** compared the crossing of rays at a pinhole to an oar turning on
  its rowlock, to explain why the image is upside down.

## 1.3 Waves { #waves }

Drop a stone in a pond and ripples spread out. Each ripple is a **wave**: a pattern that travels, while the water
itself only bobs up and down. A wave has:

- a **wavelength**, the distance from one crest to the next;
- a **frequency**, the number of crests that pass a point each second, measured in hertz (Hz);
- a **speed**, how fast the crests move.

The three are tied together: speed = frequency × wavelength.

## 1.4 Particles or waves? { #debate }

In the 17th century two great scientists disagreed about what light is.

- **Isaac Newton** said light is a stream of tiny particles, which he called **corpuscles**, shot out in all
  directions from a source. His strongest argument was that waves bend round obstacles, as sound comes round a
  corner, while light goes in straight lines and casts sharp shadows. He set out his ideas in 1675 and in his book
  *Opticks* of 1704.
- **Christiaan Huygens**, in the Netherlands, worked out a mathematical theory of light as **waves** in 1678 and
  published it in his *Treatise on Light* in 1690. Robert Hooke had already compared light to waves on water.

Both theories explained reflection. Newton's fame kept the particle theory on top for most of the 18th century.

as the wave theory said and the particle theory did not. Light, it seemed, was a wave.

## 1.6 Electric and magnetic waves { #maxwell }

But waves of what? In 1845 **Michael Faraday** found that a magnet can change light passing through glass, turning the
direction in which it vibrates: the first sign that light has something to do with electricity and magnetism. In 1847
he suggested light might be a fast electric and magnetic vibration that needs nothing to travel through.

The Scottish physicist **James Clerk Maxwell** worked out the mathematics. His equations showed that electric and
magnetic fields could make waves that keep themselves going through empty space, and he calculated their speed. It
came out equal to the measured speed of light. In 1862 he concluded that light is an **electromagnetic wave**.

Maxwell's equations also allowed waves much longer than light's. In the late 1880s **Heinrich Hertz** in Germany made
and detected such waves in his laboratory: **radio waves**. They reflected, bent and interfered just like light.
Chapter 4 follows the whole family, from radio waves to gamma rays.

    - Faraday's discovery of 1845 and suggestion of 1847, Maxwell's speed matching the speed of light, his
      conclusion of 1862 in *On Physical Lines of Force*, his *Treatise* of 1873, and Hertz's waves behaving like
      light: Wikipedia, [Light](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Light) (weaker).
    - Hertz's experiments of 1886 to 1888 producing and receiving what are now called radio waves: Wikipedia,
      [Heinrich Hertz](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Heinrich_Hertz) (weaker).

## 1.7 Packets of light { #photons }

In 1887 Hertz noticed something odd: a charged object loses its charge more easily when ultraviolet light shines
on it.
Light knocks electrons out of the metal. This is the **photoelectric effect**, and the wave theory could not
explain it. A brighter wave should knock out
electrons with more energy. Instead, experiments showed that electrons came out only if the light's frequency was
high enough, however bright or dim the light, and however long it shone.

- In **1900** the German physicist **Max Planck**, working on the glow of hot things (chapter 6), suggested that
  light can only give or take energy in fixed lumps, which he called **quanta**. The energy of one lump is
  $E = hf$, where $f$ is the frequency and $h$ is a tiny number now called **Planck's constant**.
- In **1905** **Albert Einstein** took the idea further: light really is made of such packets. Each packet gives all
  its energy to one electron, so only light of high enough frequency can free it (section 1.11). He won the Nobel
  Prize in Physics for this in 1921.
- In **1926** the American chemist Gilbert Lewis gave the packets their name: **photons**.

A photon has no mass, and always travels at the speed of light.

## 1.8 Both at once { #both }

So is light a wave or a particle? The answer from the 20th century is: it behaves as both.

Send light through two slits so dimly that only one photon is on its way at a time, as G. I. Taylor first did in
1909. Each photon lands on the screen as a single dot, like a particle. But as the dots build up, one by one, they
form the bright and dark bands of Figure B, like a wave. In 1961 Claus Jönsson did the same with electrons, and they
made bands too.

Physicists describe light with wave mathematics in some experiments and particle mathematics in others. Long-wave
radio usually acts more like a wave; short-wave X-rays and gamma rays act more like particles; visible light is in
the middle and easily shows both. Neither picture is the whole truth.

??? note "Sources"
    - Taylor in 1909 with light so dim that photon events were mostly separate, single particles detected as dots,
      the interference pattern building up one by one, and Jönsson's electron experiment of 1961: Wikipedia,
      [Double-slit experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Double-slit_experiment) (weaker).

## 1.9 Sunlight at Mars { #mars }

Mars is about 1.52 times as far from the Sun as the Earth, so the same sunlight is spread over a larger area. Above
the air, each square metre facing the Sun gets about **586 watts** at Mars, against **1,361 watts** at the Earth:
about 43 per cent as much.

A **solar panel** turns photons into electricity, by a cousin of the photoelectric effect called the photovoltaic
effect. Edmond Becquerel discovered it in 1839, and the first practical solar cell was shown at Bell Laboratories in
1954. On Mars it has done remarkable work:

- The rovers **Spirit** and **Opportunity**, which landed in 2004, ran on solar power. They were built to last 90
  sols. Opportunity kept going for **5,111 sols**, 14 years and 138 days, recharging its batteries from sunlight, and
  drove 45.16 km. In June 2018 a dust storm spread over the whole planet, and within a few days its panels could not
  make enough power to keep in touch. The last contact was on 10 June 2018; it never woke up again.
- The helicopter **Ingenuity** charged its batteries from sunlight too. Planned for five flights, it made
  **72** in nearly three years. In the Martian winter of 2022 there was so little sunlight that it shut down
  completely each night.

## 1.10 Working it out { #working }

**Frequency.** Speed = frequency × wavelength, so $f = c / \lambda$, with $c = 3.00 \times 10^8$ m/s. For green light
of 550 nm $= 5.50 \times 10^{-7}$ m:

$$f = \frac{3.00 \times 10^8}{5.50 \times 10^{-7}} = 5.45 \times 10^{14} \text{ Hz},$$

545 million million crests a second. Red light of 700 nm gives $4.3 \times 10^{14}$ Hz, violet of 400 nm
$7.5 \times 10^{14}$ Hz.

**Energy of a photon.** $E = hf$, with $h = 6.626 \times 10^{-34}$ J s:

$$E = 6.626 \times 10^{-34} \times 5.45 \times 10^{14} = 3.61 \times 10^{-19} \text{ J}.$$

**Counting photons.** A green laser pointer giving out 1 milliwatt, $10^{-3}$ joules a second, sends out
$10^{-3} / 3.61 \times 10^{-19} = 2.8 \times 10^{15}$ photons every second.

**Sunlight in photons.** Sunlight is a mix of colours, but as a rough guide, suppose every photon were green, with
$3.61 \times 10^{-19}$ J:

| | Mars | Earth |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Sunlight above the air, per square metre | 586 W | 1,361 W |
| Compared with the Earth | 43 per cent | 100 per cent |
| Photons per square metre each second, if all green | $586 / 3.61 \times 10^{-19} = 1.6 \times 10^{21}$ | $1361 / 3.61 \times 10^{-19} = 3.8 \times 10^{21}$ |

Even at Mars, every square metre catches over a thousand million million million photons each second.

      [Planck constant](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Planck_constant) (weaker).
    - Solar irradiance: NASA, [Mars Fact Sheet](https://nssdc.gsfc.nasa.gov/planetary/factsheet/marsfact.html).
    - The speed of light, about $3.00 \times 10^8$ m/s: chapter 2. The arithmetic is ours.

## 1.11 Why red light cannot free an electron { #photoelectric }

Einstein's explanation of the photoelectric effect fits in one line. An electron needs a certain least energy to
escape from a metal, called the **work function**, $\phi$. A photon gives all its energy $hf$ to one electron, so the
electron comes out with at most

$$K_{\max} = hf - \phi.$$

If $hf$ is less than $\phi$, no electron escapes at all, however many photons arrive. Brighter light means more
photons, so more electrons, but not faster ones.

**Electronvolts.** These energies are tiny in joules, so physicists use the **electronvolt**, $1 \text{ eV} = 1.602
\times 10^{-19}$ J. Since $E = hf = hc / \lambda$, a handy form is

$$E \text{ (in eV)} = \frac{1240}{\lambda \text{ (in nm)}}.$$

So a red photon of 700 nm carries 1.77 eV, a green one of 550 nm 2.25 eV and a violet one of 400 nm 3.10 eV.

**Two metals.** Caesium has a work function of about 1.95 eV, sodium about 2.75 eV.

| Light | Photon energy | Caesium, $\phi = 1.95$ eV | Sodium, $\phi = 2.75$ eV |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| red, 700 nm | 1.77 eV | none | none |
| green, 550 nm | 2.25 eV | up to 0.30 eV | none |
| violet, 400 nm | 3.10 eV | up to 1.15 eV | up to 0.35 eV |

The longest wavelength that works is $1240 / \phi$: $1240 / 1.95 = 636$ nm for caesium, orange-red light, and
$1240 / 2.75 = 451$ nm for sodium, blue light. A floodlight of red light will never free an electron from sodium; a
faint violet lamp will. That is the experiment the wave theory could not explain, and the reason metals with low work
functions, such as caesium, are used in detectors that must respond to very faint light.

## Check yourself { #check }

**1.** (beginner) How do we see the Moon, which makes no light of its own?

??? note "Answer"
    Sunlight bounces off the Moon and comes into our eyes.

**2.** (beginner) What did Ibn al-Haytham argue about how we see?

??? note "Answer"
    That light comes into the eye from what we see, not that the eye sends out rays. He backed it with experiments.

**3.** (beginner) Which has the longer wavelength, red light or blue light?

??? note "Answer"
    Red light: about 700 nm against about 400 to 450 nm for blue.

**4.** (intermediate) Why did Young's two slits support the wave theory?

??? note "Answer"
    The light made a row of bright and dark bands. Waves from the two slits add up in some places and cancel in
    others; particles going straight through would make only two bright lines.

**5.** (intermediate) What did Maxwell find when he worked out the speed of electric and magnetic waves?

??? note "Answer"
    It came out equal to the measured speed of light, so he concluded that light is an electromagnetic wave.

**6.** (intermediate) In the photoelectric effect, what happens when you make the light brighter but keep its colour
the same?

??? note "Answer"
    More electrons come out, but each has the same greatest energy. Only a higher frequency gives them more energy.

**7.** (advanced) Find the frequency and photon energy of red light of 650 nm.

??? note "Answer"
    $f = 3.00 \times 10^8 / 6.50 \times 10^{-7} = 4.62 \times 10^{14}$ Hz. $E = 6.626 \times 10^{-34} \times 4.62
    \times 10^{14} = 3.06 \times 10^{-19}$ J, or $1240 / 650 = 1.91$ eV.

**8.** (expert) Ultraviolet light of 250 nm falls on zinc. Take its work function as 3.6 eV. Do electrons come
out, and if so with what greatest energy? What is the longest wavelength that frees electrons from zinc?

??? note "Answer"
    $E = 1240 / 250 = 4.96$ eV, more than 3.6 eV, so yes: $K_{\max} = 4.96 - 3.6 = 1.4$ eV. The longest wavelength is
    $1240 / 3.6 = 340$ nm, in the ultraviolet: no visible light frees electrons from zinc.

## Summary

- We see because light comes into our eyes, as Ibn al-Haytham argued in the 11th century.
- Thinkers in China, Greece, India and the Islamic world studied pinhole images, straight rays and reflection.
- Light is a wave: we see wavelengths from about 400 nm (violet) to 700 nm (red), and speed = frequency × wavelength.
- Newton thought light was particles; Huygens thought it was waves. Young's two slits, Fresnel and Foucault seemed to
  settle it for waves.
- Maxwell showed light is an electromagnetic wave; Hertz made radio waves, its longer cousins.
- Planck and Einstein showed light comes in packets, photons, each with energy $E = hf$.
- Light behaves as both a wave and a particle.
- Mars gets 43 per cent of the Earth's sunlight; Opportunity ran on it for over 14 years.
- A photon frees an electron only if $hf$ beats the metal's work function.
